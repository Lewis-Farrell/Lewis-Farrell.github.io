## 1. The Idea You Haven't Said Yet

It is a weeknight, seven years in. The plates are stacked by the sink and the table has been wiped down, and the next two hours are already arranged without anyone having arranged them: the show you are most of the way through, the same end of the sofa each, the phone checked once around ten, the hall light left on for whoever goes up second. None of it is bad. Most of it you chose, and some of it you would defend to a stranger. But since about four o'clock you have had a different idea for tonight, and you have not said it. You have been saying it in your head instead, trying out openings, listening to each one the way your partner would hear it.

You know how it goes, which is the odd part. You can see it already: the small pause, the "sure, if you want", the question that follows, *where's this coming from?*, asked lightly enough that you will both agree to treat it as light. You know what your partner likes on a weeknight, what tires them, what they would say yes to. You have known it for years. It has been right for so long that you no longer feel yourself checking it; you notice it now only because you are using it to talk yourself out of something. Across the table your partner is a few chapters into a book you did not see them start, and it occurs to you, briefly and then not again, that you could not say what it is about.

Under the rehearsal sit two doubts, and they are not the same doubt. The first is whether the evening is even the problem. Perhaps the sofa and the show are fine, and what has gone flat is something an idea for tonight will not reach; perhaps it is the other way round. The second is harder to put down. *Let's do something different* is four words, and you can think of at least two things they might be taken to mean, only one of them says you are happy with things. Your partner looks up. "What?" You have a second, maybe less. Whatever you say next, the real question has already arrived, and it is not whether the idea is good. It is what you would have to believe, about them and about the two of you, to say it out loud.

## 2. Four Questions Before The Model

This essay treats that half-second as an economic problem, which means asking what each of you is choosing, what each is trying to work out about the other, and what it costs to be wrong. It answers four questions. First, why the choice between more of what you know and something new is not the same choice for a couple as in the textbook version of the problem, where the thing you know stays put; here the thing you know is a person, and people change. Second, why "we're bored" can mean two different things, with two different cures, only one of which involves doing anything new. Third, when a new thing really does compete with closeness and when it is one of the ways closeness is kept, and why the answer turns less on the idea than on something you cannot see from your chair. Fourth, what the one who asks and the one who is asked each have to decide, and when none of this is about novelty at all, because the trouble is the size of the evening rather than the appetite for it.

Four things belong up front. Every claim here is a result in a simple model, an assumption stated as one, or a gap stated as a gap. There is no reliable evidence on how fast anyone changes, so nothing below hangs on a number. And the essay does not say that couples decline over time, or that they do not. One more: the stale-estimate framing used here—intimacy as an updating picture rather than an owned stock—may already appear in psychology under other names. That prior-art check has not been run, and the essay does not claim the framing is new.

The place to start is the version of the choice with no person in it.

## 3. A Source You Know And One You Don't

On a given evening there are two places to spend it. One is a source you know: the thing that has worked, whose payoff you can more or less name in advance. The other is a source you do not know. It might be better, it might be worse, and trying it tells you something about whether to try it again. The known source pays you tonight. The unknown one pays you tonight and also pays you in information, which is worth something only if there are more evenings to spend it on.

What would you need to believe to take the unknown one? That its likely payoff, plus what you would learn from it, beats the sure thing. The more evenings ahead, the more the learning is worth. The better the known source, the higher the bar. That is the whole tension, and it has names. Taking the known source is exploiting; taking the unknown one partly to learn is exploring. The textbook form is the multi-armed bandit, after the slot machine with its single arm.[^1] Its best-known result, the Gittins index,[^2] says that in the classical version you can give each source a score of its own and take the highest score each time.

This essay borrows nothing from that result except its condition. The classical version assumes that a source you are not using stays as it was: the machine you walked away from is the same machine when you come back. That is an assumption, not a mistake. The technical literature has a whole line of work on sources whose payoffs drift, and in it you have to keep sampling, because what you learned goes out of date.[^3] But a common informal version of the idea—the one that turns into advice about exploring when young and settling when old—borrows the frozen form. No rule about when in a relationship to explore is derived here, and none is claimed.

One boundary. An earlier essay in this series dealt with how long to keep looking before committing,[^4] and the bandit models of dating that exist, in economics and in psychology, are about that same stage: learning about candidates before choosing one.[^5] This essay starts after the choice. The known source is a particular person, and you are not planning to walk away from the machine. That matters less than the fact that the machine keeps changing while you are not looking at it.

## 4. The Person Who Keeps Changing

Put the person back. You carry a picture of your partner: what they like on a weeknight, what is worrying them at work, what they would say yes to, what they have stopped finding funny. They carry one of you. Each of you uses that picture every time you choose something together, mostly without noticing, the way you use your map of a city you live in. The picture was drawn by looking: from conversations, from watching them handle something unfamiliar, from being surprised by them. It is only as good as the last look.

Meanwhile the person keeps changing, whether or not you look. Not constantly and not dramatically. Personality shifts across adulthood, more at some ages and around major life events than at others,[^7] and the smaller things move faster: a job changes shape, a friendship lapses, a parent gets ill, an opinion softens. None of it waits to be noticed. So every evening chosen on the picture carries a small cost when the picture is off: a plan pitched at someone your partner was two years ago. You can look again, which takes time and some exposure, or carry on with what you have.

That is the game. One chooser decides how often to look, and there is only one thing to infer: how far the picture has probably drifted since the last time. What you are "exploiting", on this view, is not a stock of knowing someone, laid down early and drawn on since. It is an old picture of a moving target, and the target moves at the same rate whether you lean on the picture or not. The technical literature has a name for this structure: a restless arm, from Peter Whittle's 1988 paper on bandits whose unused arms keep evolving.[^6] The essay takes that structure and nothing more. Whittle proposed an index for such problems that works only under conditions, and it is not used here; the restless problem has no general solution of the clean classical kind, and nothing below pretends to one.

What the structure gives is modest. In a simple model of it, the sensible gap between looks grows with what a look costs and shrinks with how fast the person changes. Two consequences matter later. A person who does not change is never worth looking at again: every look costs something and tells you nothing the old picture did not. And a person who changes but keeps returning to a settled type is cheaper to know with an old picture than one who wanders; whether looking still pays then depends on the cost of a look as well as on the drift. The functional form of how costs and drift interact sets these results up; Section 11 says what that means for the edge cases.

What counts as a look? Not newness. What repairs the picture is information about the person: what the evening draws out of them that you did not already know. A vivid new activity done side by side, in which neither of you says much, may teach you almost nothing. A real question asked at the usual table may teach you a lot. In the model, how much an activity tells you is a property of that activity, not of its novelty, and the next two sections turn on that difference.

This is where "we've been together so long we know each other" meets its cost. Knowing someone, on this view, is not a store you draw down. It is an estimate that ages whether you use it or not, and the length of time since the last real look is the length of time it has had to age.

One limit, stated once. The model values the picture for choosing evenings well. It says nothing about being known as a good in itself—the intrinsic value of a partner who sees you accurately.

## 5. Two Things That Wear Out

Two different things can wear out in a long relationship, and from the sofa they feel the same.

The first is the evening itself. Doing the same thing again may be a little less enjoyable each time, and doing something different resets the count. This has nothing to do with whether you know your partner. It can happen to two people who understand each other perfectly: each knows exactly what the other wants, and what they want is the thing they have done on two hundred Thursdays. Call it a worn groove; the psychology of adaptation calls it habituation.

The second is the picture. You can be doing exactly what each of you once loved, with no wear on the activity at all, and be wrong about each other now. Nothing about the evening has dulled. It is pitched at someone who has moved.

Both come out as "we're bored", and they need different things. The model makes the difference explicit by setting three possible worlds against three remedies. The gains in Table 1 assume the two kinds of wear add up independently; that additivity is a simplifying assumption, not a finding.

**Table 1. Two kinds of wear, three remedies**

| If the trouble is | A change that teaches you nothing about your partner | A real conversation inside the usual routine | A new thing that teaches you something |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Only the evenings have worn | +2.5 | 0 | +2.5 |
| Only the picture has gone stale | 0 | +2.9 | +2.9 |
| Both have worn | +2.0 | +2.9 | +4.8 |

*Gain over twelve evenings, against doing nothing, before counting what the remedy costs. Here and throughout, the numbers are invented to show the structure of the model; they are not estimates of anything about real couples.*

Look along the top row and the conversation does nothing: the two of you already know each other, and what has worn is the evening. Look along the middle row and the change of scene does nothing: the evening is fine, and what has worn is the picture. Only the last column works in every world, and it is the most expensive thing on the table (assuming a real conversation costs less time and nerve than a new outing), because a new thing that genuinely draws your partner out takes more time, more planning and more nerve than either of the others.

So "add more novelty" is the right advice for only one of the two problems. A change that draws out nothing repairs a worn groove and leaves a stale picture where it was. A conversation in the usual routine repairs the picture and leaves the groove alone. And that is the cost behind "we just need something new": if the trouble is the picture, a novelty that teaches you nothing leaves it exactly as stale, and you will have spent the effort only to conclude that new things don't help.

Which problem do real couples have? The data cannot yet say. Couples who do new and exciting things together report better relationships. In three short lab experiments, couples given seven minutes of a novel, arousing task showed bigger gains in rated relationship quality than couples given a mundane one.[^9] In a ten-week study of 53 married couples, those assigned exciting activities ended more satisfied than those assigned merely pleasant ones, though neither group did significantly better than couples assigned nothing.[^10] Diary, longitudinal and experimental work finds that doing self-expanding things with a partner goes with higher relationship satisfaction over time.[^11] Every one of these fits a worn groove, a stale picture or both. None measured what the couples learned about each other, and none separates learning from excitement or from simply spending time together.

Nor is it settled that the first kind of wear applies to people at all. In the model the rate of wear is a dial that can be set to zero, and the evidence does not fix it. The best-known adaptation finding is about life satisfaction after a wedding, which in a large German panel rose a little, briefly, and returned towards baseline, with wide differences between people.[^12] That is about a life event, not about a partner. A stated taste for variety is weak evidence too, since people choose more variety when they plan several occasions at once than when they choose each time.[^13] Nothing establishes that people tire of a partner, as against of what they do with one.

## 6. When Novelty Competes, And When It Doesn't

Now take the last column of Table 1 more seriously, because a new thing does not only teach.

Say the two of you try something neither has done. It can do two things to the ordinary evenings. It can show you something about your partner (how they deal with being bad at something, what they want when the usual options are gone), which sharpens the picture. It can also go badly: an evening that is awkward or embarrassing, a quarrel on the way home, a moment one of you would rather the other had not seen. That costs some of the easy confidence the ordinary evenings rest on. Which effect is larger depends on three things: how out of date the picture was, how big the experiment is, and how fragile the confidence is.

Call the new thing *refreshing* when the first effect wins, so its net effect on what you already had is positive, and *substituting* when the second wins and the net effect is negative. These are this essay's working terms. The field has no standard names for the two cases; the nearest are self-expansion, the psychology of growing through a partner's world,[^9] and relational boredom.[^14]

Fix one activity with a one-in-ten chance of going badly, and vary only how stale the picture is.

**Table 2. One activity, five couples**

| How out of date the picture is (illustrative scale) | Most risk of going badly the activity could carry and still help | The activity, with a 10% risk, is |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Very fresh (0.02) | about 1% | substituting |
| Fresh (0.05) | 5% | substituting |
| Middling (0.1) | about 13% | refreshing |
| Stale (0.2) | about 32% | refreshing |
| Very stale (0.4) | about 71% | refreshing |

*The staleness scale (0 to 1) and the threshold arithmetic are both illustrative; the crossover point moves if the learning and damage parameters change. The scale runs from a picture with little time to age to one with much more time to age; no real-world time units underlie the numbers.*

The activity is the same in every row, and so is the risk. What changes is how much there is to learn. A couple with a very fresh picture have little to find out and the same amount to lose; for them, a risky experiment is a net loss. A couple working from a picture that has had much more time to age have a great deal to find out, and the same risk is worth running. So whether novelty competes with the familiar is not a property of the activity. It is a property of the activity and of how stale your picture is.

> So whether novelty competes with the familiar is not a property of the activity. It is a property of the activity and of how stale your picture is.
{: .pull}

Both slogans pay for that. "Novelty is good for relationships" meets its cost in the top rows: for a couple who are up to date, a risky experiment does harm. "Novelty threatens closeness" meets its cost in the bottom rows: for a stale picture, it is the repair.

Two assumptions sit under the table and should be visible. That a bad experiment can damage the ordinary evenings is not part of the restless-arm structure from Section 4; it is a dependence between the familiar and the new that this essay adds. And the shapes chosen for how much a new thing teaches and how often it goes wrong are there to make the arithmetic tractable. Nothing in the evidence supports them, and nothing shows that novelty draws out more than routine does.

If the new thing is done without your partner, alone or with other people, nothing in your picture of your partner is updated. In this model, novelty taken apart from the partner cannot be refreshing. That follows from the definitions; it is not a finding that separate pursuits are bad for couples, and whether a partner reads one as a lower value placed on the pair is something no source found has measured. What outside novelty does to each partner's options, and whether it is a defection that needs deterring, belongs to other essays in this series[^15] and is not used here.

The same arithmetic describes a couple's sexual routine as it does any other shared routine.[^16]

Now, how big should an experiment be? A bigger experiment teaches more, but the first bit teaches most and each further step adds less. The chance of it going badly, meanwhile, grows faster than its size. Put those two together and the best size is somewhere in the middle: big enough to learn something, small enough to survive being wrong. The best size rises with how stale the picture is, because there is more to learn. It falls with how much a failed experiment would cost, because the more fragile the confidence, the less you can afford to get wrong. And an experiment larger than the best one can flip sign: in the model's illustration, with a fairly stale picture and middling fragility, the same kind of experiment that helps at a tenth of full size does real damage at six tenths and beyond. That is the conditional case for small, reversible experiments. It holds only if what you learn grows more slowly than the size of the experiment while the risk grows faster—both assumptions, and no source tests either. If a grand gesture risked no more than a modest one, the best experiment would be as large as you could afford.

There is a separate reason couples tend to drift to the smallest size of all, which is none. In a 1991 paper on organisations, James March argued that exploitation tends to crowd out exploration because its returns are nearer, surer and closer to home.[^17] That is a claim about organisations. Here it is an analogy for why the routine wins by default, not a result about couples.

> **The decision in one box** *(an illustration of the structure walked through above; no new terms)*
>
> Each evening there are three choices.
>
> - **Stay in the usual.** Costs nothing extra. The evening may wear a little, and the picture ages a little either way.
> - **Do something different that draws out nothing.** Repairs a worn evening. Leaves the picture as it was. Carries whatever risk it carries.
> - **Look: do something, new or familiar, that draws out something you did not know.** Repairs the picture, by more the staler it was; if it is also new, repairs the evening too. Carries a risk that, by assumption, grows faster than its size.
>
> Whether a look helps depends on how stale the picture is, how big the look is, and how much a bad evening would cost the confidence you both rest on. Done without your partner, it updates nothing. And before any of it, the asking is itself read (Section 8).

## 7. Two People, Two Appetites

So far there has been one chooser. There are two.

The two of you do not mind looking equally. One finds a new thing mildly enjoyable in itself; the other finds it tiring, or unnerving, or one more thing on a week that is already full. The staleness falls on both of you: each pays when the other's picture is wrong, and when their own is. You settle on how often to look, together, and each of you privately compares that with what you would have picked alone. Either of you can be either partner, and the roles can swap by subject and by year.

In the model, appetite for variety is the flip side of what a look costs you, net of whatever you enjoy about it. That cost contains both taste and time, which Section 9 pulls apart.

If the two of you can in effect compensate each other, trading an evening of one thing for a concession on another, then the frequency that keeps your combined cost lowest is pulled toward the partner for whom looking costs most, not toward the middle. In the illustration, if one partner on their own would look about once every three evenings and the other about once every nine, the shared rate that keeps the combined cost lowest is about once every seven rather than every four. The plain claim: each extra look costs the reluctant partner a lot and saves the keen one only a little. By the same arithmetic, deferring to the reluctant partner costs the keen one less than deferring to the keen one costs the reluctant one. All of this leans on the compensation assumption, which is strong. The sentence is also built on transferable-utility and cost-ratio assumptions that do not hold if partners cannot trade or if the cost gap is small. Without compensation, who sets the default is a bargaining question this essay does not solve.

A sharper question is whether you disagree about how much, or about whether. If the person keeps wandering, the reluctant partner also wants some looking, because the picture of them keeps getting worse; the disagreement is only about frequency. If the person keeps returning to a settled type, the reluctant partner may want none at all once looking costs them more than a threshold, and only then is the conflict a straight mismatch of tastes. No study found measures appetite for variety in both members of the same couple, so nobody knows which case is common.

The cost of "I'm just not a novelty person" sits here. The staleness still lands on you. And if your partner keeps changing, you want some looking too, only less of it than they do.

## 8. What "Something Different" Sounds Like

Back to the table, and the four words.

Before any of the above can happen, someone has to say something like *I want something different*. Your partner does not know which of two people is saying it: one who means "I like us; let's change something", or one who means "I am looking for the way out". They have to decide how to take it, and you will read how they take it in turn. What your partner infers from the words, and what you infer from their reaction, is the whole game.

If both kinds of asker say the same words, the words tell your partner nothing new. Your partner's update comes entirely from their prior belief—what they already thought the probability was. In the vocabulary used across this series, words that cost the same to say regardless of what you mean are cheap talk; when both kinds of asker send the same message, they pool, and the listener learns only what they already believed.[^18] So a vague request is read at the average of the people who might be sending it. The listener engages if the chance that the asker is heading for the door is below a cutoff set by what engaging gains when the request is honest and what it costs when it is not. With invented payoffs in which meeting an exit-minded request halfway costs twice what meeting an honest one gains, the cutoff is one in three: put the worrying reading at a third or less, and engaging pays; above it, it does not.

Now run it from the asker's side. When the listener's starting suspicion is high enough, the honest asker does better not to ask, because a vague request will be met defensively. That silence has a cost the model can name: the picture keeps ageing. Not asking is a choice, not the absence of one.

What separates the two askers is not tone but cost. A specific plan that commits time together (this Saturday, this thing, the two of you) separates them if it costs the asker heading for the door more than it costs the one staying, as it would if the first values time in the pair less. That ordering is an assumption, and there is no evidence for it. For the separation to work, the cost of the plan must sit between the two askers' valuations of being engaged—assuming both kinds of asker want to be engaged at all.

There is some reason to think the vague form is the usual one. In one set of studies, bored partners reliably turned away from familiar, security-restoring activities but did not reliably propose novel ones.[^19] If that holds, most real requests are closer to "not this" than to "this, instead", which is the pooling case.

Each seat gets a rule, and both are conditional on the payoffs assumed. For the listener: a vague request is an average of senders, and what should move you is specificity, not wording; your starting suspicion that it means leaving is what a history of reassurance changes, not anything said tonight. For the asker: a vague request will be read at that starting suspicion, whatever you meant, so the content of the ask matters more than how warmly it is put.

"I'm not leaving, I just need something different" lands in the same pool as an asker who is. "They overreacted" meets its cost when the starting suspicion was high and the reaction was the reasonable reading of an average.

## 9. When It Is Not About Taste

Your partner's rate of change and your cost of looking are different things, and the second has two parts. If the evenings are too short, you can want more novelty and have no room for it. From outside, and sometimes from inside, that looks exactly like not wanting it.

In the model, a fourfold rise in the net cost of a look—which bundles the time it takes and how much each partner enjoys it—halves the best frequency of looking and more than doubles the combined cost of staleness and looking, with no change in how fast your partner changes and no change in how much either of you likes new things. A time squeeze produces the same signs as a fall in appetite. In practice no change teaches nothing and no conversation leaves the evening unchanged; the two are ideal types that a real design would approach.

That is an identification problem. The cost of a look contains a time part and a taste part, and how often a couple does new things cannot tell them apart. What could is a change in free time from outside (a grandparent who takes the children every Saturday, a shorter commute) set against a change in how pleasant the activities are. No study found for this essay uses that kind of variation.

When the trouble is time, the model points away from novelty. The efficient response is the cheap look: the real conversation inside the usual routine, the middle column of Table 1, which repairs a stale picture without the extra hours. That holds only if the conversation genuinely draws something out; a check-in that runs on rails is a change that teaches nothing.

Two accounts from psychology sit close to this one and compete with the novelty story rather than support it. Parenthood is associated with lower marital satisfaction, most in the years with very young children.[^20] And the "suffocation" account argues that couples now ask marriage to meet higher needs while investing less time and attention in it.[^21] Both are about time and expectations. Neither is evidence that couples need novelty, and the second is contested. Who does the housework, and how it bears on the rest, belongs to another essay in this series;[^22] here "no time" is only a constraint on what a look costs.

"We've just got no time" meets its cost in two ways. For some couples it is the right diagnosis, and the model then says to spend less on novelty and more on the cheap look. And "it's just the kids" is not a diagnosis on its own: the same pattern appears when the picture has gone stale and the time has gone too.

## 10. What The Evidence Can And Cannot Say

Couples who do more new things are happier. Couples who are happier also do more new things. And any sample of long-married couples consists of the ones who stayed. Each of those facts limits what any study of novelty can show, and this essay claims a mechanism and its conditions, not a ranking and not a trend.

Boredom does seem to matter later, in one sample. In 123 couples interviewed in their seventh year of marriage and again nine years on, feeling in a rut at year seven predicted lower satisfaction at year sixteen, while satisfaction at year seven did not significantly predict later boredom, which the authors read as evidence against a purely reverse story.[^23] It is one item, one Detroit-area sample of couples who stayed married, and one null result, which does not settle the direction.

The novelty experiments are short and unspecific. The lab tasks lasted minutes; the longest randomised design ran ten weeks and found that exciting beat pleasant but neither beat doing nothing new.[^10] Independent replication is thin, and none of the studies separates information about the partner from arousal or from time spent together.[^9] [^11]

Evidence on whether couples decline cuts both ways, and the essay makes no claim about it. In a national panel followed over seventeen years, the familiar U-shape in which satisfaction dips and recovers turned out to be an artefact of comparing different couples at one time; followed individually, happiness fell at every duration.[^24] In studies of newlyweds, most couples stayed high, with large declines concentrated among those who started low.[^25] The model is consistent with either. Its loss is measured against a perfectly updated couple, not against the couple's own past, so it predicts no trend in average satisfaction—a couple that keeps updating well keeps no better than before; a couple that stops updating falls compared with that benchmark, not compared with themselves at an earlier time.

Some rival explanations could be separated by data in principle: reverse causation by experiment, at least in the short run, and the selection of who stays by following leavers as well as stayers. Others cannot be with anything found: whether a couple's flatness is a worn groove or a stale picture, and whether it is taste or time. Whether what is missing is distance and separateness rather than new activities—the sense that two people have remained distinct persons—was not addressed by any source found.[^26] Mood, conflict avoidance and misattribution of dissatisfaction are each potential rivals that the data cannot separate. The samples behind the core experiments are mostly students, or couples in the United States and Canada. Same-sex couples, couples outside the West and couples together for decades are largely missing.

The model makes two predictions that data could in principle falsify. First: an intervention that increases real information about the partner—measured independently of arousal or time spent—should improve satisfaction more than one that adds novelty alone, when the picture is stale. Second: the same intervention should harm satisfaction when the picture is fresh, because confidence is spent on learning nothing new. Neither has been tested.

H5 in the framework: whether the model is missing distance and separateness as a good in itself—the value of two people remaining distinct persons who continue to surprise each other—stays open. Adding it would require a different object (partner novelty as a primitive rather than as evidence about a person), and it would reverse the sign of staleness on satisfaction. That revision is available and would not destroy the structure; it would change what you should do about it.

## 11. Where The Claim Stops Holding

The claim has edges, and each is a setting of the model, not an excuse. In three groups:

**Partner and picture.** Your partner is not changing, or keeps returning to a settled type. If they do not change, looking again never pays. If they wander and return, an old picture serves longer and looking pays only when it is cheap. And there is the edge the model does not cover: accuracy the partner does not want—being known more precisely than they find comfortable.

**Cost and confidence.** The staleness is really a time budget; more novelty is then the wrong remedy, and the cheap look is the right one (Section 9). Confidence is fragile; any request reads as a bid for the door, and experiments cost more than they return, so the best experiment shrinks toward nothing and the honest asker stays quiet (Sections 6 and 8). The only novelty available bypasses the relationship; it cannot refresh, by construction (Section 6). The asker has changed, not the partner; then the stale estimate is in the wrong direction, and the model runs in reverse.

**Preferences and what the model leaves out.** Both of you prefer security, and your partner settles back to type; neither of you wants to look, and there is no trade-off to manage (Section 7). Routine is a good in itself—the particular pleasure of doing the same thing in the same way with the same person, irreducible to the picture's freshness. Nothing wears; the worn-groove half of the argument then goes, and the stale picture remains. Happiness causes novelty, or seeking novelty changes what the relationship means; both sit outside the model. It does not say they are false; it does not handle them.

What is left after those edges is the claim in its proper size. Novelty competes with closeness when it teaches you nothing about your partner or costs more confidence than it gains; the rest of the time it is how the picture is kept current.

Where that claim stands needs saying once, plainly. The stale-estimate framing is this essay's own mapping. No economics model of a couple with a depreciating, updated picture of each other was found; psychology's work on intimacy as an ongoing process of disclosure and response,[^27] [^28] and on how well partners know each other, may already say much of this under other names. That has not been checked, and the essay does not claim the idea is new.

---

## 12. The Objection That The Model Is Unnecessary

The strongest objection is not that the model is cold. It is that the model is unnecessary.

> *You have dressed up "keep learning about each other" in a slot machine. Psychology already treats intimacy as an ongoing exchange of disclosure and responsiveness, not a stock, so the picture you argue against is a straw man. Love is not a bandit. The data say couples decline whatever they do. And every quantity that would make the model bite—how fast people change, what a look costs, how much an experiment risks—is unknown.*

Most of that deserves a concession. The slogan is old, and the intimacy-process view does treat closeness as something done continuously rather than something owned.[^27] [^28] If the model added only "keep learning", it would add nothing.

What it adds is three conditional claims the slogan does not make explicit. The same activity can help or harm depending on how stale the picture is, so the question "is novelty good for us?" has no answer without that (Section 6). Two kinds of wear call for different remedies, and "do something new" fixes only one (Section 5). And a shortage of time produces the same visible pattern as a fall in appetite, so frequency alone cannot tell you which you have (Section 9). Whether psychology already has these in other words is unchecked, as Section 11 says.

Love is not a bandit, and the essay does not say it is. It says that part of the question of what to do on a Tuesday has that shape, and Section 11 lists what the shape leaves out.

The data on decline are answered in Section 10: no trend is claimed, and selection is a caution on any study, not a variable in this one. As for the unknown quantities: correct, which is why the essay offers mechanisms and conditions, not numbers. Each unknown has a named side, and the conclusions say which side does what.

Three secondary objections, briefly. *Seeking novelty means you are not satisfied.* That is the pooling reading of Section 8, and what separates it from the honest reading is specificity, if specificity costs the two kinds of asker differently. *This is advice in disguise.* Each rule here is a conditional with a named way to fail; none says what you should want. *Treating a partner as something to be estimated is cold.* That is a fair limit; the model says how a picture of a person goes out of date, and it is silent on what the picture is for.

What survives is a condition, not a slogan: the sign of a new thing's effect depends on the state of the picture, and the two kinds of wear call for different repairs.

---

## 13. Questions For Each Seat

None of these says what to do. Each asks which side of a line a situation is on.

If you are the one who wants something different:

**What do you think you know about your partner that you have not checked in a while?** When did they last surprise you, and about what? If you cannot remember, that is information about the age of the picture, not about them.

**Is it the evenings that have worn, or the picture?** Would a change of scene with nothing learned in it help? Would a real conversation in the usual routine? And what is the cheapest thing that would teach you something? Nothing shows which looks are cheapest for actual couples; the question is which one is cheapest for yours.

**Is your idea something the two of you would do together?** If not, what would it update about the pair?

**How big is it, and what happens if it goes badly?** Is there a smaller version that would teach most of what the big one would?

If you are the one being asked:

**How likely do you think it is that "something different" means leaving, and what is that based on?** Does the request come with a specific plan for the two of you, or only a dissatisfaction?

**What does looking cost you: time, or taste?** If an hour came back to you every week, would your answer change?

---

## 14. What You Were Going To Say

Go back to the table. The plates are by the sink, the show is waiting, your partner has looked up and said "What?", and you have a second, maybe less.

The idea you have been rehearsing since four o'clock is not good or bad on its own. It competes with what the two of you have if it would teach you nothing about your partner, or if it risks more than it could return. It may be how what you have is kept, if your picture of them has gone stale and the idea is small enough to survive being wrong. And if the real trouble is that the evenings have no room left in them, none of this is about novelty at all.

You cannot settle which from your chair. The certainty you felt about how your partner would answer, the pause, the "sure, if you want", the *where's this coming from?*, is itself the picture, and it is exactly as old as the last time you checked it. You knew the show, the sofa and the hall light. You did not know what the book was about.

Whether you say it is still open. The harder question is underneath it: when did you last find out something about them you did not already expect?
