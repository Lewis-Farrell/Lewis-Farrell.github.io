## 1. The Ten Minutes At The Table

Your partner has gone to the bar. Or stepped outside to take a call, or gone looking for the bathroom; it does not matter which, only that it will be about ten minutes, and that you are now alone at a table with four people who have known your partner far longer than you have. Someone tops up your glass without asking. Someone else asks what you do, and listens to the answer a little longer than the answer deserves. A story starts about a holiday years ago, one you were not on, and it is told in your direction, with a pause at the end that seems to be waiting for something. You laugh in the right place. You are not entirely sure it was the right place.

Everyone at the table is deciding something about you, and nobody is saying what. You can feel the sorting without seeing its terms. One of them is warm in a way that might be kindness and might be a test. One has gone quiet, and you cannot tell whether that is boredom or attention. Not all of them, you suspect, want the same outcome: one might be glad to see your partner settled, another might be quietly sorry to lose a standing Thursday. A question about how long the two of you have been seeing each other gets an answer from you slightly too fast, and it hangs there a beat longer than it should. Underneath all of it sits a second doubt you can do nothing about. Whatever this table concludes, which part of it will reach your partner, and in whose words? And which part of you will reach this table next time, in your partner's?

Somewhere, at some point, someone told you how this works. *Don't try to impress her, try to impress her friends.* Maybe it was offered as a joke, maybe as hard-won wisdom; either way it stuck, and it is the only rule you have for these ten minutes. It sounds like advice. It is also a claim, and a strange one: that the people at this table matter more to your prospects than the person who has just gone to the bar. Then your partner comes back, sits down, rests a hand on the back of your chair, and the conversation moves on to something else. Nobody tells you what was decided. You cannot tell whether that was the audition or only the small talk.

## 2. Taking The Tip Seriously

This essay takes the tip seriously enough to derive it, which means treating it as economics rather than folklore. It reads the tip as a claim about information: that how you treat your partner's friends shows what you are really like in a way that charming your partner does not. The tip has other readings (do not try too hard with your partner; be good company), and impressing friends can pay for reasons that have nothing to do with information: a partner who wants their friends' approval, a calmer life when they like you, allies later, status. None of those is modelled here.

On the information reading, the essay does three things. First, it shows why the tip can work at all: a performance for your partner's friends can tell a good partner from a poor one where charming your partner directly cannot, but only if the performance costs the wrong kind of person more than the right kind. Second, it shows whose incentives run it. Your partner's friends do not read you neutrally; they have interests of their own, they hear about you partly through what your partner chooses to tell them, and their verdicts travel on to other people. Third, it says when the tip fails, including one result most readers will not expect: the same ten minutes pay off in different ways depending on where your partner already stands.

Three boundaries apply throughout. The tip comes out as a conditional (a window, in its simplest form), not a rule. Every claim here is a result in a simple model, an assumption stated as one, or a gap stated as a gap. And there is no dating evidence that the tip works; where that absence matters the essay says so, and Sections 10 and 11 say what the data could and could not show.

The place to start is the person who is not at the table, and why their own impression of you is not enough.

## 3. Why Your Partner's Own Impression Is Not Enough

Strip the evening down to its moving parts. You have a limited amount of effort, and you can spend it in two places: on your partner directly, or on a friend of your partner's while your partner is out of the room. Your partner has one decision to make, whether to go on with you. Your partner can see how you treat them. The friend sees how you treat them, and may have picked up something about you from elsewhere. Afterwards the friend gives your partner one word, yes or no, and your partner gives that word some deference: the chance that the friend's word is what decides it.

One piece of vocabulary. A good partner, here, is one who treats your partner well when nobody else is watching; a poor one does not. "Unwatched" is the whole meaning. It says nothing about sex, and it is the part the friends cannot see directly.

What makes the friend's word worth having? Consider what your partner sees first-hand. In the model, attentiveness, compliments and the easy warmth of someone who is keen are things almost anyone who wants to stay can produce: flattery aimed at your partner costs a person who would be good to them when unwatched exactly what it costs a person who would not. When something costs everyone the same, everyone who wants the prize does it, and watching it tells you nothing about who is doing it. Your partner's own impression of you, in this setup, is close to useless for telling a good partner from a poor one.

That is an assumption about one channel, not a verdict on your partner's judgement. If attention aimed at your partner could separate people (if keeping track of their particular history costs someone who does not care more than someone who does), then direct effort can do the sorting and nothing about friends is special. The tip beats direct effort, in the model, only where direct effort fails to separate. Why it would fail is an assumption the next section puts under pressure.

Two inferences now carry the evening. The friend infers something about you from what you do at the table. Your partner infers something about you from what the friend says. The tip is a bet that the second inference, routed through the first, carries information that your partner's own impression does not.

Economists call the first half signalling (you act, knowing your action will be read) and, loosely, the second half screening (your partner uses a test they cannot run alone). In this model the friends are the test.

Is there a second impression to be had at all? There are hints that the people around a couple see it differently from the couple. In a three-wave study, the members of couples' social networks were more negative about the relationships than the couples were, and both groups' views predicted which relationships ended.^1 In two longitudinal studies of students, the students were more optimistic and more confident about their own relationships than their parents and roommates were, and less accurate.^2 Both are observational, and both draw mostly on student or young-adult samples. They show that a differently pitched view exists. They do not show that it adds anything to the partner's own, and they say nothing about whether performing for friends works: the observers in these studies watched the couple together over time, and none received a performance while the partner was away. The same results also fit a different story, in which an infatuated partner is simply over-optimistic and observers help by de-biasing, from watching the couple, not from anything done for them at the table. On that story, performing for friends would do little.

## 4. The Window

Say you spend the ten minutes remembering what each friend does for a living and asking about it. That is a performance: effort aimed at the friends, and it costs something. Here is the central result in the simplest version of the model (one friend, one meeting, a performer who knows their own cost, effort that is all or nothing). There is a way for a performance like that to sort good partners from poor ones if the prize riding on it is big enough that the right person bothers, and small enough that the wrong person does not bother to fake it.

The prize is not your partner's whole decision. It is a fraction of it, built from three things multiplied together: what is at stake for you if your partner goes on with you; how far your partner defers to what the friends say; and how likely the friends are to see the performance, or to hear of it from someone who did. On the other side sit two costs: what the performance costs a person who would be good to your partner when unwatched, and what it costs a person who would not. If the prize lies between those two costs, the good person performs and the other does not, and the friends' yes means something. If it does not, it does not.

Say the performance costs the good person 2 and the other person 8. The table runs the all-or-nothing arithmetic with invented numbers. Here and throughout, numbers illustrate structure and are not estimates of anything about real people.

| Case | At stake for you | Deference | Chance the friends see it | Prize | Good person (cost 2) | Other person (cost 8) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Prize too big | 20 | 0.5 | 1.0 | 10.0 | Performs | Performs: no separation |
| Inside the window | 10 | 0.5 | 1.0 | 5.0 | Performs | Abstains |
| Prize too small | 10 | 0.5 | 0.3 | 1.5 | Abstains | Abstains |

Only the middle row separates. When the prize is too big the other person fakes it and the friends' yes means nothing; when it is too small nobody bothers. In the all-or-nothing version, with these numbers, the friends must see or hear of the performance at least four times in ten for the window to open.

Two things follow, and both matter later.

The first is the mimicry gap, this series' term for how cheaply a misaligned person can imitate an aligned one.^3 The performance has to cost the wrong person more than the right one. A gesture that is costly in money (picking up the round) separates nobody if the wrong person is no poorer. A gesture that is costly in attention might separate, if attention costs the wrong person more. The model does not say where such a gap would come from. Perhaps sustained interest in a partner's world is cheaper for someone who cares about the partner; perhaps it is not. That is a conjecture, nothing in the record measures it, and without the gap the window is empty.

Put that next to Section 3 and the structure shows: the tip rests on two opposite assumptions about cost. Flattery aimed at your partner costs everyone the same, so your partner's own impression pools. A performance for the friends costs the wrong person more, so theirs separates. The essay does not claim either describes real couples. The result is sensitive to the first: if flattery's cost differs even slightly by type, direct effort separates too and the friends' channel is redundant.

The second consequence is a comparison, not a separate result. Because the prize scales with how much rides on the channel, if direct effort also had a cost gap, the larger prize riding on your partner's decision would invite mimicry while the smaller prize riding on the friends could stay inside the window. In the all-or-nothing version, that is the model's best case for the tip over direct effort, and it is the cost-gap assumption restated. The window itself belongs to the all-or-nothing performance the model uses. If effort were a matter of degree, with the wrong person's cost a multiple above the right person's, standard signalling logic says the wrong person is priced out at any prize size, by the level of effort required, and the good person keeps a positive surplus. Both edges then disappear, and what survives is a positive prize plus a cost gap. Two things are left out. Someone who means to be good has more to gain from your partner staying, a different source of separation, through the prize and not the cost. And what you do while your partner is away shows how you behave unwatched, of which the friends are one audience and the waiter and your partner's parents are others.

So in the all-or-nothing version the tip is a window: a range of prizes inside which performing tells good from poor. With graded effort the edges loosen and what remains is a positive prize and a cost gap; the conditions below are what keep the prize positive and the gap real. The rest of this essay moves them.

## 5. Your Partner's Friends Are Not Neutral

An earlier essay in this series looked at friends and family as enforcers, people who would respond if one partner broke the relationship's rules, and left open that third parties have interests of their own.^4 This essay starts there, but earlier in the relationship: before any commitment, with friends as a source of information.

Take the friend from the table who would be quietly sorry to lose a standing Thursday. That friend knows something about you that your partner does not, having watched you from outside the relationship. But the friend does not want exactly what your partner wants: they might lose time with your partner, might distrust anyone your partner dates, might simply be protective. The friend decides how much of what they know to pass on. If the gap between their wants and your partner's is small, the friend can say a lot: several grades of opinion. If it is large, less: two grades, then nothing your partner can use.

Talk of this kind, words that cost nothing to say, is what economists call cheap talk, and the classic analysis is Crawford and Sobel's. In their model the better-informed party cannot credibly report the truth itself, only which band it falls in, and communication is more informative the closer the two parties' preferences are.^5 Mapping their model onto a friend advising your partner is this essay's own step; they wrote about senders and receivers in general, not about friends.

The textbook version assumes your quality is spread evenly over a scale from 0 to 1, errors cost the square of their size, your partner knows the friend's bias, and your partner's choice is a point on the scale. Your partner's real choice is go or not, so the model is used here as a picture of what gets communicated, not as your partner's decision problem. On those assumptions, with the friend's bias measured as a share of the whole scale, the number of grades the friend can credibly convey falls like this:

| Friend's bias: how far apart the two want things, as a share of the whole scale | Most grades of opinion the friend can credibly convey |
| --- | --- |
| 0.01 | 7 |
| 0.05 | 3 |
| 0.10 | 2 |
| 0.25 or more | 1 (nothing usable) |

Read the last row with care. A bias of a quarter of the whole scale is a very large divergence. Short of it, the friend's advice is coarse, not empty: the robust prediction is that friends speak in few categories, not that they say nothing useful. The table shows how coarse the advice gets, not how a decision is made.

The size of the bias sets how much the friend can say; its direction sets where the coarseness falls. A protective friend, who wants your partner to be more demanding than your partner would be, gives a "not great" that covers a wide stretch of the scale (more than half of it at a bias of 0.05) and a "really good" that is narrow and precise. A friend who wants your partner settled gives the mirror image. Both words carry information; one carries less. So the tempting reading, that a sceptical friend's no is worthless while their yes is gold, is not a Crawford-Sobel result. A version of it survives under different conditions, derived in the next section.

The friend's own interests can do more than coarsen the advice. What does the friend lose if your partner is partnered at all? Time, standing, a match the friend preferred. If that loss exceeds what the friend would gain from your partner being with someone good, the friend disapproves even of a partner they know to be good; their word carries nothing, and the prize for performing for them is zero. If the loss is real but smaller, the friend is just more demanding than your partner: their yes is rarer and means more. Whether friends typically carry such a cost is open. One study's abstract notes that friendships can compete with romantic partners for companionship;^6 that is consistent with the idea, not a measurement of it.

"Friends are biased, so discount them" sounds like realism. Its cost is that their messages are coarse, not worthless, and discounting them wholesale throws away what they say at the end of the scale where the partition is finer.

The model treats a friend as a cutoff and a bias. A friend is a person, with a history with your partner that no parameter captures. The model can say which part of a friend's role is information and which is interest; it cannot say whether information is the main thing friends are for.

## 6. When Your Partner Cannot Tell Which Friend They Have

This is a different kind of bias from the last section's: not a pull in the friend's preferred direction, but a friend who says no whatever they saw. Crawford and Sobel's listener knows the friend's bias. Your partner usually does not. So change the game. Some friends are aligned: they want what your partner wants and tell the truth as they see it. Some say no to everyone, whatever they saw. Your partner knows roughly how common the second kind is, but not which kind is speaking, and hears one word from one friend.

Follow the words back to their sources. A yes can come from only one place: an aligned friend whose impression of you was favourable. A no can come from two: an aligned friend whose impression was unfavourable, and every friend who says no to everyone. So as the share of such friends rises, approval gets rarer but keeps its force, and disapproval gets commoner and loses most of it. With friends whose impressions are right four times in five, and a partner who starts at even odds:

| Share of friends who say no to everyone | 0 | 0.25 | 0.5 | 0.75 | 0.9 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Chance your partner hears a yes | 0.50 | 0.375 | 0.25 | 0.125 | 0.05 |
| What a yes does to the odds you are good | ×4 | ×4 | ×4 | ×4 | ×4 |
| What a no does to the odds you are good | ×0.25 | ×0.47 | ×0.67 | ×0.84 | ×0.94 |

A multiplier of 1 would mean no information. Approval stays at four to one however many such friends there are. Disapproval drifts towards one.

The asymmetry has a precedent. Morgan and Stocken studied stock analysts whose investors were unsure whether the analyst's incentives were aligned with theirs, and found that, under certain conditions, aligned analysts can credibly convey unfavourable information but not favourable information.^7 Analysts lean towards favourable reports, and it is the unfavourable word that carries weight; a friend who leans the other way would be expected to show the mirror pattern. The asymmetry here is derived in this model. Morgan and Stocken is a precedent, not a proof.

The result carries four conditions:

- The friend's lean points one way and does not depend on who you are.
- Your partner is unsure which kind of friend is speaking.
- The exchange is one-shot. Over many partners, a friend who says no to everyone might say yes now and then to keep later noes credible; that case is not derived.
- A yes is not strategically available to that friend.

Reverse the lean, a friend who wants your partner settled with anyone, and the picture mirrors: the no becomes the conclusive word and the yes loses its force. Which way friends usually lean is unknown. Network members in one study were more negative about couples than the couples were,^1 and in another, closeness to a best friend went with lower stability;^6 both are consistent with a lean against partners, and neither tests it. Several friends who share a lean are not independent draws, and how to combine their words is not derived.

Now the decision. Your partner goes on unless a no makes you look too unlikely to be good. The bar comes from the stakes: if going on with a good partner is worth 4 to your partner and with a poor one costs 3, your partner goes on with anyone at least 43 percent likely to be good.

The same logic splits the tip in two. If your partner is already leaning in, only a no can change the outcome: the friends' word works as a veto, and you are playing to avoid disapproval. If your partner is wary, only a yes can change it: the word works as a certificate, and you are playing for approval. In the simplest version the prize is the same size either way, and it shrinks with the share of friends who say no to everyone in both cases, since a yes needs an aligned friend. What differs is what happens past a point.

In the veto case, a no stops mattering once enough friends say no to everyone. Suppose your partner starts at even odds on you and the friends' impressions are conclusive. Then the threshold is three friends in four. Below it, a no leaves you too unlikely to be good and your partner walks. At or above it, your partner rationally ignores the no, and performing for those friends changes nothing. The threshold depends on how good the friends' impressions are: if they are right four times in five, as in the table, a no does less damage and stops mattering sooner, at about three in five (0.62). In the certificate case a yes keeps its force for any share below one.

With conclusive impressions, below the threshold, the cost of such friends to your partner is good matches lost, never bad ones accepted: a good partner is turned away with probability equal to their share.

The tempting line, "my partner's friend says no to everyone, so the no can be ignored", is true in the model only above the threshold; below it, that friend's veto costs your partner good matches. And if it is true and your partner knows it, the no stops mattering, and so does whatever you did for that friend: your prize shrinks with the share of friends who would never say yes, and past the threshold it is gone.

## 7. You Are Read Through Your Partner's Edit

The friends learn about you in two ways. One is by watching what you do for them, the chance in Section 4. The other is through your partner's account of the two of you, which is this section. The second channel does not carry your performance. It gives the friends a second, selected view of you, and it is also how the friends' impression comes to be partly your partner's own.

In the model, your partner is at the table again and again, was present for a great many moments with you, and chooses which to pass on. Telling a good story pays the teller something, a lift among their friends, and the vivid stories pay most. The friends then read you through what was told.

The stories that reach the table are not a random sample of your time together. Following economic models in which people care how an audience sees them,^8 the essay treats the lift from a good story as a payoff to the teller: a modelling device, not a claim about anyone's psychology, and with no dating evidence behind it. The nearest evidence is from elsewhere. In experiments on sharing, people broadcasting to many were less likely than people speaking to one to pass on things that made them look bad;^9 in a lab game, gossip carried reputations and could override what players had seen for themselves.^10 None of this is about couples.

Suppose your partner tells one moment in five and picks the most tellable. In the textbook case, where how tellable a moment is and how good it was are jointly normal, even a correlation of +0.3 leaves two-thirds of your best fifth of moments untold, and at zero correlation four-fifths. That is arithmetic for that case, not a finding about people. What matters for how the friends see you is the average quality of what is told. At zero correlation the told moments are typical, an unbiased but small sample; at +0.3 they average about 0.4 standard deviations better than typical, and at −0.3 about 0.4 worse. These figures assume your partner pays no attention to how well a story shows they chose; if they care about that, the sign can change. The real question is the sign and size of the correlation, and no source found for this essay measures it. A friend who understands the selection can correct for it; one who does not will not. The suspicion that the grand gesture makes a better story than a better evening is the negative case: a possibility, not a result.

Selection also changes what you do. If approval rides on retold moments, a rational performer bends effort towards the tellable ones, at the expense of your partner's own evening. The more the performer plays to the friends' approval, the larger the shift, and with enough weight on it nearly all the effort goes there. The direction is the result; its size comes from an assumed shape for the costs.

One more consequence reaches the foundation of the tip. In the three-wave study, the authors' tests of how friends come to hold predictive views supported a role for what the couple discloses to them.^1 To that extent the part of the friend's view that is the couple's own account is your partner's own, retold, and adds nothing new; what the friend sees directly still does. This is the first of the tip's failure conditions. The friends' view still differed from the couples' (it was more negative), but a more negative view is also what a friend who leans against partners, or who has interests of their own, would give. The difference alone does not show that the friends know something your partner does not. How much of their view is independent information, nobody knows. Section 11 returns to it.

Two pairings. "My partner only tells the best stories, so I am not read fairly" has its cost: the same selection can make you look worse than a typical evening as easily as better, and the sign is unknown. "Then I will do what is tellable" has its own: the effort comes out of your partner's experience.

## 8. When Friends Hear From Friends

A friend's verdict does not stop at your partner. It travels outward, and three things happen to it on the way.

First, a friend who vouches for you to their own group puts standing at stake, so they vouch only when fairly sure; the bigger the stake, the surer they need to be, and a vouch becomes rarer and means more. Certification models offer a parallel: a profit-maximising monopoly certifier may do best by disclosing only whether quality clears a minimum standard, a second route to coarse messages.^11 Reading friends as certifiers is a step beyond that literature, whose intermediaries sell certification; a friend's stake is an assumption here. It also disciplines the friend's threshold, not your later conduct: in field experiments on job referrals, referrals carried real information about workers, but the data did not support the idea that referred workers worked harder to protect their referrer.^12

Second, each hop loses information. Suppose the friend's impression is right four times in five and each retelling passes the verdict on correctly nine times in ten. Then the verdict's chance of being right falls from 0.80 at the friend to 0.74, 0.69, 0.65 and 0.62 over four hops. The verdict that reaches a friend's friend is less reliable than the one their friend held, before any bias enters.

Third, order starts to matter. Picture a group chat where friends answer one at a time with a thumb or a one-word verdict. Each sees the earlier verdicts, yes or no, but not the reasons, and each has a private impression of you that is right with some fixed probability, independent of the others' once we know whether you are good. Each says whatever is best given everything they know. Once the yeses lead the noes by two, or the reverse, the next friend's own impression no longer changes what they say: two matching verdicts outweigh one private impression, so they follow. Everyone after does the same. This is an information cascade, in the sense of Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer and Welch, and of Banerjee's model of herding.^13

With impressions right seven times in ten, about 15.5 percent of the cascades that form lock onto the wrong verdict. In a group of eight, simply averaging the private impressions, with ties split as coin flips, would have been right about 87 percent of the time, against about 83 percent for the chain; the gap widens with group size. Position matters too. Suppose later friends simply count the verdicts, without knowing which friend has the sharper impression. Then making one friend's impression sharper (right eight times in ten rather than seven) raises the chance that a good partner ends up approved by about four percentage points if that friend speaks first or second, and by under one point if they speak seventh or eighth. If later friends do know who is sharper, the ordering can reverse. A performance a friend sees would sharpen that friend's impression, so loosely, it is worth most with the friends who speak first.

All of that needs verdicts that come in sequence, are coarse, and travel without reasons; a group chat of thumbs and one-word opinions might qualify. Friends who exchange reasons share their information and do not cascade. That friend groups judging a partner have this structure is an interpretation, not a finding. In the model a formed cascade is permanent, by assumption. In the lab, cascades form in most rounds where early choices line up,^14 and also collapse and re-form, with reversals usually towards the truth;^15 several studies find that few subjects behave as the model prescribes.^16 There is no field evidence on cascades among friends. And a group that agrees because agreeing confers status looks much the same from outside as a cascade;^17 the data cannot tell them apart.

"Everyone already agrees, so it is settled." In the model, a formed cascade can be wrong, about one time in six or seven in the example, and it stays wrong only because the model assumes it cannot break.

## 9. Where The Tip Fails

Every condition under which the tip fails is derived above or here. Five change the prize, the costs or who is performing (in the all-or-nothing version, they move an edge of the window). Three leave those as they are and change whether what the screen produces is worth having to your partner.

| # | Condition | What it does | Kind | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Part of the friend's view is the couple's own account | That part adds nothing new; what the friend sees directly still does | Changes what it is worth | Some support: the study found a role for couple disclosure^1 |
| 2 | Little deference | The prize falls to zero | Changes the prize or the costs | Model; no measure of deference exists |
| 3 | Friends who say no to everyone | The prize shrinks; zero past the threshold | Changes the prize or the costs | Model; direction of lean unknown |
| 4 | A cascade has formed | Later friends echo earlier ones; value falls with position, under a counting rule | Changes what it is worth | Model; mixed lab evidence |
| 5 | Cheap for the wrong person | Good company, poor when unwatched: no separation, so the screen measures sociability | Changes the prize or the costs | Model; no evidence |
| 6 | Displacement | Effort on them comes out of effort on your partner | Changes what it is worth | Model; no evidence |
| 7 | Friends rarely see or hear of it | The chance of being seen falls | Changes the prize or the costs | Model; no dating evidence |
| 8 | Friends gain from another outcome | Past a threshold, their word goes uninformative | Changes the prize or the costs | Model; magnitude unknown |

Every row marked "model" has no measure behind its decisive number. Three deserve more than a line, and one more point sits beside them.

**Conditions 2, 3 and 8.** Each sets the prize to zero by its route in Sections 5 and 6 (past a threshold, for 3 and 8). Deference has a sharper form: if the friends are right four times in five and your partner starts at even odds, a yes moves your partner to 80 percent and a no to 20. A partner whose bar sits at 15 percent, or at 90, is unmoved by either word.

**The weights are not fixed.** The model treats deference, the friends' lean and the friends' access as fixed features of the world. In life they can be moved, and the person with most reason to move them is the one the screen exists to catch. A poor partner's cheapest move may not be to fake a performance but to lower your partner's deference, discredit the friends, or limit what reaches them: to push the model's parameters so that the prize falls out of range. The model leaves strategic action on these weights out. Seen from your partner's seat, a story that the friends say no to everyone is a claim about a quantity your partner cannot observe, made by someone who gains if it is believed.

**Condition 6, displacement.** The tip helps your partner only if the effort diverted from them costs less than the bad matches the screen removes. With even odds, the payoffs above and deference of 0.5, the screen is worth 0.75 to your partner when no effort is diverted, 0.25 when the diverted effort costs 1, nothing at 1.5, and less than nothing beyond. Displacement is a condition on the tip, not a footnote to it. Nobody has measured it.

**Condition 5, the wrong person finds it easy.** Add a third kind of person: good company, poor when unwatched. If the performance costs them less than it costs the good partner, then whenever the good partner performs, they do too, and no prize separates them. The screen measures sociability, not conduct when unwatched. It may still be worth something. Suppose four in ten candidates are good, two in ten are charming but poor when unwatched, and four in ten are neither; with the payoffs above, two-thirds of performers are good, comfortably above your partner's 43 percent bar. Going on with performers pays your partner 1.0, against 1.6 for a perfect screen and −0.2 for going on with everyone. The screen helps only while the charming-but-poor are not too common relative to the good: in this example, once they outnumber the good by more than four to three, the performance is worse than useless as a ticket.

"I am not performing, I am genuinely friendly." Perhaps. But genuine friendliness is also cheap for the person who is good company and poor when unwatched, so it cannot be told from the outside. That says nothing against sincerity; it says the friends cannot read it as a signal.

The rest were derived in their places: independence in Sections 3 and 7, retelling in Section 7, cascades in Section 8. The tip works where the prize is positive and the cost gap is real (inside a window, in the all-or-nothing version), and it is worth having only if three further conditions hold.

## 10. What The Data Could And Could Not Show

There is a body of research on friends' approval and relationships. Perceived approval from the respondent's friends went with greater stability in one study,^6 and perceived support from the wider network went with deeper romantic involvement in another.^18 A widely cited 1970s finding that parental opposition intensified love did not hold up in a replication with 396 participants, which found the ordinary pattern instead: more perceived interference, or less approval, from friends and family went with poorer relationship quality.^19 The original concerned parents; the replication asked about friends and family together.

None of this can say why approval and stability go together. At least five explanations predict the same association. Friends might hold an independent impression, as the tip assumes. Approval might be conformity. Your partner might simply want the friends' approval, as a payoff in itself. Friends might enforce: the earlier essay's community that would respond to a breach predicts the same pattern.^4 Or couples who fit well might both earn approval and last, with no causal channel at all. Reasons for impressing friends that do not run through information, such as allies, a calmer life or status, could produce the association too, and are not modelled.

The approval studies face three concrete problems. The first is selection. In an illustration where approval has no effect whatever, a hidden match quality that raises both friends' approval and the relationship's survival still makes approval predict staying together, with a regression slope of about half an outcome standard deviation (the loadings of approval and survival on the hidden match quality are invented: 0.8 and 1.0). The second is reverse causation. Relationships also reshape who one's friends are: in one nine-month study of residents of a graduate housing community and their cohabiting partners, partnership ties strongly shaped who became friends with whom.^20 That is a different thing from friends' approval, but it shows the arrows run both ways. The third is measurement. In the stability study, perceived approval predicted stability better than actual approval,^6 and perceived approval is what most studies measure.

Behind these sits a more general difficulty. When people in a group behave alike, it is hard to tell whether they influence one another, share background, or chose to be near each other. Manski showed that in a standard linear setup the group's influence on a member cannot be separated from the group's characteristics, because the group's average behaviour is made of its members' behaviour, which moves with their traits.^21 Call the friends' problem a Manski-style identification limit. It is an analogy, since the reflection problem proper concerns a linear peer-effects model. The dating studies fail earlier: on selection (Manski's correlated effects), reverse causation and measurement.

Some of the competing explanations could in principle be separated by data, and Section 11 says how. Others could be separated only by theory: the independent impression against your partner's wish for approval, conformity against cascade in observational data, and any of them against enforcement. No study found for this essay varies the effort aimed at a partner's friends, or measures what friends know beyond what the partner told them.

## 11. The Serious Objection

The strongest objection is not that the model is unrealistic. It is that the whole tip rests on a cost gap nobody can point to.

> *Flattery costs every kind of person the same, so your partner's own impression pools. Performing for the friends costs the wrong person more, so theirs separates. Nothing says why. Without that second assumption the window is empty, and with a small cost gap in flattery the friends are redundant. And impressing friends can pay for reasons that have nothing to do with information, so "it pays nothing outside the window" is false of the tip, even if it is true of the screen.*

The reply concedes most of it. The cost gap is an assumption, labelled as one in Section 4, and it does all the work of separating people; the essay offers a conjecture about where it might come from and no evidence. What the model earns is a conditional: if there is a gap, here is the size of prize that keeps the screen working and what else must hold. As for the other reasons, a reason that pays whoever performs adds a prize that does not depend on who is performing, which rewards the wrong person too. It is a different tip, outside the window and outside the model.

A second objection has a source behind it. Your partner says you are wonderful, the friends say you are wonderful, and an observer cannot tell which came first. Friends' views are partly built from what the couple tells them,^1 which is the first failure condition. It does not make the friends' view worthless: the networks in that study differed from the couples, and both views predicted which relationships ended. But a more negative view is also what a friend who leans against partners would give, so the difference does not show that the friends know something your partner does not. The value of the tip depends on how much of the friends' word comes from what they saw and how much from what your partner told them, and nobody knows the split.

A conditional with this many exits can look as if it cannot fail. It can. Three of its predictions are checkable in principle:

- If friends speak in order with one-word verdicts, the first two verdicts should predict the group's verdict more than later ones. Group verdicts unrelated to order would count against the cascade reading.
- If told moments are selected on tellability, a log of the moments people rate best and the moments they retell should differ. No difference would count against selection.
- Where a partner is leaning in and believes the friends oppose every partner, the friends' disapproval should not change what the partner does. A partner who still moves would count against the unknown-bias result.

No study found for this essay runs any of them.

The model leaves out some common settings. Shared friends lose a friend whichever way the couple's end falls, which may push them to approve anyone or to say nothing. Couples introduced by a friend were endorsed before the table. For long-distance couples and friends met online, the "performance" is typed and the chance the friends see it is low, which is Section 4's too-small row. And the roles here are gender-neutral by choice of the model, not because nothing differs: the essay has no evidence on whether the stakes, the friend networks or the lean of friends' views differ by who is being assessed. "Your partner decides, not the friends" is a parameter: at zero deference the tip fails by derivation.

What survives is a positive prize and a cost gap (a window, in the all-or-nothing version), with the conditions that keep them named and three ways to show it wrong.

## 12. Which Side Of The Window Are You On?

None of these tells you what to do. Each asks which side of the window a situation is on. There are two seats, and the questions run both ways.

From the table:

**Does your partner's decision actually move with what the friends say?** On a no, on a yes, or on neither? If neither, the prize is zero, whatever happens at the table.

**Is your partner leaning in, or wary?** If leaning in, the friends' word can only work as a veto. If wary, it can only work as a certificate. The same evening is a different game in each case.

**What would the thing you might do for them cost someone who is poor when unwatched?** If it costs that person nothing, it tells the table nothing, however warmly it is received.

From your partner's seat:

**Where do the friends' views come from?** Their own contact with the person, or what you told them? And did the first two opinions you heard set all the rest?

**Does a friend's no mean something, or would they say it to anyone?** How do you know, and who told you?

**Has how much you listen to your friends changed since this relationship began?** And whose doing was that?

---

## 13. The Audience You Cannot See

Take friends who lean against partners, the case Section 6 worked through. The tip then has three faces.

When your partner is wary, the friends' word is a certificate. What counts is earning a yes, and a yes keeps its force even when some of the friends would say no to anyone, though yeses become rarer, so the prize still shrinks.

When your partner is already leaning in, the friends' word is a veto. What counts is avoiding a no, and the payoff falls as the share of friends who say no to everyone rises.

Once that share passes the threshold, your partner rationally stops listening to the noes, and performing for those friends earns nothing.

If the friends lean the other way, towards approving anyone, the picture mirrors: the no keeps its force and the yes loses it, so the certificate fades first and the veto holds. That is the unknown-bias part of the tip. Behind it sits the textbook condition for a working signal, applied to friends. The friends at the table can hear you only if what you do reaches them; what you do means something only if it would cost the wrong person more; and what they make of it is worth something to your partner only to the extent that it is not your partner's own impression coming back. The cost condition is the one nobody has measured, and without it the rest does not start.

Go back to the table. The ten minutes are over, your partner's hand is on the back of your chair, and nobody has told you anything. Those ten minutes could have been a certificate, if your partner came in wary and someone there was in a position to say yes. A veto avoided, if your partner was already leaning in and nobody there was set to say no. Or nothing at all, if your partner was leaning in and enough of them would have said no to anyone. Which it was depends on two things you cannot see from your chair: how far your partner was leaning, and how many of the people around the table wanted you to pass.
