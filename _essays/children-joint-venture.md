## 1. Ten Past Three

Ten past three. The monitor makes the small sound that comes before the large one: a dry intake of breath, a rustle, the noise of someone very small assembling a complaint. You are awake. You were awake before you knew why. And you know your partner is awake, because the breathing beside you has changed the way breathing changes when someone is deciding not to move.

Nobody says anything. Nobody needs to. You could work out, to the night, how many times this month it has been you. So could they. Each of you keeps a tally neither of you would admit to keeping, the two tallies probably do not agree, and neither of you will say the number, because the number would turn a tired household into a negotiation, and you both know it. Say it, and the next question is what it is a number *of*. Hours? Favours? Something owed?

Behind that number sit larger, quieter ones. Who goes in late tomorrow. Whose meeting can move. Whose employer has already learned to expect a certain amount of absence and has started, politely, to price it. Who knows which of the three identical bottles on the draining board is the clean one, and who would have to be told. Whose evenings have quietly become the ones that bend. None of that is in the room, and all of it is in the room.

You love this child with a ferocity that still catches you off guard. So does the person lying next to you. You wanted this, both of you, on purpose, and if anyone asked you tomorrow whether it was worth it you would both say yes before they finished the question. Nobody in this bed is shirking. Nobody is the villain. That is what makes the next few seconds so strange.

The sound arrives properly now. The duvet is warm. The floor is not. Somewhere in the next four seconds one of you will get up, and it will feel like no decision at all, which is precisely the problem: this is how the thing gets decided, and it is how it was decided last night.

## 2. What This Essay Will Explain

This essay is about those four seconds. More exactly, it is about why a child whom both parents wanted changes the terms between them, even when nobody behaves badly and the two of them agree on nearly everything.

The usual answer, that babies are exhausting, is true and explains less than it seems to. Exhaustion falls on both of you. What also changes is where each of you stands relative to the other, and exhaustion does not explain that.

Four things move at once when a wanted child arrives. What each of you has put in, and how much of it would survive if you parted. Who decides the things nobody planned for. Who else now has a claim on both of you. And what each of you could fall back on if the arrangement broke, which turns out to govern what you agree long before anyone walks.

To see these shifts, the essay borrows an idea from business: the joint venture, in which two parties build a separate undertaking together. The borrowing earns its keep on some of the four shifts and fails on others, and the failures teach as much as the fit; the essay says which is which. On the way it reaches an uncomfortable result about the parent who cares more about how a breakdown would land on the child, and what that caring does to their position. It says exactly what that result depends on. It ends with a way to read your own arrangement, one test for each shift.

## 3. A Wanted Child and a Different Bargain

The usual answer to what a baby does to a relationship is true as far as it goes: less sleep, more work, less sex, less time. All of it explains why both of you are tired. It does not explain why one of you, a few years in, can find that your position relative to the other has quietly moved, so that you now need the arrangement more than your partner does, or can less afford to lose an argument, when neither of you set out to make it so. Nor does it explain how that can happen with no bad faith anywhere in the story: two people who both wanted the child, who would both say, truthfully, that they are doing their best.

That is the puzzle. The shift is structural. A child changes the shape of the partnership, and the shape then does things to the people inside it that neither of them chose.

One limit, stated once, before going further. Children are persons, not assets. Nothing below treats a child as an investment, an output, a project or a bargaining chip. The frame this essay borrows applies only to what passes between the parents: their coordination, their contributions and their obligations to each other and to the child. The child enters the analysis in exactly two ways. First, as someone whose welfare both parents care about, often more than they care about their own. Second, as a person whose interests are not the parents' to trade away. Where the law comes into it, the point is structural; the specifics vary from place to place, and nothing here is legal advice.

Three lines of scope. The essay concerns two parents who are, or were, a couple. Single parents, step-families, co-parents who were never together and grandparents raising children face versions of these problems, but the model is not built for them. And where one parent is abusive or controlling, the situation is not a bargain between two people with choices, and the model is silent.

Last, roles rather than sexes. In many couples, at the margin, one parent ends up putting more into the child and less into their own earning power outside the home. Call that person the specialising parent, and the other one the other parent. Either parent can fill either role, and the argument runs on roles throughout. Who tends to fill which, on average, is an empirical question with a fairly strong answer, which Section 9 gives plainly.

## 4. What a Joint Venture Is, and What We Borrow

Here is the borrowed idea in ordinary words. Two parties who remain separate set up an undertaking together. Each brings something the other could not easily supply. They share control of it, because neither can run it alone. They cannot write down in advance everything that will happen to it, because nobody knows. And sooner or later they must settle the question most partnerships would rather not ask aloud: what happens to the undertaking if they part.

Business calls this a joint venture. The literature on it concerns firms, and the companies that set up a joint venture are called its parent firms. That is the last this essay will make of the pun.

The earlier essays did the groundwork on bargaining and governance, and this one picks up from there rather than repeating it. The second essay argued that every couple has governance, including couples whose only rule is "we'll work it out", and that when nothing is decided the job tends to fall to whoever can least let it sit.[^1] The third argued that marriage creates deep joint exposure without creating anyone with final say, and asked which parts of a shared life should be shared, coordinated or left to each partner alone.[^2] The idea that the split of a partnership's gains depends on what each partner could fall back on outside comes from this publication's essays on making it official and on exit and voice.[^3],[^4]

What a child adds is three things. The investments become specific and unequal beyond anything marriage alone requires. A third person now constrains the governance, and, unlike money or weekends or whose friends come to dinner, the child cannot be ring-fenced as one partner's autonomous domain, because every decision about a child is made on behalf of someone who did not make it. And exit no longer ends the undertaking. It changes it.

From the joint-venture literature the essay borrows questions, not conclusions: who controls, what happens in the case nobody foresaw, who can leave and on what terms. One idea it borrows outright. Andrew Inkpen and Paul Beamish define the instability of an international joint venture not as its ending but as a major, unplanned and premature change in the partners' relationship, one that in their account follows a partner learning enough to shift the balance.[^5] Hold on to that definition. It is the sense of "never fully exit" that Section 8 needs — though the essay borrows only the structural point that instability need not mean termination, and applies it to a different kind of bargaining-power shift.

What the JV vocabulary earns its keep through is three specific questions that are harder to ask without it: who holds residual control over the unforeseen case, what determines the threat points, and what instability means when it does not mean the end. Those questions can be asked of any serious joint obligation; the JV literature has thought harder about them than most.

## 5. Shift One: Equal Commitment, Unequal Exposure

Start with what each parent puts in. The items are not the same kind of thing: pregnancy and birth, where they apply; the nights; the planning, which is invisible exactly when it works; the income; and, for many, an interruption to a career that was going somewhere. It is tempting to put these on one scale and settle who is doing more. Resist. The question that matters is not how big each contribution is but whether it travels.

Some contributions keep their value whatever happens to the couple. A salary is a salary; it buys the same groceries in one household or two. Others lose most of their value the moment the arrangement changes. Seniority given up. Skills left to rust during years of part-time work. A city chosen because it suited the other parent's job. A deep, specific knowledge of how this household runs, from which paediatrician to call to which school-gate parent can be trusted with a sleepover, that is worth a great deal here and nothing on a CV.

When one person has sunk a great deal into things that only pay inside the arrangement, and the other has not, the second has more choices if the terms change, and the first has fewer places to go. Neither arrived there by design. The possibility alone shifts who needs whom. Economists call this hold-up, from work on firms that build a plant beside a single customer and then discover how much that customer can now demand.[^6],[^7] The idea entered this publication's account of couples in the essay on making it official, and this essay leans on that treatment rather than rebuilding it.[^3] Applying it to parenthood is the essay's own extension: the research for this piece found no family application.

Two things are new with a child. The investment cannot be fully squared by a payment: nobody can name, in advance, a lump sum for a decade of career that both parents would accept, and neither would like what the cheque implied. And a third person is involved, whose interests limit what either parent can do with any leverage the arrangement creates.

Two tests have to be kept apart here. Equal commitment can sit beside unequal exposure: both parents can be wholly devoted, and one can still have far more to lose if the arrangement changes. And efficiency is not fairness — the two tests are kept separate here. A household that specialises can be doing the sensible thing for the household while the specialising parent is exposed. The fairness test this essay proposes is not who did more last Tuesday. It is who would be left holding what if the arrangement changed.

Specialising often is the sensible thing. Claudia Goldin's account of the remaining gender gap in pay points to how jobs are built: in many occupations, pay rises more than in proportion to long or inflexible hours, so a household with one parent in the inflexible job and one in the flexible job can out-earn a household that splits both.[^8] That specialisation pays households is old ground in this publication.[^9] The child's addition is that the parent in the flexible role is making exactly the investment that only pays inside the arrangement.

The best field evidence for hold-up logic in couples, as the making-it-official essay noted, comes from divorce law. Betsey Stevenson found that when US states let one spouse end a marriage without the other's consent, newlyweds in first marriages invested less in their marriages, on measures that included household specialisation and having children.[^10] Easier exit, less specific investment: the direction hold-up predicts. The setting is the United States between 1970 and 1980; whether it holds today is unknown, and estimates in this literature are contested. How large the exposure is for any given couple, no study can say.

## 6. Shift Two: The Contract Nobody Could Write

Now the agreement itself. Couples planning a child often talk a great deal beforehand: who takes leave, whether to pay for help, which grandparents live near. All useful, and all radically incomplete. Nothing either parent could write down in advance specifies what this child will need, because nobody yet knows whether the child will sleep, or be ill, or be anxious, or be the sort who scales the bookcase at eighteen months. The arrangement is a contract with most of its clauses blank.

Somebody fills the blanks. Go back to ten past three. The rule that settles who gets up was never agreed; it is simply operating. It is a small case of what the third essay in this series called residual decision rights: who decides what nobody foresaw.[^2] In every unforeseen case someone decides, and usually it is the one who is there. The second essay showed how unowned jobs settle on whoever can least let them sit.[^1] The step this essay adds is narrower. The parent who is present gains authority over the blank clauses and, in the same motion, keeps making the non-portable investments of the last section. Being there earns the say; having the say keeps you there. The default compounds, in this account at least — a plausible mechanism, not a documented one: there is no evidence on how often "whoever is present" is the rule that actually operates.

> Being there earns the say; having the say keeps you there.
{: .pull}

The blanks come in classes (health, schooling, discipline, religion, where to live, how much risk is acceptable), and the night waking is the cheapest of them. Here is a dearer one. A doctor, a teacher, a form with a box to tick: someone asks a question about your child that the two of you have never discussed. You are in the room; your partner is not. You answer. Or your partner is in the room and you are not, and you hear about it over dinner, and you notice that "we" has been doing the job that "I" should have done. "We decided" has become "I decided, and you live with it". Neither of you has done anything wrong. The blank was filled by whoever stood nearest to it.

The economics of incomplete contracts, in Oliver Hart and John Moore's version, asks who holds the right to decide what an agreement left open, and what that allocation does to each side's willingness to invest.[^11] Their answer runs through the ownership of assets, and that metaphor is dropped here without regret: nobody owns a child. The question survives. Who fills the blank clauses, and what does filling them do to what each of you goes on putting in?

## 7. Shift Three: The Person Who Cannot Bargain

A business venture has third parties too: customers, regulators, lenders. They matter because they have power. A customer can walk away, a regulator can fine, a lender can call in the loan. The third party in this venture has none of that. A toddler cannot negotiate, and an eight-year-old can only lobby. Where a business's third parties protect themselves by bargaining, a child is protected, if at all, by constraint: by the parents' own care and, in law, by some version of the principle that decisions about a child should serve the child's interests. How that principle is applied varies by jurisdiction, and this essay makes no claim about any particular one.

That puts the child's welfare in two places at once, and they must be kept apart. Inside each parent, it is something they care about, part of what each of them wants. Outside both of them, it is a floor: a limit on what any arrangement between the parents, a separation included, may leave the child with. The first is a preference; the second is a rule. They behave differently, and the next section turns on the difference.

Then the analogy fails in its most useful way. The child grows up. A child's say over their own life rises, slowly and then quickly, until the child is the one making decisions about their own life. Nothing in the corporate world corresponds; no joint venture ends with a third party taking over its own governance because they have turned eighteen. The governance between two parents is therefore temporary in a way they almost never plan for. Each year, some of what they decided together stops being theirs to decide.

The consequence for what counts as a good deal is plain. Between two firms, a good agreement is one both sides will sign. Between two parents, agreement is necessary and not enough: the standard for what counts as a good deal between them cannot therefore be agreement alone — the child's interests set a floor on what any arrangement between them may leave. And success is measured neither by whether the parents stay together nor by how much the arrangement gives the two of them. A couple who part carefully can still be running this undertaking well. A couple who stay can be running it badly.

## 8. Shift Four: Exit That Does Not End the Venture

The fourth shift is the title's, and it sets the terms on which the other three are bargained.

Two people splitting the gains from a joint undertaking each compare the agreement with their outside option, what they would get without it. The better your outside option, the better the terms you can hold out for; the worse it is, the more you will accept rather than risk the break. That logic comes from the making-it-official and exit-and-voice essays.[^3],[^4]

What changes is what "without it" means. For a childless couple, walking away means, roughly, being out. For parents it means the post-separation arrangement: where the child lives, who pays, who decides, under whatever defaults the law supplies and however well they are enforced. Robert Mnookin and Lewis Kornhauser called this bargaining in the shadow of the law: the law shapes what separating couples agree without dictating it.[^12] This essay moves the point one step back. The shadow falls before anyone separates. The terms on which parents run their household are struck, at the margin, against what each would face if they stopped.

That position outside the arrangement depends on legal defaults, enforcement and earning power, and the last leads back to Section 5: the specialising parent's investments do not travel, the other parent's do, so the specialising parent's outside option is weaker and the terms inside quietly reflect it.

Now the uncomfortable result. This is two separate things. First, the specialising parent's career investments do not travel, so they have less to fall back on regardless of who cares more — this is the investment channel. Second, suppose one parent is more hurt than the other by what a failed agreement would do to the child: a less settled home, thinner contact, less money. In a model where what one parent gains can be shifted to the other — a strong simplification — that parent has less to threaten with. The parent who cares more about what failure would cost the child takes less.

The derivation requires that material payoffs can be shifted between parents — a strong assumption that is named in the box below.

> **What the caring result depends on.** Three assumptions: the parents bargain over material terms that can be shifted between them; each compares any deal with a fixed outside position, the post-separation arrangement; and separating leaves the child worse off than agreeing would. Given those, a parent's material share falls for two separate reasons: their earning power outside is weaker, and they care more than the other parent about the child, in proportion to how much worse separation would be for the child. The second reason disappears if both care equally, or if separation costs the child nothing, for instance because a strong legal floor and reliable enforcement protect the child whatever the parents do. The first does not. Formally this is Nash bargaining with transferable utility; under alternating-offer bargaining — a different model, where the parties take turns making offers — a threat that is not credible may not bind, and the caring channel may not appear at all.

That protection depends on the floor being real and enforced; where enforcement is weak, the caring channel operates fully regardless of what the law nominally provides.

The structure is not new to this publication; the place is. The exit-and-voice essay found that in a relationship that is not working, the most loyal partner is often the least powerful, because loyalty makes leaving expensive and drains the force behind complaint.[^4] The second essay in this series showed how caring changes who moves first.[^1] This is the child-specific instance of that structure, on the exit side. It is derived: it follows from stated assumptions in the theory work behind this essay, and no source found states or tests it.

The result is not advice to care less, and it is not a story of bad faith: the other parent need not exploit anything, because the terms settle where the outside positions put them. What it does say is that legal defaults and enforcement decide whether caring is penalised in the arrangement. They protect the caring parent's position, not only the child.

So in what sense can you never fully exit? "Never fully exit" means the legal obligation and the child's developmental need are not extinguished by any act of the parent, including separation — it is a structural fact about the arrangement, not a moral instruction. In practice, parents do leave, pay less than they owe, see the child less; some vanish. What a parent cannot resign from is the legal obligation, the moral one, and the child's need. Of US custodial parents due child support in 2017, figures that are unverified pending direct access to the source suggest about 46 percent received the full amount, and about 62 percent of the total due was received, by the custodial parents' own report.[^13] The obligation outlives the separation more reliably than the payment does. That does not show parents bargain badly (separations that end in support orders are not a random sample), but it bears on outside positions: a parent who can cut payment or contact has a better one than the legal default suggests, and so a stronger hand inside the relationship. This is Inkpen and Beamish's instability at household scale: not an ending, but an unplanned and premature change in the partners' relationship after a shift in bargaining power.

Investment cuts two ways: it can spend one partner's leverage while raising the pair's reasons to stay together. The exit-and-voice essay reached the first half as a conditional result;[^4] which effect dominates, for which parent, is left open here. The closest existing economics is Yoram Weiss and Robert Willis's paper on children as collective goods and divorce settlements, which, in a secondary-source reading, treats the child's welfare as something both parents value after the marriage ends.[^14] It may anticipate parts of this account; nothing here is offered as new against it.

Policy moves these positions; this is the essay's one policy point. Whatever restores the specialising parent's earning power raises their outside option and shifts the arrangement inside. In Norwegian data, Martin Eckhoff Andresen and Emily Nix found that a childcare reform cut the child penalty by about a quarter, while paternity leave made little measurable difference.[^15]

## 9. What the Child Penalty Shows, and What It Does Not

Section 5 claimed that the cost of specialising falls unevenly and lasts. Here is the evidence, in one place, with its limits.

The cleanest picture comes from Denmark. Henrik Kleven, Camille Landais and Jakob Egholt Søgaard followed parents' earnings in Danish data around the birth of a first child.[^17] Before the birth, mothers' and fathers' earnings track each other. After it, they diverge, and they do not converge again: in the long run, the earnings gap caused by children is about 20 percent, built from hours, participation in work and wage rates, with choices of occupation, sector and firm implicated. The regularity is gendered and should be stated plainly. On average, in that country and over that period, it is mothers' earnings that fall. That is a statement about averages in one dataset, not about any particular couple, and how to interpret it is contested.

A companion study across six countries (Denmark, Sweden, Germany, Austria, the United Kingdom and the United States) found the same shape everywhere and sharply different sizes.[^18] The cross-country measures are not the same as the Danish headline, and this essay does not set one against the other.

Why it happens is harder. Biology is the obvious suspect. Andresen and Nix tested it in Norwegian data by comparing heterosexual couples who had a child by birth with couples who adopted, and with same-sex couples.[^15] Their results largely rule out giving birth, and the father's advantage in the labour market, as the main explanations. What remains (preferences, gender norms, discrimination) the work cannot fully separate. Be precise about what this shows. It speaks against a story in which the penalty is mainly biology, or mainly a matter of who earned more beforehand. It does not say whether the penalty attaches to whichever parent takes the main caring role; that question is not what the paper's design answers. That is why this essay's model treats roles as an assumption rather than as a finding. Goldin's job structure is another channel, compatible with several of these.[^8] No head-to-head test of norms against workplaces was found.

The deepest question an event study cannot answer is whether the divergence was chosen or imposed. A couple who want one parent at home and a couple who feel they have no alternative produce the same line on the graph. What the evidence actually shows, stripped of what both sides want it to say, is narrower than either would like: in the typical case the cost is not shared evenly, and it persists.

| Evidence | Setting | What it shows | What it cannot show |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Kleven, Landais, Søgaard | Denmark | Divergence after first birth; about 20% long-run gap | Whether chosen or constrained |
| Kleven et al., six countries | Six rich countries | Same shape, very different sizes | A single comparable magnitude for this essay |
| Andresen, Nix | Norway | Not mainly birth or prior earnings advantage | Norms versus preferences versus discrimination |
| Stevenson | US, 1970-1980 | Easier exit, less specific investment | Whether it holds today |
| Goldin | US labour market | Pay structure makes specialising rational | Whether norms or workplaces matter more |

Against the rival explanations, the evidence sorts like this. The adoption and same-sex comparisons bear on biology and simple comparative advantage; divorce-law variation bears on hold-up; occupational comparisons bear on job structure. Preferences against constraints, and norms against the bargaining positions of Section 8, stay out of reach: these designs cannot separate them, and a model can only say what each would predict. The evidence base also has edges: it is concentrated in a few rich countries' records and says much less about same-sex couples, unmarried parents and families elsewhere.

## 10. Where the Model Fails, and the Serious Objection

Here is the objection at full strength — and it is a stronger one than the usual commodification complaint.

A skeptical reader can put it this way: *the joint-venture frame adds nothing over the simpler claim that a child creates an asymmetric, irrevocable commitment. Everything this essay says about specific investment, incomplete contracts and asymmetric exit costs is available without the JV vocabulary. "Residual control rights" is just "who decides things we haven't agreed about." "Instability without termination" is just "the arrangement changes without ending." "Threat-point analysis" is just "each of you has a fallback." The vocabulary adds a layer of business jargon without adding a prediction or a mechanism.*

This objection is worth meeting directly. The JV vocabulary earns its keep because it carries with it a literature that has thought hard about three specific questions: who holds residual control over the unforeseen case, what determines each party's fallback position, and what "instability" means when it does not mean the end. That last question is the important one. Without Inkpen and Beamish's distinction, "never fully exit" is just a vague observation; with it, it becomes a specific claim about unplanned change in the partners' relationship following a shift in bargaining power. The difference is not decoration. The JV frame is not the only way to ask these questions; it is a frame that has already developed the instruments. And those three instruments — residual control rights, instability-without-termination, and threat-point analysis — are what the essay uses throughout. The vocabulary is the value, not the metaphor.

The softer version of the objection should also be conceded. A child is not a venture, and calling parenthood one commodifies the most important relationship most people will ever have. Most parents do not bargain. They love, they improvise, they absorb. The parent who does the nights because the other is wrecked, the parent who goes part-time because they want to, the couple who never once think about what each could fall back on: none of them is splitting the difference between outside positions. And the most striking result in the essay, that caring more costs a parent bargaining power, rests on one bargaining story. Change the story, let the parents take turns making offers, and a threat that would never be carried out does not bind. The result may simply disappear.

Much of this should be conceded. The model does not describe conversations, and nobody in it is calculating. The caring result depends on a particular, strong model of bargaining and has never been tested on real couples; it is derived, and it stays derived. The model also captures the channel through which caring about the child's post-separation welfare weakens a parent's position. It does not model the altruism most parents feel most directly — caring about the partner and the household — which is a different force and outside the model.

But the objection proves less than it seems to. The frame never covered the child; it covers what the parents owe each other and the child, which is a fit subject for analysis (Section 3). The claim about love and adaptation is not a rival to the model; it is what happens inside the structure the model describes. Two people can adapt generously for years, and the adaptation can still leave one of them with skills that do not travel and a fallback that has quietly shrunk. That was the point of Sections 5 and 8: good faith and exposure are compatible. Recognising that the structure did it is not the same as saying nothing can be done about it; it means asking the right questions about the structure rather than looking for the villain. And the investment channel does not need the bargaining story the objection attacks. Hold-up needs only that one parent has sunk more into what pays inside the arrangement. It is a conditional claim with named limits. The caring channel is the fragile one, and the essay has said so where it appeared.

The limits are worth stating. If earnings and careers stay symmetric after the child, or help is bought in, there is no asymmetry. If a credible settle-up exists, so that the specialising parent is made whole by explicit agreement or by law, hold-up is not a risk. If the specialising parent wants the role and does not feel its costs as costs, the asymmetry exists on paper and not in their life; this preference objection is partly conceded, since the evidence cannot separate choice from constraint. If disagreement is rare, the blank clauses never need filling. If the law is symmetric and enforced, outside positions converge. If both parents care equally, the caring channel vanishes. These are not clean thresholds. Each condition weakens the channel as it is more fully met; the channel fades rather than vanishes. If the pair never separate, the shadow still shapes the arrangement in the model, though nobody has measured it doing so — but the model predicts that the agreement inside the relationship is struck against it, whether or not the threshold is ever crossed. Where one parent coerces or abuses the other, the situation is not a bargain between two people with choices, and the model is silent.

One cost deserves conceding plainly. Asking "what would happen if we split?" can feel like preparing to split, and preparation can corrode the trust it was meant to protect. That cost is real. It is also the cost every serious joint obligation pays: people insure houses they mean to live in for ever, and write wills without wishing anyone dead. Designing an arrangement that would survive the relationship is not a forecast that the relationship will fail. It is a way of making sure that, if it does, the person who has sunk the most, and the person who never had a vote, are not the ones who pay for it.

The model never claimed to capture everything. Its claim is that it improves the question. The better question is not "who loves more" or "who works harder". It is who is exposed, who decides, and what each of you could fall back on.

## 11. Reading Your Own Arrangement

What follows is not advice about what any couple owes. It is a set of tests, each tied to a shift, and which of them matters depends on which shift binds for you.

**What has each of us put in that would not travel?** Start with the first shift. List, honestly, what each of you has put into this household that would be worth much less if you separated: career ground given up, a pension that stopped growing, a city chosen for the other's job, skills left idle. Then ask whether any of it has been dealt with: priced, saved against, or given a date on which the two of you look at it again. The second essay in this series supplied the review trigger as a remedy for unowned work;[^1] here a review date is one of three ways to settle up for an investment that only pays inside the arrangement. Settle-up is a device, not a rule, and the essay on commitment devices covers the choice of devices better than this one could.[^19] What the test catches is the investment that has been left to goodwill, which is to say left to land on whoever made it.

**Who fills the blanks, and do we mean it?** For each class of decision (health, schooling, discipline, religion, where to live, how much risk), who decides, who breaks a tie, and what happens when one decides and the other bears the cost? Then the uncomfortable follow-up: is "whoever is present" your actual rule? If it is, decide whether you mean it. It may be a perfectly good rule. It is a worse one if you did not know you had it, because then the parent who is present gathers authority and exposure together without either of you having agreed to the trade.

**What binds us if we separate, and is it already shaping today?** If you separated tomorrow, which of your agreements would still bind, who would hold what, and does the answer already shape what you agree now? There is a specific test for the caring channel, and it is the way to separate it from the investment channel. Look at how disputes resolve. On money disputes, the investment channel applies: the parent who has less to fall back on concedes more regardless of who cares more. On child-related disputes, the caring channel adds its weight on top. If the parent who worries more about the child concedes more on child-related disputes than on money disputes, that pattern points to the caring channel at work: the investment asymmetry would predict money-dispute concessions, and the extra weight on child disputes is the caring channel showing itself. No pattern does not rule it out; it only means you have not yet seen it.

**When the child's interests and ours diverge, which wins, and how will that change?** The fourth test runs the other way in time. Ask what you do when what suits the two of you does not suit the child, and who decides that it does not. Then ask how the arrangement will move as the child's say grows: a date, a trigger, some route by which the child's own voice comes to count. An arrangement that fits a toddler will not fit a twelve-year-old, and the parents who plan for that can hand over decisions instead of losing them in a row.

None of these yields a verdict. Each tells you which mechanism from this essay is live in your household, which is the most a model can honestly offer a couple it has never met.

---

## 12. The Venture That Outlives the Alliance

Back to ten past three. The monitor is still making its sound, and one of you is about to move. You now know what those four seconds hold. A specific investment one of you has been making, night by night and promotion by promotion, that would be worth much less if the two of you came apart. A default nobody chose, filling a blank nobody wrote, compounding a little with every night it operates. A third person whose needs do not wait for the arrangement and are not yours to trade. And an exit that would end the romance without ending any of the rest.

This series has asked how two separate people get from uncertain exploration to permanent joint obligation without pretending they have become one person, and without letting the costs of their shared life fall on one of them. The first essay asked whether something viable could be discovered at all.[^20] The second asked whether two autonomous people could coordinate to build it. The third asked how much integration and shared exposure to accept. This one asks what happens to whoever has sunk the most, once what they built has a third person in it.

The answer the essay offers is its own synthesis, and a hypothesis rather than a result. At this stage the two fictions become most tempting and most costly. The pair cannot become one actor, because a third person's interests bind both of them and belong to neither. And the costs concentrate, because the investments are specific, unequal and hard to compensate, and the outside positions drift apart. The way past both fictions is institutional: a parenting arrangement designed to outlive the relationship that made it, with the cost of specific investment settled up rather than left to goodwill.

That is not a rule, and it is not a prediction that anyone leaves. It is a description of what would have to be true for the four seconds to be a choice.

The floor is still cold. One of you is about to get up, and the other is about to let them. The question is not who. It is whether the arrangement you are both lying in is one you chose, or only one you have not yet left.
