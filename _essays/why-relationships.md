## 1. The Renewal Email

It's Sunday evening. The email from your letting agent has been sitting in your inbox since Friday. You know what it says without opening it: another twelve months, a modest increase, sign here. You can afford it. That's the strange part. The flat is fine, the job is fine, and there are three people you could call tonight who would pick up.

Last week, over a second drink, the person you've been seeing for six months pointed out that your two rents together would get a place with a garden. You both laughed, and neither of you said anything else. You've been thinking about it ever since.

You're not lonely, exactly. Loneliness is a deficit, and nothing here is in deficit. What you feel is closer to standing at a fork. Down one path, the next three years are a series of decisions made by you, for you, about you. Down the other they aren't. The cursor is over *renew*, and you haven't clicked.

Notice what's odd about this moment. Nearly every material reason that once made partnership close to compulsory has weakened. You don't need a husband to own property or a wife to get fed. And yet the question on your screen weighs more than a rent decision should. This article explains why, or more precisely it explains the part that can be explained.

Here is the plan. I am going to treat a relationship as what an economist would call a joint production unit: a small firm that makes things neither partner could make alone. First, I'll walk through its three production lines. The first is dividing labour, the second is insuring each other against bad luck, and the third is splitting costs that don't grow when a second person moves in. Second, I'll show how the historical terms of the deal were set less by what the firm produced than by who could afford to walk away, and why the long decline in marriage is roughly what that logic predicts. Third, I'll take the strongest objection head-on: you can now buy almost everything on that list. Then I'll turn the model into decisions you can actually make. The piece ends where the economics runs out, which is roughly where your cursor is hovering.

The backdrop is not in dispute. Marriage rates across most of the OECD have fallen from their mid-twentieth-century highs.[^1] Stated desire for a long-term partner remains high by comparison.[^2] Formal uptake fell, and the pull did not fall with it. Keep that gap in mind, because the whole article is an attempt to measure it.

## 2. Two People, One Production Unit

Start with what it costs to run a life alone. You earn the money. You run the household. You manage your own health, and you handle the paperwork of modern adulthood: renewals, tax, the boiler, the GP. You also do all the emotional processing of whatever happened to you this week. One person, one supply of hours, one pool of attention. There is no slack in the system, and nobody to cover you when it breaks.

Now add a second person who has different skills, a different schedule and a different exposure to bad luck. Under the right conditions, the two of you together produce more than double what either produces alone. That extra output is the surplus, and the surplus is the economic reason relationships exist.

It comes from three places:

- **Specialisation.** Two people who divide tasks according to what each gives up least by doing them produce more than two people who each do everything.
- **Insurance.** Two people whose bad luck rarely arrives at the same moment can carry each other through it.
- **Scale.** Rent, heating and the cognitive overhead of a household don't double when a second person moves in.

Family economists have catalogued these gains for decades. Gary Becker built the theory of the household as a site of production, and Martin Browning's taxonomy of the gains from marriage lists scale economies, comparative advantage and informal insurance as separate sources.[^3][^5] What this article adds is interpretation rather than literature. It claims that these gains are best read as three production lines of a single firm that add to one another rather than compete, and that most of the modern history of marriage can be read off what happened to each line. No single study makes that integrated claim. Each component has its own evidence base, and the integration is mine.

Two boundaries apply. First, like any firm, this one has overhead. Negotiating, coordinating, fighting about the dishwasher and risking a bad match all eat into the surplus. The model describes what a partnership produces when it works, not a promise that every partnership does. Second, the logic doesn't care about gender or about whether the state has stamped the arrangement. It applies to any two people sharing a life. Most of the empirical evidence comes from heterosexual marriage because that is what the data mostly track.

## 3. You Don't Have to Be Better at Anything. You Have to Be Cheaper at Something.

Take two people. Alex is better than Sam at almost everything that matters to running a shared life: Alex earns more, cooks faster, deals with landlords better and loses their temper less often. On a naive reading Sam is dead weight, and Alex should do it all and simply put up with Sam's company.

That reading is wrong, and the reason it's wrong is the most useful idea in household economics. Alex's time is not free. Every hour Alex spends sorting the bills is an hour Alex doesn't spend on work that pays much better. The relevant question is not who is *better* at a task. It's who gives up *less* by doing it.

> **Box: The arithmetic of comparative advantage (illustrative numbers)**
>
> Alex earns £40 an hour and can clear the week's household admin in 2 hours. Sam earns £15 an hour and needs 3 hours for the same admin.
>
> - Cost of Alex doing the admin: 2 hours × £40 = **£80** of forgone earnings
> - Cost of Sam doing the admin: 3 hours × £15 = **£45** of forgone earnings
>
> Sam is slower at the admin *and* earns less. Sam should still do the admin, because the household loses £35 less when Sam does it. Alex has the *absolute* advantage in both tasks. Sam has the *comparative* advantage in admin. Specialise, trade, and the household ends up richer than if each did their own share of everything.

This is David Ricardo's logic of trade between nations, which Becker carried into the household.[^3][^4] The gains don't require one partner to be brilliant and the other useless. They require only that the two of you differ in what your time is worth across tasks. That difference might be pay, flexibility, temperament, tolerance for phone calls with the council, or whose job can absorb a dentist appointment at 11 a.m. on a Tuesday. Nearly every pair differs somewhere. The exception is two people whose skills, schedules and capacities overlap almost completely. For them the specialisation gain shrinks toward zero, and the partnership has to earn its keep through the other two lines.

The traditional division of labour, in which men earned and women ran the home, was one historical *instance* of this mechanism. It was not the mechanism itself. When women were legally barred from many occupations, denied control of property and paid less for the same work, their market time was artificially cheap.[^6] The comparative advantage that pointed them toward the home was produced by law and custom, not discovered by the market. As those barriers fell and women's earnings rose, the gendered version of the gain shrank, which is exactly what the model predicts when two people's opportunity costs converge. The mechanism survived. It just stopped pointing the same way in every house.

## 4. You Are One Bad Month Away From Needing Someone

The specialisation gain depends on difference. The insurance gain doesn't. It would exist between two identical people with identical jobs, because it rests on a single fact: bad luck rarely arrives for both of you on the same day.

Picture losing your job while you live alone. The income stops on a fixed date. Savings start draining, spending gets cut, and every month without an offer pushes you toward taking the wrong one. Now picture the same redundancy with a partner whose salary keeps arriving. Household spending dips but doesn't collapse. You can afford to hold out for the right job. The shock still happens, but it lands on a household rather than on you alone.

This is informal mutual insurance, the oldest form of risk pooling there is. Economists have studied it in villages, where households with imperfectly correlated harvests smooth each other's consumption without any written policy.[^7] A couple is the smallest possible version of that village.

> **Box: Why two people are less risky than one**
>
> Suppose each partner's income swings around its average with the same variance, σ². Let ρ be the correlation between their shocks: 1 if they always rise and fall together, 0 if they move independently.
>
> If the couple pools income and each consumes half, the variance each person faces is:
>
> **σ² × (1 + ρ) / 2**
>
> If the shocks are independent (ρ = 0), each person's risk is halved. If they are perfectly correlated (ρ = 1), the pool does nothing. The partnership doesn't raise anyone's average income. It narrows the range of bad outcomes.

If partnership really works as insurance, it should make a testable prediction. When the world gets riskier, more people should want into the pool.

France seems to have provided something close to a test. Around 1999, a change to the rules on layoffs left part of the workforce with less job protection than before. The workers affected did not choose the reform, so it works as a natural experiment. What did they do next? According to a 2020 working paper using the reform, they became more likely to marry, and the rise was concentrated in marriages to partners whose own jobs were *more* secure.[^8] People facing new risk appear to have sought out a partner whose income could carry them. That is the insurance motive, observed in the wild. The citation still needs external verification before publication, and the article treats the finding as evidence *consistent with* the insurance prediction rather than as settled fact.

One distinction matters here. The French study measured *marriage*, the legal contract, and not merely the decision to share a life. Marriage turns an informal pool into an enforceable one: shared assets, obligations on separation, a public commitment. The insurance logic applies to cohabiting couples too, but people facing sharper risk may want the version with a contract attached. That is not romance. It is the reason insurers don't sell policies on a handshake.

> The best insurance partner is not the one most like you.
{: .pull}

The scope condition follows straight from the box. When shocks are correlated, the pool fails at exactly the moment you need it. Two people at the same company, in the same sector and the same city during a downturn will be hit together. The best insurance partner is not the one most like you. A freelancer paired with a salaried employee, or a private-sector worker paired with someone in the public sector, holds a more valuable policy than two consultants at the same firm.

## 5. The Least Romantic Gain Starts on Day One

The third line is the dullest, which is probably why people underrate it. It begins paying out the first night two people share a front door.

Some costs scale with the number of people: food, clothes, train fares. Others barely move. These include rent on a one-bed versus a slightly larger flat, the heating, the broadband, the fridge, the council-tax admin and the mental load of remembering when the boiler service is due. Split those fixed costs across two people and each person's cost of a decent life falls. Cohabiting partners save a meaningful share of what two separate households would cost. The precise size of that saving varies widely by city, housing type and local rents, and a properly sourced figure is still outstanding.[^9]

The garden flat in the opening email is this line in its purest form. Two rents buy more home than either rent alone.

This is part of why partnered people, on average, end up with substantially more wealth than single people.[^10] The size of that gap is hard to interpret. People who are wealthier and more stable are also more likely to form partnerships and keep them, so the association can't be cleanly split into cause and selection. Strip out as much selection as you like, though, and the scale saving remains, because it's arithmetic. A fixed cost shared by two costs each person less than the same fixed cost borne alone. Saved money can be invested and compounds, which means the couple who wait years for perfect certainty before moving in are, in strict economic terms, giving up a stream of returns.

Two caveats will matter later. Scale applies equally to cohabitation and marriage, since the landlord doesn't check your certificate. And it's the line most easily bought elsewhere. A flatmate captures most of it, and so does a lodger.

## 6. The Terms Used to Be Non-Negotiable

So far this has been about how big the surplus is. History turned on a different question: who gets which share of it.

How a joint gain is split depends on what each side could credibly do if they walked away. Bargaining theory formalises this. A party whose fallback is miserable will accept a poor share, because any share beats the fallback. A party with a good fallback can hold out for more.[^11]

> **Box: The split depends on the walk-away point**
>
> Let the partnership produce total value S. Let each partner's outside option, their life if they leave or never join, be d₁ and d₂. Under the simple symmetric Nash bargaining solution, each partner gets their outside option plus half of what the partnership adds on top:
>
> **Partner 1 receives d₁ + (S − d₁ − d₂) / 2**
>
> Lower d₂ and Partner 2's share falls even if S doesn't change at all. A worse walk-away point means worse terms. The firm can be just as productive while one partner is just as squeezed.

Now apply that to women's walk-away point across most Western legal systems before the twentieth century. Under the common-law doctrine of coverture, a married woman's legal identity was largely absorbed into her husband's. In England her property passed into his control until reforms in the late nineteenth century.[^6] Many occupations were closed to her, her wages were lower when she could work at all, and a woman outside marriage faced exclusion from respectability, property and income alike. Her outside option was not a weaker version of his. It was close to a cliff edge.

That did not make marriage a fraud. The specialisation, insurance and scale gains were genuine, and households did produce them. But the *apparent* gain from marriage, for women, was massively inflated. The difference between being inside and being outside looked enormous, and the reason was not that the firm was so productive. Solitude had been made punishing by design. The production was real and the distribution was coerced.

The twentieth century took that cliff edge apart in stages. Legal reform established property rights, employment protections and independent legal standing. Reliable contraception gave women control over the timing of births, and with it the timing of careers and marriage.[^12] Women's labour-force participation climbed. Each change raised d₂, and the bargaining frame makes a clear *directional* prediction about what follows: either the terms improve for the party whose fallback improved, or fewer people sign up, or both. The historical record moves in that direction. US marriage rates peaked in the mid-twentieth century and declined over the decades that followed.[^13] The frame does not prove it was the only cause. Secularisation, urbanisation and shifting norms all line up with the same trend, and a single pattern can't tell them apart. The bargaining story is a strong candidate, not the sole one.

The decline was not even across groups. Marriage has held up far better among more educated, higher-earning people than among everyone else. One Brookings analysis finds marriage rates for the top tenth of female earners holding steady or rising over four decades while the bottom 70 per cent saw declines of more than 15 percentage points.[^14] Here is one reading consistent with the model, and it is interpretation rather than a finding: when the specialisation line weakens for everyone, the couples who keep marrying are those with the most left to pool, meaning bigger incomes to insure and more wealth to accumulate.

The strain shows in a further pattern. Couples in which the wife out-earned the husband were historically more likely to divorce, although at least one study finds that association weakening in more recent marriages.[^15] It can be read two ways. One reading is economic: the arrangement was built around an income gap that has since reversed. The other is about identity: social norms about who should earn more are costly to violate. Both readings are consistent with the evidence, and the fading of the effect suggests the norms are catching up with the economics.

## 7. The Strongest Objection: You Can Buy All of This Now

Here is the hostile reader's case, and it's a good one.

Everything the firm produces can now be bought separately. A cleaner, a meal-kit subscription and an accountant cover most of the specialisation gain. The welfare state, unemployment benefits and a decent emergency fund cover much of the insurance. A flatmate covers the scale. A therapist covers the Tuesday-night debrief. On this view the economic case for partnership isn't merely weaker than it was in 1955. It has been unbundled, and every piece is on sale.

Most of that should be conceded. Substitutes for each line have improved, and the model itself predicts what follows: as outside options get better for everyone and not only for women, fewer people should sign up for the full bundle, and they haven't. The unbundled life is real, viable and increasingly common.

The concession has limits, for two reasons.

First, substitutes are sold transaction by transaction, and a partner is not. The cleaner is paid per hour and has no stake in whether your life goes well. A partner is a co-owner of the household's output, so they notice the symptom you're ignoring and cover the logistics when you're ill without sending an invoice. They watch the household's finances because their own outcome depends on them. The insurer can't see your life well enough to cover most of what goes wrong in it. Your partner can, and has every reason to. Some parts of the bundle are cheaper to make in-house precisely because the person making them shares the result.

Second, and more important, the objection proves too much. If the productive case has weakened this far, the desire for partnership should have weakened about as far, and it hasn't. Most adults who want a long-term partner still want one, and those who actively prefer to stay single remain a minority.[^2][^16] Substitutes make the economic case weaker, and the persistent pull more puzzling as a result.

Partnered people do appear to be healthier and to live longer, and that is consistent with the in-house argument. Social ties in general predict lower mortality.[^17] A plausible story fits the production model: someone enforces bedtime, notices the mole, books the appointment. Whether that mechanism explains the premium is a harder question. Healthier, steadier people are also more likely to pair up and stay paired. At least one genetically informed study finds evidence of both selection and a genuine effect of marriage, without settling the proportions.[^17]

The model also makes a negative prediction, and it holds. A partnership in bad shape is not a neutral outcome. Marital quality tracks health, and a bad marriage can leave people worse off than no marriage at all.[^18] In the model's terms, a firm whose partners' interests have come apart, or whose coordination costs have swallowed its output, produces less than zero. A miserable marriage is a failing firm, not a weak one.

Which leaves the softer objection: nobody calculates comparative advantage when they fall in love. True, and beside the point. The model makes no claim about what goes on in your head. It predicts what happens in aggregate: which pairs form, which dissolve, and how rates move when outside options change. Feelings may well be how the calculation gets done without anyone doing it.

So the model doesn't explain everything. It does improve the question. "Why do I want love?" is unanswerable. "What do I want that I can no longer simply buy?" can be worked on.

## 8. Running the Firm You're Actually In

The model is useful only if it changes what you do, and it does. Every prescription below follows from one of the mechanisms above.

### Reading the deal you're offered

**Name the production lines, out loud.** For any relationship you're in or considering, ask which of the three lines it actually runs. Do you divide labour in a way that saves you both time? Would this person really carry you through a bad year, and could they? Are you splitting costs? If the honest answer is "only the third", you have a flatshare with feelings. That may be fine, but price it as a flatshare.

**Screen for uncorrelated risk, not matching CVs.** The insurance line pays out only if your bad luck and theirs arrive at different times. Assortative matching pulls the other way: people tend to pair with people who look like them on paper, and that often means the same sector, the same city and the same exposure to the same downturn. Two people who are both on short-term tech contracts haven't pooled their risk. They've doubled it. A partner whose working life looks nothing like yours is worth more as insurance than one who could finish your sentences about quarterly targets.

**Check the walk-away point, both of them.** The bargaining box applies today, not only in the nineteenth century. If one of you could leave easily and the other couldn't, the terms will drift toward the one who could, whether or not either of you means them to. The cliff edge is gone from the law. It can still be rebuilt inside a house.

### Building the firm

**Specialise, but price the exit.** Comparative advantage tells you to divide the work. Bargaining tells you what dividing it costs. The partner who specialises in the unpaid, non-transferable work (the house, the children, the logistics, the other person's career) is quietly lowering their own outside option every year that arrangement runs. That is how the historical trap rebuilds itself voluntarily. If you specialise, protect the specialist's walk-away point on purpose: shared ownership of assets, a maintained ability to earn, and an explicit account of what was traded. The gain from specialising is real, and so is the leverage it hands to the other side.

**Don't move in for the rent, and don't delay for no reason.** Scale is the only line that starts paying immediately, so waiting has a real cost. It is also the line a stranger with a spare room can supply. If the garden flat is the main reason to move in together, you are taking on the overhead of a partnership in exchange for a flatmate's return. Move in for the other two lines and treat the rent saving as the bonus.

**Close a failing firm.** A bad partnership produces negative output for health and wellbeing, not merely zero. The relevant comparison is not "this, or nothing". It is "this, or a life whose fallback is better than it was for any previous generation." When the partnership's coordination costs exceed what it produces, the model's advice is blunt.

## 9. The Part the Spreadsheet Can't Price

Go back to the email.

The economics can now tell you a lot about the garden flat. It can tell you that splitting rent will cut your costs from the first month and that a stranger could give you the same saving. It can tell you that someone in a different line of work holds a better insurance policy on your life than you can buy, and that dividing up the dull jobs will free hours for both of you. It can even tell you which of you would lose bargaining power if you let the division of labour harden, and what to do about it. It can tell you why your grandmother's version of this decision was barely a decision at all, and why yours is a real one.

What it can't tell you is why the flatmate option makes your stomach drop.

On the numbers, a flatmate and a well-chosen insurance policy capture a large share of what this partnership would produce. You know that. You don't want the share. The economics explains why people want *a* partner: one who produces, pools and splits. It does not explain why the wanting points at *this* person, or why it so often persists after the productive case has gone, or why the surplus gets packaged as two people and only two. The happiness premium for being partnered is real but modest, and plenty of single people are happier than the average married couple.[^19] Partnership is not a trump card. It's a firm that works when it's well matched and well run. Even so, people keep trying to found one, at rates the production model can't fully explain.

So the model has done its job. It has stripped away the parts of your Sunday evening that were really about money and risk and fixed costs, and the history that once made those parts compulsory. Whatever remains when you subtract all of that is the real question, and it's harder than the economic one.

The cursor is still over *renew*. The economics can't tell you whether to click it. It can tell you that whatever stops you isn't the rent.
