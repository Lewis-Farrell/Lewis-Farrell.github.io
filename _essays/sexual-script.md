## 1. The Point In The Sequence

It is not a special night, and that is the point. The lamp goes off when it usually goes off, and whichever of you usually starts, starts. From there it runs the way it runs: an order you both know well enough that neither of you could say who first chose it, or whether anyone did. There is real comfort in that. Nobody has to guess; nobody has to perform; and it is good. But somewhere in the middle, at a point you could find again to within a few seconds, there is a moment where you could do one thing differently. Your hand could go somewhere else. You could slow down instead of carrying on. You could say one sentence, quietly, close to their ear. It would take no time at all. It is a thing you have never said.

You do not say it. What catches you is not that you chose silence but that you notice you did not choose anything: the moment arrives and goes as it always goes, and they do the next thing at exactly the usual time. Two doubts arrive with it, and both are yours. The first is about you. Do you actually want the other thing, or is it a passing thought you would like less in practice than in prospect? The second is about them, and it is the one you can do nothing about tonight. Would they? They have never said either. Their quiet might mean that this, precisely this, is what they want. It might mean they have a sentence of their own. From where you are, the two look identical.

And what would the sentence cost? Not much if they wanted it too; then it would be the easiest thing you ever said. The harder case is the other one. A no would be simple enough to hear. What would stay is that they would now know you had wanted it, and you would know they had not, and they would know they had turned you down for it. Both of you would carry that into every ordinary night after this one. So the moment passes, and the rest goes the way it goes, and it is good, and you are fairly sure it was good for them. Fairly sure. If you could each change what you do together, and neither of you does, is that because you are both happy, or because neither of you can find out?

## 2. What This Article Will Explain

That question has an answer, though not the one either of the obvious replies would give. Both of the obvious replies — "just say it" and "if it mattered, you would" — turn out to be incomplete; this essay shows where they stop. It works the moment through as economics, in a small game with two people, each with a private preference, a guess about the other's, and a cost to asking. It does four things. First, it shows why a shared default is useful, and when it is simply a gift that saves two people from having to work out the same thing every time. Second, it finds the condition under which the same default also keeps two people away from something both would take. Third, it shows why, from inside the couple, there is no way to tell which of those two situations you are in. Fourth, it asks who pays for asking, and what would have to change for the silence to come apart.

Three things belong up front. Every claim here is a result in a simple model, an assumption stated as one, or a gap stated as a gap; where the evidence is thin, which is most places, the essay says so. There is almost no couple-level evidence for the central pattern in this piece; the sexual pluralistic-ignorance literature is about peers, and the couple-level misperception studies show patterns that do not quite line up with it. This essay is about two people who both want to be doing what they are doing; it is not about pressure, refusal or anything worse. Roles are used throughout, not sexes: "the one who usually starts" rather than any pronoun that would fix a sex. The literature that sorts those roles by sex is noted where it appears, and it is sample-bound (mostly heterosexual, mostly student). The game below is this essay's own synthesis of pieces from several sources; the sources did not write it.

The place to start is why the default is there at all, and when it is simply a gift.

## 3. Why A Default Is Good

Strip the evening down to its moving parts. Two people, a sequence they both know, and in principle a different sequence either could try. If they both prefer the usual way, the usual way is the better outcome and the only question is how they both landed on the same one. If they both prefer the variant and each knows the other does, they just do the variant. If one prefers the usual way and the other the variant, there is a real disagreement, and the usual way is what happens when nobody changes anything. Worth noting now: the trap mechanism that Section 4 derives applies primarily to the first case gone wrong — when both prefer the variant, both are silent, and neither knows the other agrees.

The first case is the one economists have the cleanest name for. A **coordination problem** has two or more outcomes that both players prefer to not coordinating at all, and the question is how they end up on the same one. **Schelling's focal point** was his name for the outcome a pair of strangers would both guess, told nothing except that they had to meet somewhere in New York.[^1] **Lewis's convention** was its formalisation: a self-sustaining equilibrium that holds because each person expects the other to hold up their side.[^2] The sexual default that both prefer is this: a Lewis-convention sustained by expectations and precedent, with no author and nobody to thank. Nobody needs to perform the coordination each night, because the coordination has already been done.

The third case is different in kind. Economists call it **Battle of the Sexes**: two outcomes, both players prefer coordinating to not coordinating, but they rank the two outcomes oppositely. Suppose on the usual way one person gets 3 and the other gets 1, and on the variant it reverses, and failing to coordinate pays zero. There are two pure equilibria, (usual, usual) and (variant, variant), each giving one person their best and the other their second-best. There is a mixed equilibrium in which each plays their own preferred move with probability three-quarters and the expected payoff is about three-quarters each.[^3] Which equilibrium gets picked turns on precedent, on who moved first in the first instance, on which outcome was easier to settle on at the start. The usual way wins because it is what happens when nobody changes anything. The convention label fits this case only in a stretched sense: Lewis's analysis was built for pure coordination problems and critics have noted it cannot straightforwardly accommodate partial conflict.[^2] Where preferences diverge, the practised script is the status quo of a disagreement, not evidence of agreement. In a symmetric game where both players rank the variant above the script — in this symmetric game — risk dominance and payoff dominance coincide: each player's risk calculation points the same way as the payoff ranking.[^3]

Both cases earn the default its keep. In the first, the default is simply a gift. In the third, the default settles a disagreement in favour of whoever happens to prefer what both are already doing. Neither case is a trap. Already we can state a derived necessary condition for the trap thesis: a better option that both know about and can reach without cost does not lock in. If both prefer the variant and both know it, the variant is both the payoff-dominant and the risk-dominant equilibrium, and nothing holds the couple to the usual way. If a trap exists, it needs something else: either an information failure, or a cost of asking, or both.

A word about the word "script". The term is sociologists', and in sociology a sexual script is a learned template at three levels: a cultural scenario (what the surrounding world treats as the shape of the thing), an interpersonal script (what this pair does in practice), and an intrapsychic script (what each person internally expects). The three-level scheme is more securely attributed to a 1986 paper by Simon and Gagnon than to the 1973 book that gave the field its name, and neither text was read for this essay.[^4] The sociological script is a template, not an equilibrium. Reading it as an equilibrium, which is what this essay does, is this essay's own move; the cultural scenario is where the belief about the other's preferences comes from, the interpersonal script is the practised equilibrium of the couple, and the intrapsychic script is each person's private preference. That mapping is original synthesis, not something Gagnon and Simon say; they do not, as far as the sources suggest, say anything about the cost of deviating from a script.

Everything in this section is about couples for whom the default is working. The rest of the essay is about the one case left: when the default is holding two people away from something both would take.

## 4. The Ask

Return to the moment in Section 1. The way to get at the trap is to ask what it would cost to end the silence.

Consider two people. Each privately either prefers the usual way or prefers a variant; call the two private preferences **S** (for the script) and **V** (for the variant). Neither can see which the other is. Each has a guess about how likely the other is to prefer the variant; call that guess *q*. Asking for the variant costs something: effort, to begin with, the ordinary cost of getting a sentence said in a moment where no sentence usually goes. And an extra cost, paid only if the other turns out to prefer the usual way: now they know you wanted something else, and you know they did not, and you were wrong about them. If the other also prefers the variant, the ask works and both of you gain. If you both prefer the variant and both guess the other probably does not, nobody asks, and nothing is learned.

Before any formal name, the criterion for what "prefers" means has to be stated. Someone "prefers the variant" in this essay if they would take it in the limit case where asking cost nothing and the other's answer was certain. Call this the **free-and-certain test**: *if asking cost you nothing and you knew they would say yes, would you take it?* It is a test about current preference; it says nothing about who you would have been without the practice, which is a separate matter the essay flags briefly below. If the practice itself shaped the preference, that may be why you pass the test or why you fail it. That is a limit, named and left; adaptive-preference theorists disagree about whether it matters at all,[^5] and this essay does not try to settle them.

Now the result. Call *g* the gain to a person who prefers the variant from actually getting it; call *E* the costs that are paid whichever way the answer goes (effort of saying it, plus whatever ongoing cost comes from being known as the one who wanted a change); call *x* the extra cost paid only if the other prefers the usual way (the exposure cost: being read by someone who does not share the preference). A person who prefers the variant and believes the other prefers it with probability *q* gains *q·g* on average from asking, and pays *E* for certain plus (1 − *q*)·*x* on average, assuming the other person is also silent — the condition used to find when silence is a best response. Asking is worth it when the first exceeds the second: when *q* is above a threshold *q\**, where

> *q\** = (*E* + *x*) / (*g* + *x*).

Below that threshold, silence is the best response. One illustration, with numbers invented to show the structure. If getting the variant is worth 4 to the person who wants it, the ongoing cost of being known as the one who wanted a change is 0.5, and the cost of being read by someone who does not share the preference is 2, then *q\** is about 0.42: the believed chance that the other also prefers the variant has to be more than two in five before asking becomes rational. If the only cost were a flat cost of asking, independent of the other's type (what the project brief originally wrote as *q·g* < *c*), the threshold would be (*E* + *x*)/*g* = 0.625: higher. A model that charges the exposure cost only in the event it is actually paid gives a lower threshold than one that charges it always. Both numbers are illustrations of structure, not estimates of anything about couples.

| What the model needs | What it means in English | Example value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *g* | What getting the variant is worth to a person who prefers it | 4 |
| *E* | Costs paid whichever way the answer goes (effort, ongoing cost of being known) | 0.5 |
| *x* | Extra cost paid only if the other prefers the usual way | 2 |
| *q\** | Belief threshold at which asking just breaks even | 0.42 |
| (Dossier sketch) | What a flat-cost version gives, for comparison | 0.625 |

The row labelled "Dossier sketch" is not a parameter of this model. It shows the threshold a constant-cost version would produce; the corrected model, which charges the exposure cost only when it is actually paid, gives the lower threshold of 0.42.

Now the names, once. The silence region is a **pooling equilibrium**: both types of person, those who prefer the variant and those who prefer the usual way, behave the same way (silence), so nothing is revealed. A proposal, if it ever came, would be **separating**: a person who prefers the usual way never proposes the variant, because they stand to gain nothing and pay a cost whichever way the answer goes, so proposing is dominated for them. In the pooled silence, nothing is learned about who prefers what. That is a signalling fact, not a therapy observation; it does not say anyone should do anything. Silence would separate types only if the cost of staying quiet differed between people who prefer the variant and people who prefer the usual way — if, for instance, a type-V person paid something extra to suppress the ask. The model does not assume that, so in the silence region both types look the same.

A note on *x*. It is the pivotal parameter and it is also an assumption. The evidence for this runs to felt risk: people describe sexual disclosure as risky, cite rejection and embarrassment and judgment as reasons for not raising sexual preferences, and report fear of rejection as a motive for avoiding disclosure.[^6] That evidence is consistent with the exposure cost in this model, but it is also consistent with a plain emotional cost, or with a simple cost of effort; the studies do not separate them. The reputational-signal reading, that the cost of asking comes partly from what the ask tells the partner about you, is this essay's assumption, labelled now and not re-labelled below. Research on sexual communication finds that it correlates with sexual function, with the correlation about twice as large in married samples as in dating samples,[^7] but a correlation between communication and function does not establish any cost of communicating; it is a different claim.

Call that the central mechanism of the piece. Below the threshold, both people stay quiet, and if both happen to prefer the variant, both lose the gain they would have shared. Above the threshold, something else happens. That is the next section.

## 5. Even When You Are Both Sure

Suppose the belief is above the threshold. You think, pretty strongly, that the other person wants the variant too. Does it follow that someone asks?

Not immediately. If you both prefer the variant and you both believe each other does, you both expect that the other will raise it. If the other raises it with probability one, your own ask only adds the cost of asking to a result that was already going to happen. The best move, given that the other will certainly raise it, is to stay quiet. But if you stay quiet and the other follows the same logic, nobody raises it. Both-silent is not an equilibrium above the threshold, but neither is both-propose. What survives is a mixed equilibrium: each person who prefers the variant raises it with some probability between zero and one, calibrated so the other is just indifferent between asking and waiting.

Economists call this a **volunteer's dilemma**: a situation in which a shared good is produced if anyone acts, nobody wants to be the one who acts, and the equilibrium is probabilistic.[^8] The arithmetic says: at a belief of one-half (both think the other prefers the variant about half the time), the probability a person who prefers it actually raises it is 0.25, and the probability a couple who both prefer it reach it in one round is about 0.44. At a belief of 0.8, those become 0.72 and 0.92. Even at full certainty (both prefer it, both know the other prefers it), the probability of adoption in a single round is about 0.98, not 1. That gap is not a mistake. It is what the model says happens when neither person wants to carry the cost of starting something that the other was going to start anyway.

The one-shot framing is doing some work there. Couples do not meet each other once. Over time, the volunteer's-dilemma failure can be solved by taking turns: one of you raises things one week, the other raises things the next, and the arithmetic above becomes an upper bound on how often a mutually-wanted variant is missed. Whether real couples take turns, how quickly the alternation forms, whether the default hardens as the years pass (ossification is the next-door essay's object[^9]) are questions this one does not answer. The point this section wants is narrower: ignorance of the other's preference is not the only way the variant goes untaken. Even with full certainty on both sides, a single round can miss it.

That matters for what the trap means. A reader could imagine the trap as a straightforward belief problem: fix the belief, and everyone asks. The model says no. Fix the belief, raise it to one, and in a single round there is still a volunteer's-dilemma miss. So the lock-in is not purely about what each person believes about the other. It is also about who moves first, and when nobody is sure, and both would gain if someone did.

## 6. Silence Looks The Same From Outside

Here is the hardest part of the argument, and the honest reason the essay offers conditions rather than frequencies.

Below the threshold, every type of couple behaves the same way. A couple who both prefer the variant: silent. A couple who both prefer the usual way: silent (they were going to be anyway; they have no reason to raise anything). A couple where one prefers the usual way and the other prefers the variant: silent (the one who prefers the usual way had no reason to speak; the one who prefers the variant judged their belief below the threshold). Silence is what you see from a couple who are in a quiet trap, from a couple who are quietly happy, and from a couple where one person's preference is the one practised. From outside, from the behaviour, you cannot tell them apart.

Neither can you tell them apart from inside, in the way a questionnaire would. A survey asks a person what they prefer; the person answers; but the preference reported is itself produced inside the script. If the person has lived with the usual way for a long time and the question is whether they would want something else, the answer carries the shape of the thing they have lived with. That extension from behaviour to self-report goes beyond the model's literal scope; it rests on the assumption that reported preferences track the equilibrium the couple has been running, not a separately accessed private state. The couple-level studies that have looked at misperception of partners' sexual preferences find a role-dependent, direction-dependent pattern: in one, both members of 152 heterosexual couples said the actual durations of foreplay and intercourse were shorter than their ideal, and women underestimated their partners' preferred durations; both guessed the partner's ideal in a way that tracked their own stereotypes more than the partner's actual report.[^10] In another, following established couples, men tended to underperceive their partners' desire; the authors suggest this may be adaptive, a way of balancing pursuit against the risk of rejection, and underperception was associated with higher partner satisfaction and commitment.[^11] Those are not the pattern the lock-in needs. The pattern the lock-in needs is: both prefer the variant, both think the other does not. No study has measured that.

The sharper distinction sits beneath the phrase "mutual misreading." One version: the share of people who prefer the variant is below the threshold, everyone's belief is correct on average, and a couple who happen to both prefer the variant are silent because their correctly-held belief is below the threshold. Call this *calibrated and unlucky*: nobody is in error, and the loss happens anyway, to the small share of couples who happen to be in that profile. This is the stronger result: it does not depend on any mistake and is not addressed by correcting beliefs about peers. The other version: the true share of people who prefer the variant is higher than the believed share. Everyone underestimates how common the variant preference is, and silence spreads further than it should. Call this *miscalibrated*: there is a genuine error on top. The peer evidence bears on the second, not the first.

The peer-norm studies that most closely bear on this are not about couples. In a study of 507 students, each gender attributed higher comfort with sexual behaviours to same-gender peers than those peers reported being — a version of the miscalibrated pattern.[^13] A separate study found that students assumed peers were more comfortable with hooking up than those peers reported, though that finding is drawn from secondary accounts of the paper rather than its abstract.[^12] These findings are evidence for the miscalibrated pattern in a peer-norm context. They do not settle whether it holds in couples. The nearest classic peer-norm result, on students' misperception of their classmates' comfort with campus drinking, is the paper that gave the phenomenon its visibility in psychology; the women's attitudes in that study did not drift toward the misperceived norm over the semester, while the men's did.[^14] Replication status of these studies is not established in the record. All are abstract-level in the research dossier.

What could separate the versions? Variation in what people believe (hearing from a credible source what the other actually prefers, in a controlled way), variation in the cost of asking, or a baseline measured before the practice began. Nothing in the record has measured any of these in couples. So the essay offers a model of the mechanism and a map of the cases, and declines to say which case any given couple is in. That is not a hedge; it is a derived result of the identification problem. Silence is uninformative, and the couple who go through it cannot tell what case they are in by thinking about it.

> Silence is uninformative, and the couple who go through it cannot tell what case they are in by thinking about it.
{: .pull}

That has one other consequence. The brief this essay inherits originally spoke of "mutual misreading" as the engine of the trap. The model needs less: it needs the belief to sit below the threshold. The belief can be correct and the loss can still happen. The error reading is a possibility on top of the calibrated-and-unlucky case; the peer-norm evidence makes it plausible but non-identical to the couple problem, and the couple problem has not been directly tested.

## 7. Who Asks, And Who Pays

Suppose the belief is above the threshold, both prefer the variant, and someone does raise it. The question that answers is: who?

So far the two roles have been symmetric; the thresholds and the volunteer's-dilemma logic applied the same to each. Make them different. Suppose the two roles differ in cost. One role (call them A) finds it less costly to raise things: lower effort cost, lower exposure cost, maybe because the shape of what they already say makes a new sentence less costly. The other role (B) finds it more costly, for whatever reason. In the model, A's threshold is lower than B's. There is a stretch of belief, between A's threshold and B's, in which A would ask and B would not. In that stretch, whenever the variant gets raised at all, it is A who raises it. For the same gain, from the same wanted variant, the one who speaks is selected by cost.

That is a bare-bones distributional claim, and it needs care. It says who, in the model, bears the cost of asking: it is the role with the lower cost, not the role that wants it more. Where both roles want it equally and only one role is cheap enough to speak, the whole load of raising it falls on that role. Where no role is cheap enough (both thresholds above the belief), nobody speaks. This is cost selection of the asker, in the economics sense; it is not about who is more entitled to speak, or who should, or who deserves to pay. The model has no answer to those questions and the essay does not pretend otherwise.

Now initiation. Even where both roles want to begin at all, someone has to start. The same volunteer's-dilemma shape returns. If both wait, nothing happens. If both start together, one person's effort of starting was wasted. The symmetric mixed equilibrium, with starting costing some amount *c* against a benefit *b* if anyone starts, has each person start with probability 1 − *c*/*b*; in a plain example, each starts about two-thirds of the time, nobody starts about one time in nine, and both start about four times in nine. A rule that says who starts removes the failures of waiting and of doubling. It also moves the whole cost of starting onto one role. The initiator pays the cost of starting; the other person gains the benefit without paying it. This is the model of why the arrangement whichever-of-you-usually-starts, starts exists at all. It solves a real coordination problem. It also distributes the cost of solving it to one role.

A script that both agreed to is not neutral on that account alone. A default that assigns the cost of asking to one role is not neutral even when neither of you ever chose that allocation. Both the raising of a variant and the starting of an encounter have this shape. The cost falls on whoever's role carries lower cost of raising, and in the initiation case, on whichever role the precedent assigned to starting.

One honesty about roles. The sources that have looked empirically at who initiates sexually, at who holds which preferences, have mostly looked at heterosexual couples, mostly students or emerging adults, and they sort roles by sex. The script literature on heterosexual encounters consistently reports gendered patterns; a measurement scale for endorsing dominant heterosexual scripts was validated in that population.[^15] The model does not need the sorting. All it needs is that the roles differ in cost. Whether any particular real script gives one role a lower cost of raising a variant, or assigns the initiator role to the role with the higher cost (an inefficient script, which the model allows), is a question the model does not answer. Whether any real script gives one role what it wants, and leaves the other to carry the cost of asking without the gain, is the thing the brief's angle B pointed at and that the model gives a shape for; the record has nothing to say about which real scripts do that.

## 8. A Rule With No Author

A sibling essay in this series, on screens and the rules that are run on them, works across three elements: what someone discloses they prefer; the rule they announce to the person they are watching; and the rule they actually run.[^16] Its subject was a screener, someone who holds information and sets a rule and runs a different one; its central concept is a gap between what the watched believes the price of an action will be and what will actually follow. That is a gap about a stated price, held by someone who can set it.

The sexual script has no screener. There is no one who sets the price of asking. There is no stated rule to run differently from. Layer (a), disclosed preference, is empty: nobody has said anything. Layer (c), the strategy actually run, is the practised script: what the couple does. There is no layer (b): there is no stated rule to follow or depart from. What stands in for (b), structurally, is a mutual belief about the other's preference; and that belief sits in no one's head as a policy. It sits in both heads as a guess.

That is the honest link between the two essays. The sibling essay named the layers and pointed out that disclosed preference, layer (a), was mostly outside its own frame. This essay takes layer (a) as its object. The thing that is holding the pair in place is not a hidden rule or a concealed strategy; it is an empty layer (a) and a shared belief about a partner's type. Concealment, screens, tiers of information: those belonged to that essay, and are not carried here. The sibling essay's vocabulary for the gap between a stated and a run rule does not transfer, because there is no stated rule.

In a different family of defaults — the architectural ones (what you are automatically enrolled in on your pension, what the default option is on a form) — the default was set by someone. There is an architect, and the architect's choice of default has effects that have been studied; the robust finding is that defaults set by architects stick, even when the material terms of the choice have not changed.[^17] The sexual default has no architect. Nobody set it. It is the equilibrium that holds because neither of you can change it cheaply, not the equilibrium somebody set because they wanted you in it.

## 9. When Silence Is Not A Loss

The thesis so far: below the threshold, a couple who both prefer the variant are silent and lose the gain they would have shared. That is "lock-in" in the model. But the thesis has a boundary, and the boundary is a serious part of the argument, not a hedge.

When you weigh the ask, you ignore two things that happen to the other person. If the other also prefers the variant, your ask gives them *g* too; your gain was in your calculation, theirs was not. If the other prefers the usual way, your ask puts a cost on them: not just the awkwardness of the ask, but a cost of having to turn you down. Someone who has to decline a partner's wish for a variant pays something for giving the no. Call it *d*; it is a cost to the partner, not a moral claim, and it sits on their side of the ledger.

Add those two into the calculation. The calculation treats both people's gains and costs as comparable in one unit — an additive-utility assumption — so that "the gain to them" and "the cost to you" can be summed. That is a strong premise, and the parameter *d* inherits it. The joint ask-is-worth-it condition, from the pair's point of view, gives a different threshold, *q\*\**, where

> *q\*\** = (*E* + *x* + *d*) / (2*g* + *x* + *d*).

With the illustrative numbers from Section 4 (*g* = 4, *E* = 0.5, *x* = 2) and *d* = 0, the joint threshold is 0.25, well below the private threshold of 0.42. With *d* = 1, it is 0.32. With *d* = 2, it is 0.375.

Here is the formal content of "trap". It is the band between *q\*\** and *q\**: the stretch of belief in which a person privately stays quiet and the pair would, on average, have been better off if someone had spoken. In that band, private silence is a loss to the pair. Below the band, nobody speaks even on the joint criterion. Above it, people speak on both. The band is the one place where the thesis of this essay has formal content: private silence where asking would have raised the pair's expected joint surplus. That is narrower than "both want the variant and nobody asks", and it is narrower than the project brief's original phrasing. Not every (both-prefer-variant, both-silent) couple is in the band; some are below it on the joint criterion too.

Now the counter-reading. If turning someone down is costly enough to the person who has to turn you down, the band disappears entirely and silence in the whole region is jointly efficient. The condition for that is: if a decline is costly enough relative to what is shared, the two thresholds coincide and the band is empty. In the running example, that closes when the decline cost reaches about 2.86 — a number which rises when the shared gain *g* is larger and falls when the asker's ongoing cost *E* is large. In that case, every couple who are silent are silent for a reason the pair's own joint calculation agrees with. The default is doing exactly what a good convention does.

That is where the counter-reading in the couple evidence lands. The underperception of a partner's desire found in established couples went with higher partner satisfaction and commitment;[^11] the authors read it as motivationally adaptive, a way of protecting the pair from asks that would have been unwelcome. The model says: whether under-perception protects the pair or costs them turns on *d*. The parameter *d* is not a quantity either person in the couple can observe directly, so whether the model puts them in the trap or in the adaptive case cannot be determined from the couple's own reports. Where *d* is large, under-perception is adaptive, as the authors suggest. Where *d* is small, the same under-perception costs the pair the band. So the model does not choose between the trap reading and the adaptive reading. It says which parameter decides.

Adaptive preferences deserve the same weight in the same place, and they carry a harder implication. If the practice itself has shaped the preference, so that a person who once would have preferred the variant now prefers the usual way, the free-and-certain test comes out "prefers the usual way". By current preference, the couple is in the mutually-preferred case, and the convention is working. Elster called this autonomy-undermining and bad; others have argued it is often not a problem.[^5] This essay does not pick between them. But the adaptive-preferences qualification is the binding epistemic limit of the essay's central claim, not a named side condition: if preferences have drifted with the practice, the trap diagnosis is unavailable from inside by any means available to the couple. It names the condition under which adaptation makes the trap diagnosis vanish by current preference, and leaves what to make of that to the reader.

So the surviving claim is narrower than the brief's title. Defaults are fine in the mutually-preferred case. Silence is not a loss in the whole silence region when a decline is costly. The trap exists in a band whose width depends on parameters nobody has measured. Even inside the band, a single round can miss the variant for volunteer's-dilemma reasons (Section 5). And across all cases, silence looks the same from outside (Section 6).

## 10. What Would Unravel It

If silence persists partly because asking reveals your preference to someone who might not share it, suppose there were a way to say "yes" that revealed nothing unless the other also said "yes". A device, in the economist's abstract sense, that each person privately entered a preference into, and that produced the variant only if both entered "variant", and that revealed nothing to either side otherwise. Would such a thing unravel the pooling?

Partly, and under a condition. In the model, the device lowers the belief needed to make a wanted-variant get taken, because the exposure cost is no longer paid; a person who enters "variant" and whose partner enters "script" learns nothing and reveals nothing. For small device costs, the threshold is well below the direct-ask threshold. So the device opens up the (both-prefer-variant) case that was being lost in the band and some way below it.

That is only half the result. The other half is that non-participation in the device is always an equilibrium too. If you expect that nobody uses the device, using it costs you something and gets you nothing. So the device unravels the pooling only when there is common knowledge that people use it: cheap and public, not just cheap. A device that is low-cost but obscure is just another silent default, with the same information structure the ordinary silence had.

That is a model result, not a recommendation. The essay is firewalled from how-to; what to do about any of this is a different conversation, and belongs to another kind of book. The relevant point is that the lock-in has a shape, and the shape says what would and would not change it. Nothing cheap is enough on its own; cheap plus commonly-known might be.

The device also does not help the mixed cases. Where one person prefers the variant and the other prefers the usual way, no mechanism that requires both to enter "variant" produces the variant, and the ordinary disagreement is unchanged. Section 7's distributional point survives too: a device is a lever on the belief side, not on who pays for raising a variant inside a couple where the belief is already clear.

## 11. What The Model Does Not Prove

Put the strongest version of the objection in full.

> *You have formalised the feeling that asking is awkward. The exposure cost is an assumption the model needs rather than a measurement anyone has made. Nobody has tested the central pattern in couples. And you have shown conditions, not frequencies — so what exactly has this model added?*

Most of this is right, and the reply concedes it.

It is right that there is no direct couple-level test of the pattern the model needs. The research found pluralistic-ignorance studies about peers, misperception studies about partners' desire with a different direction of error than the model's pattern, and felt-risk studies about sexual disclosure that could not separate a reputational cost from an effort or emotional one.[^6][^7][^10][^11][^12][^13][^14] The piece has been explicit about this throughout. It is right that no prevalence claim is being made: this essay says nothing about how often couples are in the trap, how often they are in the mutually-preferred case, how often they are in the band. The parameters that would decide those frequencies have not been measured.

It is right that the exposure cost is an assumption. Section 4 said so.

The model cannot be applied to any real couple. It offers a vocabulary for a situation, not a diagnosis of one.

Two further precision points. First: the mechanism described in this essay applies where the exposure cost *x* is non-trivial. Couples who feel no reputational risk from asking — for whom *x* is negligible — are outside the model's scope; whatever holds them quiet is not what this essay has argued about. Second: the population of silently-trapped couples is a subset of the silent population whose size is unknown. The identification result in Section 6 shows all silent couples look alike from outside. The selection problem says that the couples this essay is really concerned with — (V,V) couples in the trap band — may be a small minority of the silent population. The essay addresses the condition rather than the prevalence; these are different claims.

The essay's distinctive contribution is also narrower than its general framing. The model's genuinely new machinery is the (V,V) case: both partners prefer the variant, both are silent, and neither knows the other agrees. Desire discrepancy — one role's preference is the one practised, the other role carries the cost of asking — is a better-evidenced and well-studied phenomenon that the model accommodates in H5 without defeating. The separation of H1 (both want it, wrong beliefs), H4 (one wants it, cost of asking too high) and H5 (one role's preference is practised) is the model's content, even though the cases cannot currently be distinguished empirically.

It is right that the model turns people into types with gains and costs, and that intimate life is not reducible to that. Every model turns its subject into something smaller than the subject is; the gain of the model is that it can say which part of the problem is a belief problem (the belief *q* about the other), which part is a cost problem (the exposure cost *x*, the decline cost *d*), and which part is a distributional problem (the different costs by role). Separating them is what economics does here. It does not tell anyone to raise or not raise a question.

What survives: a derived threshold for silence, a derivation that silence looks the same across very different couples, a volunteer's-dilemma result that works even above the threshold, a formal definition of the trap as a narrow band whose width depends on *d*, a cost-selected asker, an original synthesis of why there is no screener and no stated rule to blame, and a device result with a two-part condition. "Communicate more" is not the content of any of those. The reply to "it just adds jargon" is that the jargon is the condition, not the exhortation. There is a difference between telling someone to speak and telling them where silence sits in a map.

A few smaller objections, briefly. The model treats people as strategic about intimacy: it does, as every model does; the exposure cost is the strategic premise and has been named. Roles are often sorted by sex in the sources: they are, and the model does not need the sorting; the source literature is heterosexual-sampled and mostly student-sampled, and the generalisation is bounded accordingly. The author is excusing a shared silence: no anchor case is in play, no narrator is implied, and the essay has described a mechanism and left who it fits to the reader.

The default is not a trap, you might reply. Agreed, in the mutually-preferred case (Section 3) and in the band's empty case (Section 9). The claim this essay makes is narrower than "scripts are traps". It is: there is a band in which silence is a loss, and silence looks the same inside the band as it does outside it. From inside, the couple cannot tell which case they are in.

## 12. Questions To Ask Of Your Own Case

None of these tells you what to do. Each asks which side of a boundary of the model you are on. The answers are not advice; they are the beginning of a classification, and the last line of this section says why even that is only partial.

**Is what you do together something either of you would pick, or something neither has ever said?** Try the free-and-certain test on yourself. If asking cost you nothing, and you knew the answer would be yes, would you take something else? If you would not, nothing in this essay applies to you. If you would, you are at least a type-V, by this essay's definition, for whatever the something-else is. That tells you about yourself. It does not tell you about them.

**If you imagine the other saying yes to a variant, do you think they would, and what is that belief based on?** This is *q*, in plain language. Is the belief based on things they have said, or on things they have not said? Silence does not tell you where their silence sits in the map any more than yours tells them where yours sits.

**What would asking cost you: the answer, or what the asking would tell them about you?** If the answer, you are weighting the gain against the response. If what the asking would tell them, you are weighting the gain against the exposure cost: the thing you were going to find out from the no is less than the thing they will know about you forever if they say no. The two feel different, and the second is the one the model needs. If asking does not feel like it would cost you anything to be known for, the silence this essay is about is not yours.

**Who, in your script, pays the cost of asking?** The model says cost selects the asker. If you look at your own patterns, is there a role in your couple that does more of the raising of things to be done differently? Does that role have the lower cost of raising, or the higher stakes? And does the role that does more of the raising get what it wants most of the time, or do the two things not line up?

**Has the thing you do now changed how much you want it?** This is the adaptive-preferences question, and it has to come last because it undoes the others. If the answer is yes, your current preference is a product of the practice, and the free-and-certain test is being run on a preference the practice shaped. There is no way out from inside; a baseline from before would have been needed. If the answer is no, your current preference and your free-and-certain preference are the same, and the earlier questions carry.

The point of asking these is to classify, not to decide. And the last honesty is that your answers are produced inside the script too. A person who has lived with the usual way for a long time answers "would you want something else" out of a self that lived with the usual way. The questions do not escape the problem the model names in Section 6. They just put you a little further on from where you were before you had read it.

## 13. The Thing You Have Never Said

Return to the point in the sequence. The lamp has gone off; whichever of you usually starts, started; the moment arrived where you could have done one thing differently, and you did not. Nothing resolves. The next ordinary night goes the way the ordinary nights go.

Here is what the essay has given you. A shared silence between two people carries no information about the preferences behind it. It can be a convention, if both of you prefer what you do and the quiet is a gift. It can be a courtesy, if a decline would cost them enough that silence is doing both of you a kindness. It can be a trap, in a narrow band of belief, where speaking would raise the pair's expected gain and neither of you has judged the ask worth its cost. And it can be a disagreement neither of you would call that, in which the usual way is the status quo of a difference you have not said out loud. From inside, in a single ordinary night, none of these is distinguishable from any of the others.

The economics does not tell you which one you are in. The parameters that would decide it have not been measured, and your own report of them is produced inside the script. What the economics tells you is: there is a map, and the map has a band in it. In the band, two people who both would gain do not. Outside the band, silence is doing what silence is for. The silent moment that passes in your sequence tonight is somewhere on the map, and no information available to you from inside the moment places it.

That is a diagnosis, not a plan. You could sit with it, or you could not; the essay has not told you which. If what you do together is what you both assumed the other wanted, how would you find out, and what would finding out cost?
