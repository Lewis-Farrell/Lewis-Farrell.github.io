## 1. Whatever You Think

It is late, the laptop is closed between you, and the offer expires on Friday. The job is good, better than good, and it is in another city: different rent, different schools, a different set of people who will learn your names. You have both done the arithmetic. You both know the facts. You have each said what matters most, and, slightly disconcertingly, it is the same thing.

"I'm fine either way," you say.

"Me too," says your partner, a little too fast.

Neither of you is fine either way. Somewhere under the agreement sits a preference, held by at least one of you and possibly by both, that has not quite been said out loud, because saying it would turn it into a demand, and you are not the kind of couple that makes demands. So the conversation does what these conversations do. It circles. One of you mentions the commute that would no longer exist. The other mentions a friend who would no longer be around the corner. Each remark is offered as information, and each is received as a move.

By Thursday one of you is going to say "okay, let's do it", or "okay, let's not", and it will sound like agreement. A year from now, if anyone asks, you will both say *we decided*. It will be true, in the sense that nobody was overruled and nobody shouted. It will also be close to useless as an account of what happened, because neither of you will be able to say with any confidence who decided. Possibly not even the one who said "okay", who may never be sure whether that was a choice or something else.

Two people who love each other, want the same future and have exactly equal standing still have to end up somewhere. This essay is about what puts them there, when nothing in the structure of their marriage does.

## 2. What a Merger Is Made Of

The previous essay in this series ended on who decides, and left an awkward question open: what changes when two people's exposure to each other's decisions keeps growing, but neither has the final say?^1 Marriage is where that question stops being hypothetical.

There is an obvious explanation for the scene at the laptop, and it is the one people reach for half-jokingly in wedding speeches: marriage is a merger. This essay takes that explanation seriously. It lets the metaphor do the work it can genuinely do, on money, routines and the slow grind of combining two households, and then shows where it breaks. The break is not a footnote to the argument; it is the argument. By the end you should have a way to read your own arrangement, domain by domain, that tells you what is actually settling your disagreements.

Start with what a merger is, stripped of the deal-room language. When two companies combine, three different things get bundled together. The first is **exposure**: who bears the gains and the losses. After the deal closes, one group of owners carries the risk of the combined firm. The second is **authority**: who has the last word. After closing there is one hierarchy and, at the top of it, someone whose decision ends the argument. The third is **operations and culture**: the routines, systems and habits through which the place actually runs. The first two are settled on closing day. The third takes years, and it is where much of the trouble lives.

Now put marriage through the same split.

Exposure is fused almost completely. A shared home, shared debts, the costs of any children, a joint reputation, a mortgage that one partner's bad year will dent for both, much of it backed by law. Culture is fused slowly and with friction, which is what the first years of most marriages are made of. Authority is not fused at all. Nobody becomes the boss. Two people of equal formal standing are jointly exposed to every large decision either of them might want to make, and neither has the right to make it.

| | Corporate merger | Marriage |
|---|---|---|
| Exposure (who bears gains and losses) | Fused at closing | Almost fully fused, often by law |
| Authority (who has the last word) | Fused at closing: one hierarchy | **Not fused** |
| Operations and culture (how daily life runs) | Fused over years | Fused over years, with friction |

That is the puzzle in a line: marriage gives a couple merger-level exposure and alliance-level authority. The three-way split is this essay's own way of cutting the problem, not something lifted from a study, and the rest of the piece is an attempt to find out what follows from it.

One calibration before going further. "Never quite merges" is a title, not a law of nature. Plenty of couples, and plenty of legal systems, really do treat the pair as a unit for many purposes: joint tax filing, joint tenancy, a shared surname, a shared answer to "how are you two?" The claim here is narrower. It is about decision authority when the two people actually want different things. If you and your partner have never disagreed about anything large, nothing that follows applies to you, and you are free to go and be quietly smug somewhere else. The same logic extends to long-term cohabiting couples who pool their lives: the legal fusion is weaker, but the missing tiebreaker is identical.

## 3. Closing Day Is the Start

In an acquisition, the signing ends one kind of work and starts another. The lawyers go home; then someone has to decide whose accounting system survives, whose way of running a meeting wins, which of two overlapping teams keeps its manager. None of this was in the contract, and much of it matters more than what was.

A wedding is closing day. What follows is integration, which looks nothing like a corporate process and works in much the same way. Is the money one pot, two pots, or two pots and a resentful third? Whose family's Christmas becomes the household's Christmas, and whose becomes the one you visit on the 27th? How quickly is a message supposed to be answered? Does a free Saturday belong to the couple by default, or to whoever claims it first? Each of these is a small routine. Together they are an operating culture, built out of two that already existed.

The research on acquisitions gives this a sharper shape. In a case survey of 61 mergers and acquisitions, Larsson and Finkelstein found that whether the promised gains were realised depended not only on how well the two businesses fitted on paper but on how integration was coordinated and on how much the acquired firm's employees resisted it.^2 Later work treats integrating the tasks and integrating the people as distinct processes that interact.^3 Haspeslagh and Jemison's *Managing Acquisitions* is usually described as arguing that the right integration approach turns on a trade-off: how much the two sides need each other, against how much the acquired side needs to keep its own way of working in order to stay valuable. That is a secondary-level summary; hold it loosely.^4

The same caution applies to the idea in this literature that matters most here. Nahavandi and Malekzadeh's work on acculturation in mergers is generally described as treating the forced absorption of one party's culture into the other's as a recognised route to failure, not a strong form of success.^5 Again, that is at second hand. But the marital version does not depend on the citation; it needs only to be stated. **Integration that works because one partner has disappeared is not a more complete marriage. It is the failure case.** A household in which one person's habits, friends, family rhythms and ambitions have quietly been replaced by the other's can look wonderfully smooth from outside. Smoothness is not the measure. Hold on to this; the analogy will break on it later.

What is all this, as evidence? Not much. Nobody has studied marriage in these terms. The merger research is a hypothesis about mechanism: it tells you where to look for friction, not whether marriages suffer from it the way firms do. Marital evidence would be something like a link between how a couple's routines are pooled early on and how their conflict runs later, and the M&A literature does not provide it. Nor does it offer a tidy failure rate to borrow, whatever the business press says. What the meta-analytic record shows is that acquiring firms' performance after a deal is, on average, flat to modestly negative, with a great deal of variation nobody has explained,^6 and that "success" means different things depending on whether you measure share prices, accounts, or whether the integration delivered what it promised.^7

## 4. Nobody Holds the Last Word

Every agreement leaves things out. A commercial contract can list prices, delivery dates and penalties, but it cannot list every contingency, because nobody can foresee them all and writing them down would cost more than it is worth. Economists call such agreements incomplete. Marriage is a particularly acute case: the "contract", such as it is, is a promise to share a life, and almost every decision that will actually matter inside it (this move, this parent's care, this risk with the savings) is unspecified on the day it is made. What makes marriage interesting is not incompleteness alone, but incompleteness in the presence of fully fused exposure and no named holder of the right to decide the gaps. Pollak argued four decades ago that the family is usefully understood as a governance structure for exactly this reason: a way of handling what cannot be written down.^8

The theory of incomplete contracts has something precise to say about the gaps. In the work of Grossman and Hart, and then Hart and Moore, when a contract cannot specify every decision, someone must hold the right to decide the cases it did not foresee, and who holds that right shapes how much each party is willing to invest and how they bargain over what the relationship produces.^9 In their theory the right comes from owning the assets the parties work with: if you own the machine, you decide what happens to it in every situation the contract forgot.

I will use one term from this literature, and define it once. **Residual decision rights** are the rights to decide whatever the agreement did not foresee. Not who decides an item under a rule you have both agreed on (I will just call that a decision right), but who decides the thing nobody planned for.

Now the honest part. Extending this theory to spouses is my analogy, not something the theory says. What can be borrowed is the logic: unspecified decisions do not vanish, they land with someone, and who that is shapes incentives. What cannot be borrowed is the source of the power. In Grossman, Hart and Moore it comes from owning non-human assets, and spouses do not own each other. Whatever residual decision rights exist in a marriage, they do not come from a title deed.

The frame explains nothing that the household bargaining models do not, except in one respect: it names the structure as one where neither party holds formal authority, which the bargaining models assume rather than explain. That is what it adds, and it is enough to earn its place.

The analogy also breaks in ways that matter. In the theory, a holder of residual rights exists by construction. In a marriage the structure deliberately names none; neither partner holds the last word by right, so if one of them exercises it, they do so in practice, not by entitlement. The mechanism differs too. An owner's power rests on the threat to withhold access to an asset. Between spouses the nearest equivalents are different objects altogether: what each partner could do instead, and how long each can bear to leave a decision unmade. Nor is de facto authority observable or transferable in the way ownership is: there is no title to check, and whoever happens to decide more cannot simply hand that position to the other. (A co-owned house may come with legal rules about who can sell it, but that is a narrow case and not the claim.)

So the useful version is a question, not a theorem. If marriage is an incomplete contract with no named holder of residual decision rights, the unforeseen decisions still get made. By what?

## 5. What Decides When Nothing Does

The uncomfortable answer: by things other than which option is better — the route need not track decision quality, as the toy below shows, even when both people accept the result.

Start with the best-supported candidate, **outside options**. Household bargaining models, from Manser and Brown and McElroy and Horney through Lundberg and Pollak's "separate spheres", and in a different register Chiappori's collective model, share a core idea: what a couple ends up with depends on each partner's position, above all on what each could get if cooperation broke down, by leaving or by retreating into a colder, more separate version of the marriage.^10 The essay on making it official works through the machinery;^11 this one imports the conclusion. That is a theoretical prediction about how bargaining outcomes depend on relative position, not direct evidence that outside options are what settle any particular marital dispute. The partner with the better fallback does not win every argument. They can just afford to lose fewer of them.

The second candidate is **tolerance of delay**. A disagreement that nobody can settle by right is often settled by whoever can stand it staying unsettled for longer. This is the same quantity the previous essay called "how long you can let it sit",^1 applied now not to the unbooked dentist or the overflowing recycling but to one large, one-off decision with a deadline. It has no obvious relationship to who is right.

> **A toy, with invented numbers**
>
> Two partners must choose between moving to the city of A's job offer and staying put. Score each outcome for each partner, purely to show the structure.
>
> - Move: A gets 10, B gets 0.
> - Stay: A gets 4, B gets 7.
>
> Taken together, staying is the better choice: 11 against 10. But suppose each week of stalemate costs A half a point and costs B two, because B finds the standoff harder to live with. For A, giving way means accepting 4 instead of 10, a loss of 6; at half a point a week, A can hold out for 12 weeks. For B, giving way means accepting 0 instead of 7, a loss of 7; at two a week, B can hold out for three and a half. B's endurance runs out first. The couple moves, and adopts the jointly worse option.
>
> Change the weekly costs and the answer flips. At no point does the quality of the decision enter the calculation.
>
> Limits: this shows that the route of settlement *can* diverge from the better decision, not that it usually does. A could compensate B enough to make moving worthwhile for both, but only if compensation is possible, trusted and priced, which is exactly where an incomplete contract bites. The numbers illustrate a structure; they estimate nothing.

The third candidate is **how much each partner cares**, and this is where "I'm fine either way" lives. The partner who minds less concedes more cheaply, which sounds fair enough. The trouble is that "I don't mind" can be two quite different things: a true report of indifference, or the residue of a concession made earlier and so thoroughly absorbed that it now feels like a preference. From outside, and often from inside, you cannot tell which.

The fourth is **who puts the options on the table**. Whoever frames the choice ("so, do we take it?") has defined the default and the ask, and the other partner is left to respond. This is this essay's own hypothesis — not something the bargaining literature names — though an unsettling one for anyone who is always the one asking. There is no direct evidence on how much it matters.

None of the four is the better argument. And because each ends in a decision both people accept, "we decided" tells you the outcome but not the route. The proposer, the resister and the partner who bore the cost cannot be read off the result. No study, to this essay's knowledge, has measured who proposed, who resisted and who bore the cost from the same event — which is the only way the claim could be tested.

Is there evidence on who tends to concede? On one large decision, relocation, there is some. In administrative data from Germany and Sweden, couples are markedly more likely to move after the man loses his job than after the woman loses hers; and women who move as the trailing partner do not see the earnings gains that moving brings men, while women who move on their own account gain much as men do.^12 Plainly, then: in these datasets the partner who trails is disproportionately the wife. That fits the outside-options story — that whose career is treated as fixing the location reflects differences in fallback positions — but it does not test the mechanism; the same pattern is equally consistent with social norms about whose career is primary operating regardless of actual outside options. The evidence comes from particular countries in a particular period, and the norms behind it are contested. Take gender as one source of outside-option differences among several, not as the organising fact, and never as a forecast about any particular couple. The evidence base also has large holes: it says almost nothing about same-sex couples, unmarried partners, or countries beyond a handful of rich ones.

Same-sex couples are, in fact, the cleanest test of the argument. Two women or two men deciding whether to move for one of their jobs face exactly the same missing tiebreaker, with no inherited script about whose career leads. If the problem exists for them, and there is every structural reason to think it does, then the mechanism cannot depend on gender roles. Roles may load the dice. They are not the dice.

Browning and Chiappori, using Canadian household spending data, and Udry, using farm plots in Burkina Faso, both reject the idea that a household behaves like a single decision-maker.^13 What Browning and Chiappori show is that income pooling fails but the collective model's efficiency restriction is not rejected — outcomes depend on a sharing rule tied to relative position, without the household deciding worse than it could. What Udry shows, in a different setting, is that plots managed by women yield less than comparable plots of the same crop managed by men in the same household, a measurable yield gap the household would not leave on the table if it were efficient. Different countries, different settings, different tests: they disagree on efficiency. I am not going to adjudicate. The consequence matters: this essay does **not** claim that couples without a tiebreaker make worse decisions. Its claim is about distribution: who concedes, and who carries the cost.

Finally, where these tools stop. A partner who controls the other by threat, isolation or force is not winning a negotiation, and nothing here describes it. Nor is structural dependence (an illness, a visa tied to the other's job) a bargaining skill; it is a position. This essay offers no tactics for winning a marital dispute.

## 6. The More You Merge, the Costlier to Leave

Integration cuts both ways. The more a couple shares (the home, the finances, a career one partner has built around the other's location) the cheaper it becomes to coordinate, because there are fewer separate arrangements to reconcile. And the costlier it becomes to leave.

That second effect is rarely symmetric. If one partner's network is local and the other's portable, or one has stepped back from a career and the other has not, each additional layer of integration worsens one fallback faster than the other. By the logic of the previous section, the partner whose fallback worsens first concedes more next time, and the time after. This is my application of a result the bargaining literature already owns, not a finding; there is no direct evidence for it.

None of which makes pooling a mistake. Olson and colleagues randomly assigned engaged and newlywed couples to merge their money into a joint account, to keep it separate, or to receive no instruction; over two years, relationship quality held up among the joint-account couples where it declined in the other groups.^14 That is genuine support for pooling, in that population and over that horizon. It does not test how disagreements get settled, which is the question here. The deeper treatment of exit costs, as a bond that makes promises credible, belongs to the essays on making it official and on commitment design;^11 ^15 what happens to a partner's voice as leaving gets harder belongs to the essay on exit and voice.^16

## 7. Where the Merger Breaks

So far the merger frame has earned its keep: it explained why the early years grind, why integration has winners, why the cost of leaving creeps upward. Now it fails, and the failure is the point.

The decisive break is authority. A merger settles disputes by hierarchy: when the two former companies disagree, someone senior decides, and that is the end of it. That is what fusing authority means. Marriage does not do this, and should not. The missing chief executive is not a bug in the institution awaiting a patch. In a marriage of equals it is the whole point, the thing that separates it from older arrangements in which one spouse's word was final. Which means the practical task cannot be to find a boss. It has to be to find rules.

The second break is exit. An acquired firm does not walk out of the parent company. Spouses can, and the fact that they can is part of what makes the marriage worth having: a partnership that either person could leave, and neither does, means something that a locked one does not. But exit is also, as the last two sections showed, the quiet source of de facto authority. The option that dignifies the marriage is the same option that tilts its arguments.

The third break is the one planted earlier. In a takeover there is a buyer and a bought, and everyone knows which is which. In a marriage there is no buyer, and formal symmetry is total. Yet in particular domains one partner may have been, in effect, absorbed: the one who moved to the other's city, took the other's name, adopted the other's family's calendar and gradually lost touch with their own friends. Nobody announced it. The takeover picture is useful here not because it describes marriage but because it detects what formal equality hides. If one partner's life looks, domain by domain, like the losing side of a consolidation, that is a finding about a hidden asymmetry. It is also, as section 3 put it, the failure case, not integration's triumph.

The remaining breaks can be stated more briefly, though none is minor. A firm has a single scorecard; a couple has plural values that do not convert into one another (dignity, affection, sex, freedom, time), so there is no marital equivalent of shareholder value, and this essay will not pretend to score anyone's marriage. People are not assets: pooling a life does not mean that each partner is worth what they contribute to the pool. Love is not an integration cost; the model explains why two people who love each other and agree on almost everything still grind, not why anyone loves anyone. And culture and law vary: where a religion, a legal code or a family system names a head of household, the chief executive is not missing, and the argument here does not apply.

What, then, does the deepest kind of alliance add to a looser one? Fused exposure to large decisions, without a fused right to make them. Which yields the thesis in full. **Marriage is not a merger. It is an unusually deep alliance whose members are expected to act as if they had merged, while remaining two people who can disagree.** The practical task is not to merge more or to merge less. It is to decide, domain by domain, what is shared, what is coordinated and what is left autonomous, and what the rule is when the two of you want different things.

## 8. The Objection

Here is the strongest case against all of this, put as its holders would put it. *Couples who decide jointly do better; you are pathologising the healthy ones and dignifying dysfunction. Your "missing tiebreaker" is a fancy name for the absence of a head of household, so either you are saying something trivial or you are smuggling in something reactionary. Most couples never fight about any of this. And the best marriages really do act as one.*

Some of that should be conceded outright. Joint decision-making, when it works, may well beat any rule, and nothing here argues otherwise. The nearest real evidence is on the objector's side: the joint-account experiment in section 6 found that pooling money helped.^14 The objection is also right that most couples, most of the time, are not locked in disputes about authority. How often real, large disagreements happen, and in which domains, is something the evidence does not tell us.

But the model is conditional: given a real disagreement, what settles it? It says nothing about whether joint decisions are good when you agree, and the pooling experiment does not test what happens when a pooled couple disagrees. Objection and model answer different questions.

On the head of household, the objection has it backwards. The argument is that formal equality does not guarantee equal influence, and that hierarchy is the one feature of a merger that marriage should not borrow. A rule is not a boss. "Each of us has the last word on our own family", or "we don't move unless both of us say yes", is an explicit default that both people can see, which is precisely what de facto authority is not.

As for the best marriages acting as one: even the best marriage, if it lasts, eventually meets a serious disagreement that love does not dissolve, and it needs some way through that is not simply endurance. A unity that rests on one partner having quietly disappeared is the failure case dressed up as the ideal. And if the deeper complaint is that an analogy cannot count as evidence, agreed: the merger material here has been used as a hypothesis about mechanism, not as data about marriage.

One cost deserves to be conceded in full. An audit of who concedes can turn a marriage into a ledger, and a ledger can manufacture the very grievance it was meant to measure. The risk is real. The audit is for couples who have already noticed a recurring pattern of unequal concession that nobody has named. Applied to a working arrangement it is more likely to cost than to give — the ledger the audit creates can be the problem, not just its description. What follows is offered as a way of seeing, not a way of scoring; the point of looking is to know what is deciding things, not to tally who is ahead.

What survives the objection is modest and durable: a mechanism with its conditions stated, evidence held to what it can carry, and a diagnostic that is a heuristic rather than a test.

## 9. The Audit

So, to your own marriage. What follows is not a quiz and not a compatibility score. It is a way of reading the actual governance of a shared life (what decides things, as opposed to what you say decides things) one domain at a time.

A domain can be in one of three states. **Shared**: pooled and decided together. **Coordinated**: kept separate but scheduled to fit, like two calendars that have to mesh. **Autonomous**: decided by one partner alone, because the other's interests are not really at stake. None of these is the right answer in general. The question is whether each domain is in the state you think it is in, and whether you would put it there on purpose.

Then take each domain through the same short interrogation. Start with what is true in practice, not by declaration: is this shared, coordinated or autonomous? Move to the disagreements: when we disagree here, what actually decides it, the better argument, or who can wait longer, who has more to fall back on, who cares less, who proposed? Then the uncomfortable one: who has been conceding, and is that a choice or a cost being absorbed? And last, the one that makes the others honest: what would it cost each of us to leave the arrangement as it stands?

| Domain | Typical exposure | Where hidden concession tends to sit | A rule worth examining | Its hazard |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Money | Fully fused | With whoever earns less, or whose spending is less visible | A joint pot plus a small autonomous allowance each | Any threshold is itself a rule that favours the status quo |
| Home and place | Fused: debt, location, often children | With whoever's job or network is easier to move | Pre-agreed turns on relocation, or a mutual veto | A veto creates stalemate; turns mis-price unequal stakes |
| Careers | Partly fused: shared income, separate identity | With the trailing partner | Priority that rotates across life stages | Rotation can hide a chronic cost to the same person |
| Extended family | Fused obligations, separate loyalties | With the partner whose family is far away | Each partner runs contact with their own family | Entrenches inequality if one family needs more |
| Social life and identity | Mostly autonomous | In the shrinking friendships of the less central partner | Autonomy by default | Autonomy can mask drift |
| Sex and body | Autonomous in principle | Not a bargaining domain | None: consent is a constraint, not an allocation | Treating it as a trade is the failure |
| Children | Where this essay stops | Not analysed here | Not analysed here | The subject of the next essay |

A deliberate arrangement is easiest to see against the three shapes it can fail into. The first is the one-person dictatorship: one partner decides nearly everything that matters, the other goes along, and both describe the result as harmony. The second is the perpetual stalemate: neither can decide, so the default wins every time, which means whoever benefits from the default wins every time. The third, and the one the "we decided" problem points at, is the hidden concession: a decision both partners call joint, one partner pays for, and neither has named. A deliberate arrangement is simply one in which, for each domain that matters, both people could say what state it is in and what happens when they disagree, and would choose that answer again.

The audit works in two directions. Pointed at your own marriage, it is screening: seeing the real arrangement beneath the declared one. Said out loud, its answers become signalling: making your own position and stakes legible to your partner, so that "I'm fine either way" no longer has to carry information it cannot carry. The second use is the less comfortable one, and the more useful.

Be clear about what the audit is. It is a heuristic built from the model, not a validated instrument; there is no evidence on whether audits like this help couples, or on the costs and benefits of any rule in the table. It is symmetric by design. If your answers come out lopsided, the asymmetry is something you found, not something the audit assumed.

## 10. A Short Menu of Rules

> **Options with hazards, none recommended**
>
> If the audit turns up a domain where nothing but endurance decides, here are five rules a couple could adopt instead. Each comes with its hazard attached, because each has one.
>
> - **Domain ownership.** Each partner has the final say in agreed areas, which entrenches inequality whenever one partner's areas matter more, and ignores the decisions that spill across.
> - **Mutual veto.** Nothing large happens unless both say yes, which invites stalemate and quietly favours whoever likes things as they are.
> - **Rotating tiebreak.** You take turns, which mis-prices disagreements where one partner's stakes are far higher, and can hide a chronic cost to the same person behind a scrupulously fair rota.
> - **Status-quo default.** If no decision is reached, the current arrangement stands, which favours whoever benefits from the current arrangement.
> - **Tie-break by stakes.** Whoever minds more decides, which works only if stakes can be seen; they cannot, and each partner has every reason to report minding more.

None of these is a recommendation. The audit exists to make the real route of settlement visible; a couple might reasonably read the menu and conclude that their unspoken arrangement beats any written one. The menu just makes that a choice rather than a default. There is no evidence on what any of these rules costs in practice; the hazards are logical, not measured. My guess: an explicit rule probably reduces stalemate in the domain it covers and may increase it at the edges, where the new argument is about which rule applies. And two things no rule fixes: a coercive relationship, and one partner's structural dependence on the other. Those are not problems of governance design. Nor is this a case for binding your future selves with commitment devices; that is a different argument, made elsewhere in this series.^15

## 11. Closing Day, Again

Marriage fuses two people's exposure and leaves their authority open. That gap, not a shortage of love or of talking, is what produces the particular kind of disagreement in which two people who want the same things still cannot simply decide. The merger metaphor explains the grind of putting two lives together, and fails exactly where it matters, because the one thing a merger supplies, a final say, is the one thing a marriage of equals declines to have.

Go back to the laptop, closed on the table, Friday coming. You now know some of what was in the room that the conversation never mentioned. Each of you had a fallback, and one was better. One of you could let it sit longer than the other. One of you had put the question on the table, which made the other the one who had to answer it. And one "I'm fine either way" was either a preference or the trace of a concession made long before this job existed.

The scene does not resolve, and this essay is not going to resolve it for you. What changes is the question you can ask afterwards. Not *did we decide together?* You did, in the only sense that sentence ever means. The question is *which of those things decided it, and would we have chosen that if we had seen it?*

The arrangement was built for two people. There is a decision coming that is not about the two.
