## 1. Monogamy Was a Deal Between Men

Start with a puzzle that usually gets skipped. Normative monogamy, the rule of one spouse at a time backed by law and custom, asks the most from the people with the most power. A king, a lord or a rich merchant in a polygynous society can have several wives. Under monogamy he gets one, the same as the ploughman. Why would the powerful sign up for that?

Because the alternative was expensive in a way that was easy to see. Wherever there is meaningful inequality, reproductive access tends to be unequal too, and often far more so than wealth. Walter Scheidel's survey of reproductive inequality in humans and other mammals suggests that historically the skew in men's reproductive success has outrun the skew in material wealth.[^1] A world where a few men hold several wives is also a world with a lot of men who marry late, marry badly or do not marry at all.

Those men are a problem for everyone else. A man with a wife, children and a household has something to protect and something to lose. A man with none is cheaper to recruit into violence, theft and rebellion, because defection from the social order costs him less. Joseph Henrich, Robert Boyd and Peter Richerson make this the centre of their account: normative monogamy, by suppressing competition among men for mates and shrinking the pool of unmarried men, is associated with lower rates of crime including rape, murder, assault, robbery and fraud, and with higher paternal investment and savings.[^2] Part of that link is selection, since men who are dangerous are also less likely to marry. Part of it appears to run the other way: marriage itself changes how men behave.

The mechanism needs one refinement, because the crude version has been challenged. The old story said polygyny creates a permanent army of bachelors. A 2025 study by Gaddy, Sear and Fortunato, using census data from thirty countries, found no clear positive association between how common polygyny is and the share of men who never marry.[^3] What survives that challenge, and what the response to it emphasises, is a narrower claim: polygyny *delays* marriage for lower-status men. It lengthens the window in which young men are unmarried, unanchored and competing hard for a mate. The instability comes from how long that window lasts, and monogamy shortens it.

So the deal goes like this. High-status men give up formal access to additional wives. Lower-status men get a guaranteed shot at one. In return, the lower-status men build, trade, pay taxes and fight for an order in which they now hold a stake. Richard Alexander called this "socially imposed monogamy," and Laura Betzig's comparative work on despotism, which found that concentrated political power and concentrated sexual access travel together across a large sample of societies, gives it historical weight.[^4] [^5] In Western Europe the medieval Church enforced the one-wife rule with a persistence that reached up to kings, through canon law, marriage regulation and ecclesiastical courts. Henrich's *The WEIRDest People in the World* traces how that programme reshaped European kinship and, through it, European institutions.[^6]

Henrich also has a story about *why* monogamous societies spread: they outcompeted the others. That evolutionary story is contested, and a rival account puts the weight on inheritance of wealth rather than competition between groups. This essay doesn't need to settle it. It needs only the functional claim that monogamy dampened competition among men and gave more of them a stake.

Notice who is missing from the deal. The parties are high-status men and low-status men. Women are not at the table. They are what is on it. That is an accurate description of most historical marriage systems, and it would be the whole story if the people being redistributed had no say over the terms of their own redistribution. They had some. The political settlement decided *who* got access to women. Something else, operating one person and one reputation at a time, was one of the few levers women held over *what that access cost*.

## 2. The Price of Sex Was Commitment

Put yourself in the position of a young woman in a pre-modern or early modern village. Your ways of acquiring resources on your own are few and poor. Marriage is less a romantic arrangement than the main economic institution open to you: housing, food, protection, recognised children, a legal and social claim on one man's future labour. What you control, more than anything else in the transaction, is access to yourself.

So the thing changing hands was never just sex. It was a bundle: sex plus commitment. The commitment part carried time, resources, stability, protection and acknowledged paternity. Roy Baumeister and Kathleen Vohs formalised a version of this in 2004 as "sexual economics," treating sex in heterosexual exchange as a resource women supply and men seek, traded against commitment, attention, money and status.[^7] The framework is controversial. Critics call it reductive and heteronormative, and it should be read as a theoretical lens rather than a finding. Its useful contribution is the supply-side idea: if collective norms restrict how freely sex is offered, the terms men have to meet go up. Baumeister and Vohs list slut-shaming among the tools of that supply restriction.

That leaves a harder question, and the obvious answers don't reach it. How does a price stay high when every individual seller has a reason to undercut it?

Take the one-shot case first, because it is the case cartels fear. Imagine a single encounter with no history and no future. Holding out for the full bundle risks losing the man to someone who asks for less. Asking for less wins him, at least for tonight. In a one-shot market, undercutting pays. This is the standard reason cartels are fragile. Every member gains by quietly shaving the agreed price, so the agreement survives only if cheating is detected and punished. George Stigler's classic treatment of oligopoly was built around exactly this problem of policing secret price cuts.[^8]

The village was not a one-shot market. It was the same few hundred people, for life. That changes the arithmetic.

> **Why repetition holds a price up.** In a repeated game, a player who undercuts today gains once and then pays in every later round: lost standing, worse offers, exclusion. Holding the price is stable when players care enough about those later rounds. Formally, when their discount factor (how much tomorrow's payoff is worth to them today) exceeds a threshold set by the one-time gain from cheating. Two things make the condition easier to meet. Cheating has to be **observable**, and punishment has to be **credible**, meaning the punishers themselves have a reason to carry it out. Nobody has to sign anything. Each player simply needs to expect that cheating will be seen and will cost more than it pays.

Here is the essay's central claim. It is our interpretive synthesis, built from the sources rather than stated in any one of them. **Slut-shaming worked as price maintenance.** The producers were women. The good was sex bundled with commitment. The posted price was the norm that sex comes with commitment, and in practice usually with marriage. A woman who offered sex with markedly less commitment attached was undercutting it, and other women had a direct, individual reason to make her pay. That reason wasn't primarily that they found her immoral, though moral language was the handiest vehicle for the punishment. It was that her lower price dragged on everyone else's. If the man can get it for less down the road, why would he pay full price here?

Cartel logic makes a specific, checkable prediction from this. Punishment should come from fellow sellers, not from buyers. Buyers benefit when a seller undercuts. They might sneer, and they might enjoy the discount while sneering, but they have no reason to spend anything enforcing a price that costs them. There is at least one experiment that fits this pattern. A study published in *Evolution and Human Behavior* found that men and women both held negative views of a sexually accessible woman, but only women inflicted *costly* punishment on her, meaning punishment they had to pay for themselves.[^9] Men disapproved. Women enforced.

Two points of precision keep this from becoming a caricature. First, no conspiracy is required. There was no meeting and no agreement, only millions of separate judgements, each locally rational, adding up to something that behaved like a cartel. Second, "women" here describes a tendency, not a uniform behaviour. Enforcement varied a great deal by class, religion, region and above all by how tightly people's lives were woven together. The model predicts where enforcement should be strong (dense, stable, watchful communities) and where it should be weak. It does not predict that every woman enforced it, or enforced it equally.

The frame explains the enforcement. It doesn't endorse it. A good model of OPEC is not an advertisement for OPEC.

## 3. Why Sex Was the Good That Got Priced

An attentive reader will push back here. Why sex? Women also supplied domestic labour, care and companionship, so why did the price attach so firmly to this one thing?

The first reason is who bears the cost. Robert Trivers' 1972 paper on parental investment set out the asymmetry that organises the rest: the sex that makes the larger minimum investment in each offspring will be choosier about mates, and the sex that makes the smaller one will compete harder for access.[^10] In humans the minimum for a woman is months of pregnancy, then birth, then, before modern alternatives, years of nursing. The minimum for a man can be minutes. When one party may be left holding a child and the other may not, the first has a much stronger reason to require a credible promise of support *before* the act rather than after it.

That is why the supply side of this market was female, and not as a matter of custom. The direction of the cartel follows from the direction of the risk. A woman who offered sex cheaply took on costs (a pregnancy, a child to raise, a damaged reputation) that the man who accepted the offer mostly did not. The incentive to hold the price was structurally stronger on her side.

> The direction of the cartel follows from the direction of the risk.
{: .pull}

The second reason is what sex creates. Sexual activity is associated with the release of oxytocin and with the neurochemistry of pair bonding.[^11] How far causation runs from sex to attachment in humans, rather than from attachment to sex, is not settled, and the point shouldn't be pushed further than that. It doesn't need to be. Whatever the chemistry, sex tends to generate attachment, and attachment is a liability if the other person can walk away freely. Commitment is the insurance policy against that. The bundle held together because its parts were connected. Sex created the exposure, and commitment covered it.

This is a different question from the one asked elsewhere in this series about what sex *tells* you about a person. The question here is why sex carried a price at all. The answer is about what it cost and what it created, not what it revealed.

## 4. Three Layers of Teeth

"Social sanction" sounds like a vague cloud of disapproval. It wasn't. The enforcement that held the price up had three layers, and they mattered because each one strengthened the next.

The base layer needed nobody at all: pregnancy. Before reliable contraception, sex outside the standard terms carried a substantial chance of a consequence that enforced itself. An unmarried mother faced a sharp fall in her economic position, worse marriage prospects, often little enforceable claim on the father, and a child who would carry legal and social disadvantages. No neighbour had to notice and no priest had to rule for any of this to happen. Undercutting the price was partly self-punishing. Of the three layers, this was the only one that worked with no one watching.

It also made the second layer work. Reputation can only punish what it can see, and most sexual behaviour is private. Pregnancy turned a private act into a public fact, a secret with a due date. In a small, stable community, where you would deal with the same families at market, at church and over marriage negotiations for the rest of your life, that fact followed you everywhere. This is the repeated game from the last section in practice: the shadow of the future stretched across a whole lifetime. The cost was not wounded feelings. It was a lasting change in which offers came your way and on what terms.

The third layer lowered the cost of the second. Monitoring your neighbours is tiring, and punishing them is risky. The Church took much of that work over. Confession, ecclesiastical courts, public penance and control of who could marry whom spread the burden of enforcement across the community and backed it with official authority.[^6] No single woman had to carry the full cost of policing her peers when the parish carried it for everyone.

The Church needs one caution, and it marks the edge of the model. It did not behave like a member of the cartel. It behaved more like a regulator that banned trading off the books altogether. In many times and places the institutional rule was not "sex costs commitment." It was "sex outside marriage is forbidden." The cartel frame fits best one layer down, where it explains why individual women had their own reasons to enforce a norm the institutions also imposed. It describes the incentive logic inside the regime, not the whole regime.

Two further concepts are often run together here, and they should be kept apart. The *norm*, one spouse publicly recognised, worked as what Thomas Schelling called a focal point.[^12] It was the obvious standard that everyone coordinated on, and anything else stood out as a deviation the moment it appeared. The *wedding* did a different job. It was a commitment device: a costly, public act that made the man's promise believable and protected both parties from being exploited once they had invested. The norm told everyone what the standard terms were. The ceremony was how a particular man was held to them.

Put the layers together and you get a system that was robust because it was redundant. Biology made cheating likely to show. Reputation made what showed costly. Institutions made the cost hard to escape. Each layer covered for the others' blind spots. That redundancy also explains why, once the layers started to go, they went quickly.

## 5. The Strongest Objection: This Is Misogyny With a Spreadsheet

Here is the case against everything above, put as strongly as I can. Calling slut-shaming "price maintenance" anaesthetises it. It takes women who were ostracised, disowned, sent to institutions, sometimes killed, for behaviour men were rarely punished for, and turns them into a line on a supply curve. It recasts a system of male control as a business women ran among themselves. The model says "equilibrium." The woman put out of her parents' house says something else.

The objection is right about the harm, and nothing here disputes it. Where it goes wrong is in assuming that the harm settles which mechanism produced it.

The feminist sociological account of slut-shaming, as patriarchal control of women's sexuality, explains a great deal. Men benefited from the arrangement, institutions run by men set its terms, and the double standard was real and enormous. All of that holds. But the account has a prediction problem. If the enforcement was mainly men policing women, men should be the main enforcers. The experimental evidence points the other way: men disapprove, and women pay to punish.[^9] The usual answer is that women had internalised patriarchal values. That may be true, but it explains the pattern after the fact rather than predicting it. The cartel model predicts it directly: fellow sellers enforce because they are the ones the undercutting hurts.

There is a second and sharper version of the objection, and it shouldn't be confused with the first. It concerns paternity, not power. Men investing in children need some confidence that the children are theirs, so a society that channels inheritance and protection through fathers has strong reasons to police female chastity. On this account women enforced chastity on one another because they had absorbed a norm that mainly served men's need for paternity certainty, not because it protected their own price. The experiment can't separate this from the cartel story. A woman punishing a promiscuous rival looks the same whichever motive is driving her. Telling them apart would take evidence of a different kind. Does enforcement track inheritance stakes, or does it track how directly the undercutter competes with the enforcer for the same men? We don't have that evidence cleanly. The honest position is that both mechanisms probably operated and reinforced each other. Paternity certainty explains much of the norm's specific *shape*: chastity before marriage and fidelity within it. The cartel explains why individual women had their own reasons to enforce it.

So the frames aren't competing for the same ground. Patriarchy explains who benefited and who wrote the rules. The cartel explains who did the enforcing and why it paid them to. You need both, and the cartel adds one thing the others don't: a test. If the order rested mainly on male power, it should have survived as long as male power did. Men did not lose control of parliaments, courts, pulpits or payrolls in the 1960s. If it rested substantially on women's distributed enforcement, it should give way when enforcing stops paying the enforcers, whatever men would have preferred. The next section shows which of these happened.

The model doesn't answer every question about this history. What it does is replace "why did people believe this?" with a better one: "what made enforcing it worthwhile, and what changed?"

## 6. The Pill Pulled the Only Tooth That Bit on Its Own

The oral contraceptive pill arrived in the early 1960s. Within a few decades, behaviour that had been the exception became the norm. The economists Jeremy Greenwood and Nezih Guner model the sexual revolution as an equilibrium shift driven by better contraception. They track the share of unmarried women who were sexually active rising from roughly 6 percent at the start of the twentieth century to roughly 75 percent by its end, and the fraction of a woman's life spent married falling from 0.88 in 1950 to 0.60 in 1995.[^13] The important feature of their account is what it doesn't need. It doesn't need preferences to change first. It needs the cost of sex outside marriage to fall. Change the cost and the equilibrium follows.

The cartel frame adds something to that account. The four supports of the old price weren't equally important, and the first one to go was the one that mattered most.

> **The four supports, and what each required**
>
> | Support | What it needed to work | What removed it |
> |---|---|---|
> | Pregnancy risk | Nothing. Self-enforcing | Reliable contraception |
> | Economic necessity of the bundle | Few paths to resources outside marriage | Women's rising labour-force participation |
> | Reputation | Dense, stable communities; long memory | Urbanisation and mobility |
> | Institutional sanction | Church and community authority over private life | Secularisation; civil marriage |
>
> Only the first support worked without monitoring. The other three each depended on someone watching and someone paying to punish.

The pill didn't simply knock out one of four legs. It removed the only part of the system that enforced itself. Everything that remained needed effort from enforcers, and effort from enforcers runs into the oldest problem in cartel economics: everyone wants the price held, and nobody wants to be the one who pays to hold it. Once cheating stopped punishing itself, the system came to depend on the costly and fragile layers. That is why, on this account, the erosion came fast rather than gradually.

The other supports then went in turn. As women's paid work grew, the commitment half of the bundle stopped being the only route to a decent life. When the buyer's side of the trade becomes less uniquely valuable, the seller loses some of her power to insist on it. Cities and mobility undid the repeated game. In a building of strangers, or after a move to a new city, an undercut no longer follows you, and the market starts to behave like the one-shot case from Section 2, the one cartels fear. Secularisation removed the regulator. Once the parish lost its say over private life, the person who had absorbed the monitoring costs was gone.

Now consider a woman in 1975 who would have preferred the old terms. She can hold out for the full bundle. But the men she meets have cheaper options, enforcement against those options has mostly lapsed, and holding the old price on her own doesn't raise it. It mainly loses her sales. The individually rational response is to adjust, and when enough people adjust, the old price is gone for everyone, including those who wanted to keep it. That is the most important prediction the model makes. Behaviour can change *before* values do, among people whose values haven't changed at all. Values then catch up, because they are partly endogenous to enforcement: people revise what they think is right in light of what they can no longer sustain.

The model also predicts where the old price should have lasted longest: in the places where the monitoring layers survived, such as tight religious communities and small towns with long memories. That fits the rough pattern, though the evidence for it is impressionistic, not systematic.

## 7. A Market Without a Posted Price

What replaced the cartel was not a single new price. It was a spread of prices.

Some couples still trade at the old terms: sex and serious commitment bundled early and explicitly. Some trade near the competitive floor, with little commitment attached and terms improvised. Most fall somewhere between, and nothing tells anyone which point on the spectrum they are at.

That last part matters more than it sounds. The old norm was a constraint, but it was also a coordination device. When the standard terms were known to everyone and enforced by everyone, couples didn't have to negotiate them. Both people knew the contract, more or less. In economists' language, a relationship is an incomplete contract, full of obligations nobody wrote down, and the cartel filled the gaps by default. When the default goes, every couple has to write its own contract from scratch, often without knowing what the other person thinks it says.

You know the symptoms. The "what are we?" conversation. The months of something that is clearly more than nothing and clearly less than a relationship. The suspicion that you and the other person are working from different versions of the same arrangement. These don't show that people have got worse at love. They are the coordination costs of a market with no posted price, costs the old system covered and you now pay yourself.

To be fair to both regimes, the old system's low coordination costs were partly achieved by taking the negotiation off the table. Women who couldn't exit the standard terms had no "what are we?" conversations, because they had no say in the answer. Low coordination costs and coercion can look alike from outside. Without enforcement, stigma and the severe penalties that fell on anyone whose life didn't fit the template, people now have room to set terms that suit them, and room to leave terms that don't. Both observations are true together. More freedom over the terms and less shared knowledge of the terms are the same change seen from two sides.

Greenwood and Guner sum up the arc in one sentence: "Historically, marriage was a mechanism to have safe sex, and as sex became safer, the need for marriage declined on this account."[^13] The building of the system and its collapse are the same story, read from opposite ends.

That leaves the question the model can describe but not answer. What does commitment mean once the machinery that made it the default price is gone? It now has to mean whatever two people can make it mean, one negotiation at a time.

## 8. How to Operate When Nobody Enforces the Terms

The model doesn't tell you which equilibrium to prefer. It does tell you how this market behaves, and that is enough to act on.

**Treat the reflex as a fossil, not a verdict.** The word that arrived first in the group chat came from a system in which punishing an undercutter protected your own position. That system is gone. Punishing your friend now costs you something real, her trust and your standing with her, and protects nothing, because the price it once defended is no longer being held anywhere you could hold it. When you notice the reflex, ask what it is supposed to be enforcing. Usually the answer is a cartel that doesn't exist.

**You can't hold a price alone. You can only choose who you deal with.** Making sex conditional on commitment worked as leverage when most people did the same. Done by one person in a market where most don't, it doesn't raise the price. It filters. That may still be worth doing, because a filter tells you who stays when the terms are explicit. But go in clear-eyed. You are selecting for someone who wants what you want, not extracting a concession from someone who doesn't. If you're waiting for leverage to arrive, it won't.

**Write the contract, because nobody else will.** The cartel used to supply the standard terms for free. Now the coordination cost is yours, and like most such costs it gets more expensive the longer you leave it. Ask early and in plain words what this is, whether it's exclusive and where it's heading. The conversation feels high-stakes because nothing else is covering it anymore. Two awkward minutes in week three cost less than six ambiguous months.

**If you want the old terms, live where they're still enforced.** The model's least comfortable implication is that norms now work like club memberships. They hold inside communities that still have dense ties, long memories and shared authority, such as religious congregations, tight-knit cultures and some small towns, and they barely hold anywhere else. If you want a partner pool in which commitment is the default price, you can't get it by holding out on your own in an open market. You get it by joining a group in which the enforcement layers survive, and accepting the monitoring that comes with that.

**If you're the one being asked for commitment, know what the discount cost you.** The bundle was a set of terms for her, but it was also a template for him: a ready-made path from attraction to household that nobody had to invent. When sex stopped requiring commitment, that path stopped being supplied. If you want the thing the old bundle produced, you now have to propose it and build it deliberately. Nobody is going to extract it from you, and nobody is going to hand you the blueprint either.

---

Go back to the group chat. Your friend's message is still there, the fire emoji under it, and the word you didn't type is still in the back of your mind. It came from somewhere specific: a settlement among men, a price held by women, and three layers of enforcement that made cheating visible, costly and hard to escape. Most of that system has been taken apart. The pill removed the layer that worked on its own, the city removed the long memory, and the parish lost its say. All that remains is the impulse to enforce, with nothing for it to protect.

What you write next isn't enforcement anymore. Nobody is collecting the penalty, and no price goes up because you sneered. Under the old system, she had been judged before she'd finished typing. Now the only judgement in that chat is the one you choose to make, and it says more about you than about her.
