## 1. The Moment Before It Resolves

Two dates in. The second one ended outside your door, later than either of you meant it to, with a kiss that lasted long enough to answer something. Since then you have had a photo of somebody else's dog, a joke about the bartender, and, on Sunday, "we should do Thursday." It didn't say which Thursday. It is now Wednesday night. You have opened the conversation four times today and typed nothing. At some point this week, without deciding to, you stopped replying to the person from the other app. You let a friend's offer to set you up slide past, because saying yes felt like cheating on something nobody has named. Nobody asked you to wait. You are waiting anyway.

Somewhere else, possibly tonight, someone is rereading a message you sent: "Would be fun to see you again sometime." You meant it. You also meant to leave it there for a while. There is someone else you are curious about, and keeping things warm seemed kinder than closing them. (It was also convenient.) The message cost you almost nothing to send, and you have not thought about it since Saturday.

This essay is about the distance between those two positions. It explains what became scarce when reaching people became nearly free; how the cost of an open status splits between two people, in two shares that differ in kind; why a thicker market makes holding pay for more pairs; and why the arrangement persists even though everyone in it is behaving sensibly. It also says which explanations the evidence can separate and which it cannot. It will not tell you what to do about Thursday. It ends on a harder question.

## 2. Contact Got Cheap. Attention Did Not.

Three things changed in how people meet. It is worth being precise about which three, because the usual story claims more than it can support.

**Who introduces you.** Online meeting has displaced the friend of a friend. In a nationally representative US survey, about 22% of heterosexual couples who met in 2009 had met online. By 2017 the figure was about 39%, and meeting online had overtaken meeting through friends as the most common route. Family and friends declined as introducers.[^1] The data are American and survey-based, and the headline figures are for heterosexual couples. Within those limits the shift is well measured.

**What a first contact costs.** Nobody has measured this directly, so take it as an argument from parts. Making contact used to involve several costly steps. You had to discover that someone existed, find a reason to approach them, start a conversation in the same physical space, and live with a slow and public response. Each step now costs roughly a thumb movement. Discovery is a feed, approaching is a tap, the conversation happens in text at whatever hour suits you, and the reply arrives, or doesn't, in private.

**How visible the alternatives are.** Many apps combine swipe decks, location-based matching and push notifications, and they do the same structural thing: they keep the next option in view and keep the supply refreshing. These describe what the designs do, not what they cause.

That is the whole history we rely on, and it is not a story of decline. The same shifts that made contact cheap for everyone made it much easier for some people. Same-sex couples relied more heavily on meeting online than heterosexual couples did, and so did other people in thin pools, such as middle-aged heterosexual couples.[^2] For someone whose realistic options used to fit inside one bar in one town, cheap contact was access. People have always left each other unanswered; there were letters long before there were apps. What changed is the scale and visibility of the structure, not its existence.

What did not get cheaper is attending to someone properly. In 1971, writing about organisations swamped by information, Herbert Simon put the general point in one sentence: "a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention."[^3] He was writing about organisations, not dating. The claim carries over in a narrower form. If the attention one person can meaningfully give is roughly fixed (a behavioural assumption about time and bandwidth) while the number of reachable contacts rises, the ratio of contacts to attention rises. That is an inference, and its consequence is that the binding constraint moves. The hard part used to be reaching people. Now it is resolving them: turning a contact into a yes or a no.

One piece of standard economics makes this sharper. In the search-and-matching models economists use for labour and marriage markets, a thicker market, meaning more participants and cheaper meetings, keeps each person's next option close at hand.[^4] The companion essay on partner utility and search develops that idea. Here it does one job. In a thick market your outside option is always live, and that is what makes keeping someone warm worth something to the person doing it.

Now go back to Wednesday. What you are paying is not money, and it is not quite time. It is the use of a slot. Some of your attention is parked on a situation whose status you cannot read. While it sits there, it is not going to the person you stopped answering, or to the set-up you let slide, or to a clear no that would let you move on. You did not agree to the price, and nobody will refund it.

## 3. The Optionality Tax

Give the two positions names. The person keeping a status unresolved while other options stay live is the **holder**. The person whose status is unresolved because of it is the **waiter**. These are roles, not types. Almost everyone is both, often in the same week and sometimes on the same evening. In the opening you were the waiter on Wednesday and the holder on Saturday, with two different people. The roles attach to the pairing, not the person. Plausibly, whoever has fewer good alternatives is more often the one waiting, because they have less ability to walk away. No source we found measures this.

What follows is our own account, built on a textbook externality. Here is the structure in plain terms, before any notation.

Two people are considering each other. Each has a limited amount of meaningful attention. A pairing between them can be in one of three states: held (both are engaged, neither has said yes or no), resolved together, or resolved apart. A held pairing occupies attention on both sides until it resolves.

The holder **chooses** to hold, and gets something for it: **option value**, the chance that something better turns up while this one stays warm. Holding also costs the holder something. Comparing options takes effort, holding out for the best one is tiring, there is regret about the doors that close, and sometimes there is the paralysis of too many good-enough choices. That is the **holder's share**.

The waiter did not choose the hold. Their attention is occupied, their own search is postponed, they invest in a pairing that may not be chosen, and they do the quiet labour of reading tea leaves about what "sometime" means. That is the **waiter's share**. The waiter is not helpless. They can ask where things stand, set a deadline, or leave. Each of these costs something. Asking exposes what they want and risks ending a pairing they would like to keep. Leaving gives up the chance of a yes and writes off what they have already put in. The waiter's share is what remains after they have done whichever of these is cheapest, and after netting out whatever they gain from the pairing staying alive. A waiter who is happy to be in play, and can leave for nothing, pays little. The argument is about waiters who are not in that position.

Why can't the waiter simply bargain? Because the only bargaining move available is the ask, and the holder's answer ("I'm just busy right now") is cheap talk that costs the holder nothing and leaves the status open. A price can be negotiated only for a status that has been named. The cost of asking is the same exposure that Section 5 calls the fear of going first.

Together, the two shares are the **Optionality Tax**: the cost of holding options when alternatives are cheap to keep live and someone's status is left unresolved. "Tax" is a label, not a mechanism. A real tax has a base, a rate, a payer, a collector and a transfer. Here the base is the amount of unresolved status being held, the waiter pays the share they cannot avoid, the holder pays a private one, and the collector is nobody. The formal home of the idea is what economists since Pigou have called a negative externality: a cost that falls on someone other than the person making the decision.[^5] The word stays because it is the name the concept already carries.

The two shares differ in more than who pays them.

The holder's share is real, but it comes with a condition. Choice-overload research, the source of the idea that too many options are a burden, finds little on average. One large meta-analysis of 63 experimental conditions found a mean effect close to zero, with large differences between studies.[^6] A later meta-analysis found the effect reliable under some conditions, reversed under others, and dependent on things like how hard the options are to compare and how unsure people are of their own preferences.[^7] Most of these studies are about consumer choices, not dating. So the holder's cost is reliable for some holders in some settings and close to nothing for others. It is not a curse that falls on everyone who keeps their options open.

The waiter's share is certain in kind and unmeasured in size. The waiter cannot price it into the holder's decision, because the status has not been named. If the holder says "I'm seeing other people," the waiter can decide what that arrangement is worth and act on it, and disclosed optionality is outside this definition. If the holder says nothing, there is nothing to price. Ambiguity is how the waiter's share goes unpaid.

### The wedge

Now the economics, still in words. Three quantities matter to the holder. **Option value** is what holding is worth. The **holder's cost** is what holding costs the holder. And **clearing value** is what the holder gets from resolving instead: the slot freed up for whatever comes next, and the end of the ambiguity. The alternative to holding is not doing nothing. It is resolving.

The holder keeps holding as long as option value, less the holder's own cost, beats clearing value. From the pair's point of view, holding is worth it only while option value, less the holder's cost and the waiter's, beats clearing value. Between those two thresholds is a range where holding pays the holder and costs the pair more than it returns. Inside that range every holder is doing something privately sensible, and the total is more unresolved status than anyone would choose if the whole bill were visible. The gap between private and full cost is the waiter's share. That is the wedge.

Two refinements keep the wedge honest. Option value is not pure social surplus: part of it is a transfer from whoever the holder eventually drops. And because nearly everyone is both holder and waiter, some of the waiting cost is offset by holding done to them in return. A net loss still remains, because each person's decision to hold ignores what it costs the person on the other side. Section 5 shows how that works with numbers.

The holder pays a real cost, so why doesn't that cost keep holding in check? There are four reasons.

1. **The holder's cost comes bundled with what it buys.** The holder holds because option value exceeds their own cost. The waiter's share never enters that comparison.
2. **The holder's cost is not felt as a price on any single decision.** It builds up in the background as diffuse comparison effort. Option value, by contrast, is vivid and pointed: *the other one might reply*. A diffuse cost seldom outvotes a vivid benefit.
3. **The waiter's share has no collector.** Ambiguity stops the waiter from charging it, and no one else levies it. A tax nobody collects does not change behaviour.
4. **Holding is the default.** Resolving takes an active choice and costs you the option. Not resolving requires nothing. Inertia is enough to produce too much holding, with no strategy and no bad character required. Not closing a pairing you could close counts as a choice here, even a lazy one.

The incidence account, compactly:

| Share | Who bears it | Chosen? | Enters the holder's decision? | Recoverable? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Holder's share (comparison effort, maximising, regret, paralysis) | Holder | Yes | Yes | Not applicable: borne by the holder |
| Waiter's share (attention, delayed search, investment, inference), after the waiter's cheapest way out | Waiter | The hold: no | No | No |

The table maps who pays what. It does not estimate how much.

Matching theory has its own externalities, and they are central to it. Entry and search intensity impose congestion costs on the same side of a market and thick-market effects on the other.[^4] What it does not do, in the sources we reviewed, is take the unresolved pairing as the unit, put its cost on a named counterparty, and explain why ambiguity stops that counterparty from charging for it. That specific incidence is what this account adds. Everything else it borrows.

How common is the waiter's position? The evidence on how attention is spread in online dating does not measure waiting. It does show that attention is lopsided. In a large study of one platform across four US cities, both men and women contacted people who were, on average, about 25% more desirable than themselves by the study's measure. A small number of highly desirable users received a disproportionate share of messages.[^8] On one large US dating site, about 71% of men's first messages and 56% of women's got no reply.[^9] An unanswered first message is usually a quick, silent no, so these numbers say something about where attention concentrates and little about how long anyone waits. There is a second gap. Everything cited here concerns earlier stages: matches, first messages, date requests. We found nothing on the post-date "maybe" that opens this essay. The mechanism is argued for that stage, not measured there.

### What the tax is not

The Optionality Tax is easy to confuse with ordinary uncertainty, which is everywhere in early dating and which nobody needs a new term for. Three tests keep the two apart. They are our own, not a source's.

- **The full-information test.** The holder has what they need to decide. Everything is known except the decision itself. You know who they are and what they do on Sundays; all that is missing is their answer.
- **The choice test.** The status stays open while other options are live and the holder could close it. Someone who honestly does not know yet, with nobody else in view, is not imposing the Tax.
- **The incidence test.** Someone other than the decision-maker bears the cost.

Honest not-knowing fails the first two tests. Disclosed optionality fails the third, because the waiter can now price their position. Neither is the Tax. These tests define the scope of the claim. They cannot tell you, from outside, which side of the line a particular person is on.

None of this claims to have discovered ambiguity in modern romance. Eva Illouz has written at length about emotional capitalism and the freedom to withdraw from relationships.[^10] Zygmunt Bauman's "liquid love" described bonds made light and disposable by a consumer culture.[^11] Both saw the ambiguity. What we add is the accounting: who pays which share, whether they chose it, and why one share never reaches the decision.

## 4. Why a Thicker Market Doesn't Fix It

The obvious response to all this is optimistic. More people, more contacts and more search should mean more information, faster sorting, and eventually more resolution. The waiting is just friction that a bigger market will work through.

The model says otherwise, by a route that does not depend on counting heads. The route is thickness.

**Thickness raises the option value of holding.** In a thick market the next option is always close and cheap to reach, so keeping someone warm is worth more. Recall the holder's rule from Section 3: hold when option value, less the holder's cost, beats clearing value. As option value rises, more pairs cross that line. Pairs who would have resolved in a thin market now hold. And across a stretch of those pairs, from the point where holding starts paying the holder to the point where it would pay the pair, the holding is more than anyone would choose. That stretch is where the Tax lives, and thickness pushes more pairs into it.

Two honest limits. First, push option value high enough and holding becomes worth it for the pair as well, so the Tax does not rise forever; it is an argument about a range, not a monotone. Second, thickness raises clearing value too, since a freed slot is more valuable when good options are everywhere. The claim therefore needs the option to be worth more than the slot as the market thickens. That is the model's assumption about the payoffs. It is not a finding, and a better outside option does make "no" cheaper to say. The case for the assumption is that keeping an option live is cheap and reversible, while using a freed slot takes the very attention the market is short of.

A second route is a conjecture, and a weaker one. If contacts per person rise beyond the number of pairings anyone can hold, each gets less attention, and a pairing might take longer to resolve, which would make each waiter pay for more weeks. But faster flow of alternatives can also shorten pairings. Direction depends on facts nobody has measured, so treat duration as an open question and not a result. Doubling the headcount of a market, holding each person's slots fixed, would double the number of held pairings. That is arithmetic about people, not about thickness, and it is not the argument.

One strong piece of causal evidence on what more search actually did does not test this mechanism but is compatible with it. When Tinder rolled out across US college campuses, sexual activity rose sharply and stayed up. There was little corresponding change in the formation or quality of long-term relationships, and inequality in dating outcomes rose, especially among men.[^12] This is a published study with a credible design. Its sample is college students, and it measures outcomes, not waiting. A large increase in search did not turn into a large increase in lasting relationships. That fits the thesis, but changing preferences or aims (a point we return to in Section 8) fit it equally well.

Other evidence warns against tidying this into a story where more search is simply bad. A working paper using US data finds that online dating's associations change with the era. Desktop-era online dating goes with more divorce, while mobile-era use goes with less marriage and less divorce, and who sorts with whom also differs by era.[^13] It has not yet been peer reviewed, and its results may shift. A small experiment found that giving people more search results led them to evaluate less carefully, search more and choose worse.[^14]

The closest formal relative of this argument is congestion. Studying professional matching markets, Roth and Xing showed that too many offers to evaluate in the time available is one of several pressures that push transactions earlier and earlier.[^15] Congestion is about **volume**: too much to look at. We take that mechanism as given and add three things. The first is **status**: a pairing left unresolved occupies attention on both sides. The second is **incidence**: the cost falls unevenly between holder and waiter. The third is **thickness**: the outside option that makes holding pay for the holder.

A large study of one dating site found that the couples who actually formed looked roughly like what a stable-matching procedure (Gale-Shapley) would have predicted.[^9] If the market matches people about as well as it could, where is the inefficiency? In timing and status, not in who ends up with whom. A market can produce roughly the right matches and still burn a great deal of unpriced waiting on the way there. Those two claims are compatible, and the second is the one made here.

> A market can produce roughly the right matches and still burn a great deal of unpriced waiting on the way there.

## 5. One Matrix, Three Games

So far we have looked at one holder and one waiter. Most pairings, though, are two people each deciding whether to show their hand or hold. What kind of standoff is that? It depends on how the payoffs are ordered, and different orderings call for different remedies. There are three cases.

**The dilemma.** Suppose holding is your best move whatever the other person does. If they show their hand, you get their clarity and keep your options. If they hold, at least you are not the only one stuck waiting. Then both of you hold, and both end up worse off than if you had both resolved. This is a prisoner's dilemma. It is the structure the thickness argument points to, since option value is large where the next option is always close. That is a hypothesis about the payoffs, not a finding.

**The coordination problem.** Now suppose both of you would rather resolve together than have one of you hold while the other commits. Mutual clarity beats exploiting the other's clarity. But showing your hand alone is still the worst place to be, so neither moves first. Both "both resolve" and "both hold" are stable outcomes, and the pair can get stuck in the worse one. This is a stag hunt. The trouble is not temptation but nerve.

**The waiting game.** Finally, suppose going first costs something beyond exposure: it gives away bargaining position. The first person to say "I'm in" reveals the stronger preference and can be asked for more. Then each waits for the other to speak, and whoever holds out longer gains. This is a war of attrition, and a matrix cannot draw it, because it unfolds over time. A walk-through follows the box.

The three cases are possibilities, not a forced choice. Different pairs face different payoffs, and the evidence does not say which case is most common.

> **Breakout box: the dilemma and the stag hunt**
>
> *Illustrative only; the numbers show structure, not size.*
>
> Two people each choose to **Resolve** (show your hand: say where you stand and stop keeping others warm) or **Hold**. A clear no ends the pairing for both, so it is outside the matrix; the matrix covers one side showing their hand while the other does not. Four quantities:
> **G**, the value to each person of clearing the pairing, freeing attention for whatever comes next;
> **O**, the option value of holding;
> **h**, the holder's own cost of holding;
> **w**, the waiter's cost of being held.
>
> | | Other: Resolve | Other: Hold |
> |---|---|---|
> | **You: Resolve** | G, G | −w, O−h |
> | **You: Hold** | O−h, −w | O−h−w, O−h−w |
>
> *Your payoff is listed first.* If you show your hand while they hold, you become the waiter. If both hold, each of you is a holder on your own side and a waiter on theirs.
>
> **Dilemma:** O−h > G, and O−h−w < G. Holding beats resolving whatever the other does, yet mutual holding is worse than mutual resolution.
>
> **Stag hunt:** G > O−h. Resolving beats holding against a resolver, so both-resolve and both-hold are both stable. The problem is getting to the better one.
>
> **Worked example (dilemma).** O = 8, h = 3, w = 4, G = 4.5.
>
> | | Other: Resolve | Other: Hold |
> |---|---|---|
> | **You: Resolve** | 4.5, 4.5 | −4, 5 |
> | **You: Hold** | 5, −4 | 1, 1 |
>
> Hold beats Resolve against a resolver (5 > 4.5) and against a holder (1 > −4). Both hold and get 1 each, against the 4.5 each they could have had. This is also the wedge from Section 3 with numbers: holding pays you privately because O−h = 5 beats G = 4.5, and it fails the pair because O−h−w = 1 does not.

### The war of attrition, walked through

Two people, each of whom would like this settled. Settled is worth 4 to each. Every day that neither speaks costs each of you half a point. If you both say "I'm in" on the same day, you split the 4 evenly. If you speak first, you have shown how much you want it, and the other can ask for more: you end at 3 and they end at 5. If they speak first, the reverse.

So each of you is weighing a two-point swing between going first and going second. Wait one more day and it costs half a point. It is worth it if you think there is better than a one-in-four chance the other person speaks first. Both of you reason the same way, so both wait, both pay half a point a day, and the standoff runs on. The mechanism is the price of going first, and that is why this game responds to a different fix from the dilemma. We take the underlying idea, that showing your hand first can cost bargaining position, from the companion essay "What 'Making It Official' Actually Buys,"[^16] and use only that idea here.

### What would change the game

The taxonomy matters because each case responds to a different kind of fix.

- If pairs are mostly in the **dilemma**, nothing about coordination helps. The payoffs themselves would have to change, with something making holding cost the holder more.
- If pairs are mostly in the **waiting game**, the problem is the price of going first, and the relevant framework is the bargaining logic of commitment.
- If pairs are mostly in the **coordination problem**, anything that lets both people answer at once, without either being exposed as the sole resolver, helps.
- Underneath all three sits the incidence account from Section 3. The holder's own cost does not stop overproduction, because the waiter's share never reaches the holder's decision. That holds whichever game is being played.

Call anything that makes holding cost the holder more a **charge on ambiguity**: an expiry timer, a requirement to show your status publicly, a cap on signals, a penalty on capacity for leaving conversations unanswered. For a single holder, the benchmark is simple. A charge equal to the waiter's share makes the holder's private calculation match the pair's, and too much holding stops. A smaller charge leaves some over-holding. A larger one suppresses holding that was worth doing, since some exploration is efficient and too steep a charge forces premature closure. The ideal charge equals the waiter's share, which nobody can observe or has measured. The model predicts that such a charge exists and which way it pushes, and says nothing about its size.

When both people are deciding at once, the picture gets harder. In the worked example, a charge equal to the waiter's cost (4 per period of holding) is not enough to end holding. Resolving becomes the best answer to a resolver, but holding stays the best answer to a holder. The dilemma has become a stag hunt, and the pair can still get stuck in the worse outcome. A charge that removes the temptation to hold does not by itself get both people to resolve. It turns a temptation problem into a coordination problem.

Some platform designs already work as partial charges. On Bumble, a match expires after 24 hours if nobody makes a first move. Hinge's "Your Turn" limits stop you from sending new likes while too many conversations sit unanswered on your side, and its Roses are a rationed way to signal unusual interest.[^17] Structurally, these raise the cost of leaving a pairing hanging, mostly by constraining what the holder can do next. Calling them charges is our reading of what the designs do, not a claim about why anyone built them. None of them returns anything to the waiter. They shrink the waiter's share by shortening the hold, not by transferring it.

The closest real test of rationing comes from a field experiment on a dating platform. Participants were randomly given a small or a larger stock of virtual "roses" to attach to date requests. A request with a rose was substantially more likely to be accepted, and the authors attribute the effect to the scarcity of the signal rather than to the rose drawing the eye. Roses also increased the total number of dates instead of merely reshuffling them.[^18] This is consistent with scarcity mattering to signals. A scarce, credible signal is itself information, so the experiment does not separate rationing from better information, and none was found that does so across a whole market.

If the dilemma is the right description, Garrett Hardin's commons supplies the vocabulary: each user gains from drawing on a shared resource while the cost of overuse is spread across everyone.[^19] One adjustment is needed. Most holders are not knowingly overgrazing anything. They are not aware of the waiter's share as a cost they impose, and the structure does not need them to be. The fourth reason in Section 3, inertia toward the default, produces the overuse with nobody intending it.

## 6. The Holder, the Waiter and the Platform

**The holder's ledger.** The holder's cost is reliable for some holders and negligible for others. The meta-analytic evidence on choice overload says exactly that.[^6] [^7] There is also dating-specific evidence of a different holder-side effect. Across three studies, people became more likely to reject potential partners the more profiles they viewed, with acceptance probability falling by about 27% from the first profile to the last.[^20] That is a rejection mindset, not paralysis or regret. It costs the browser, who turns down people they would earlier have accepted, and it may cost whoever happens to appear late in the queue. None of this makes the holder a villain. A holder is usually someone doing what the structure makes easy, and paying something for it.

**The waiter's ledger.** Here the evidence runs out. No study we found measures the waiter's share. Nothing in the research reviewed says how long statuses stay unresolved, how often it happens, how much attention a held pairing really occupies, or what fraction of holders mean to hold. The waiter's share is a structural claim about who pays, not a measured quantity.

**The platform: two different things.** The first is **thickness**, a property of market scale. Contact became cheap and pools became huge because the market became large and easy to join, and thickness is what produces the always-live outside option and, with it, the option value of holding. Whether any platform intended that is a separate question we make no claim about. The design features described in Section 5 work against holding at the edges, as partial charges.

The second is **designed friction**, a separate and documented matter. In 2025 Match Group agreed to pay $14 million to settle Federal Trade Commission allegations that included making subscriptions hard to cancel.[^21] That case confirms that designed exit friction exists and can be enforced against. Its object, though, is cancelling a paid subscription, not leaving a relationship status undefined. It is a different mechanism with a different remedy, and nothing in the argument leans on it.

**Who gained.** The same cheap contact that raises the option value of holding in dense markets created access in thin ones. Same-sex couples, and others whose realistic local options were few, relied heavily on meeting online.[^2] The overproduction story is about large, dense markets. In a thin market, where few good alternatives are live, option value is low and holding is rarely worth it. The dilemma framing should not be applied there.

**An adjacent pattern: serial monogamy.** The same always-live outside option may have a second effect, which we note without developing. Even when status is clear and exclusive, a valuable alternative that never goes away may shorten how far ahead each person is looking in any one relationship. The attention model supplies the precondition, a live outside option that lowers patience. It does not derive the pattern. In standard repeated-game models, lower patience makes long cooperation harder to sustain; whether that produces short, clearly exclusive commitments is a question for the companion essays on commitment.[^22] Serial monogamy can also be honest: people change, make mistakes or find better matches. This is a conjecture, not a second thesis.

## 7. The Serious Objection: Waiting Is How People Learn

The strongest objection goes at the premise. A great deal of what we call holding is just learning. Two dates is not much information. People need time to find out whether they like someone, and a person who resolves too early is guessing. If that is what is happening, the waiting is the price both people pay for information both need. The waiter's cost is real, but it is not imposed by one party on the other. Seen that way there is no overproduction, only the ordinary cost of finding out.

Much of that should be conceded. Some waiting is efficient, and the model says so: the socially best amount of holding is not zero, which is why too large a charge on ambiguity does harm. Whether waiting in dating tends to lead to better later outcomes is an open question. No study in the evidence base tests it, and it stays open.

The reply is that the definition already excludes honest learning, through the tests from Section 3. Under the full-information test, the Tax applies when everything is known except the decision, so it is not the case where the holder is still finding out who you are. Under the choice test, it requires other options kept live while the status stays open, so it is not the case where the holder simply doesn't know yet. Under the incidence test, someone other than the decision-maker bears the cost. Learning that both parties are doing together, openly, fails that test.

What the model cannot do is tell, from outside, which case a given Thursday belongs to. It is a structural map and cannot diagnose any particular person. It says that where options are cheap and statuses can stay unspoken, more holding will happen than the full costs justify. It does not say this holder is in that range.

Two other objections deserve shorter answers.

**"This is just search cost with a new name."** Matching theory has search externalities, as Section 3 says. The attention framing adds two claims it does not make. The first is the incidence structure: one person's option-keeping is another person's uncompensated attention, and ambiguity stops the second person from charging for it. The second is the thickness argument: a thicker market widens the range in which holding pays for the holder and costs the pair. If both claims fail, the framing is a relabel. Sections 3 and 4 defend them.

**"Nobody calculates any of this."** The model needs no one to do the arithmetic. It needs only that behaviour responds to costs: people hold more when holding is cheap and resolve more when it gets expensive. Designs that make holding costlier, such as expiry timers and limits on unanswered conversations, are where that could be checked. The tidy matching outcomes from Section 4 are consistent with this account. The inefficiency claimed is time spent unresolved, not the wrong couples forming.

## 8. Why Does the Market Keep Producing It?

If the Tax is real and it costs the people inside the market, why does the market keep producing it? Before answering, check the question.

### First, test the premise

"Everyone keeps buying it" may be an illusion of where you are standing. The people in the market at any moment are not a fair sample of everyone who has passed through it. They are the **stock**, not the **flow**. Those who resolved, together or apart, have left, and the ones still there are disproportionately people for whom resolution has not yet happened. A market seen from inside will always look as though resolution is hard, because the easy cases have gone. This **composition effect** could explain much of the appearance of universal stuckness without any of the mechanisms below. Testing it would take data on who leaves the market and why. None was found.

### Six candidate explanations

If the premise survives, there are six candidate explanations. They are not ranked, because nothing in the evidence ranks them. The likeliest truth is a mixture.

**The dilemma.** Each holder's option value exceeds their own cost of holding, so each holds, and the market as a whole ends up worse off. This is the commons case and the prisoner's dilemma of Section 5. *What would support it:* holders who say they would prefer a market where everyone resolved faster, and who hold anyway.

**Fear of going first.** Showing your hand first gives up bargaining position and risks being left exposed, so each waits for the other. This is the war of attrition, and the same exposure is what makes the waiter's ask costly. *What would support it:* delays that shrink when going first is made cheaper or safer.

**Regret and the pull of the present.** The holder pays a real cost but misjudges it. The regret of closing a door looms larger in advance than it turns out to be, and the cost of deciding is always easier to push to next week. This is a behavioural hypothesis, not a game. *What would support it:* holders who consistently overestimate the regret they would feel after closing off an option.

**The platform's business model.** A dating platform sells to two groups of users at once, and how it prices each side shapes how much each side uses it.[^23] Designs that earn from engagement or paid visibility could reward options held and attention sold. Economists have modelled attention brokers in advertising markets.[^24] Applying that to dating platforms is an analogy, and we make no claim about any platform's motives. *What would support it:* revenue tied to time spent in the market or to paid visibility, and holding that differs systematically between designs.

**Honest heterogeneity.** Many holders want optionality, are open about wanting it, and are not making a mistake. The cost falls mainly on people with different aims who happen to meet them. The finding that browsing many profiles shifts people toward rejection[^20] is consistent with preferences moving under exposure, but it does not test this hypothesis directly. *What would support it:* the spread of stated aims, and how often people disclose them.

**A collapsed norm.** The hypothesis is that people once relied on a shared expectation of resolving promptly, enforced by the friends and families who did the introducing, and that enforcement has thinned. Nobody can change a social norm alone, so this is the stag hunt. Whether introduced markets really did charge for ambiguity through reputation is itself a hypothesis, and a dangerously nostalgic one, since those markets imposed heavy costs of their own. *What would support it:* systematic differences in resolution between communities with different norms and different enforcement.

### The account underneath

One more account is not a rival to these but sits under the dilemma, the fear of going first and the collapsed norm: the incidence account from Section 3. The holder's own cost does not stop overproduction because it is private and borne by default, and the option value it comes with outweighs it. The waiter's share is paid after the waiter's best response and never reaches the holder's decision. *What would support it:* holders who report real costs and keep holding, and holding that persists even when the waiter's share is made visible. *What would count against it:* evidence that waiters mostly gain from the held period or can leave it at little cost, so that the share left over is close to nothing. Then there is no wedge.

### What the evidence can and cannot say

The search-abundance evidence (a large rise in search with little rise in lasting relationships[^12]) is consistent with the dilemma and the incidence account, and cannot tell them apart. It is equally consistent with honest heterogeneity. The rationing experiment[^18] is consistent with them at the level of one platform's design, and a rose is itself information. Nothing found for this essay ranks the explanations by how common they are. There is no test of regret in dating, no flow data for the composition effect, and no community-level evidence on enforcement for the collapsed norm. The incidence account's evidence is conditional on the holder's side, where the choice-overload results are mixed, and absent on the waiter's side, where nothing has been measured.

What separates the incidence account from the rest is that it is the claim search theory and choice-overload research do not make: two shares of one cost, only one of which reaches the decision. At the level of the model it names who pays which share, and it explains why a thicker market widens the range in which holding pays. One prediction it has not earned yet: that rationing or pricing attention helps more than better information. The rose experiment is consistent with it. It remains a prediction, not a finding.

### What the taxonomy lets you ask

This piece diagnoses; it does not prescribe. The mechanisms above do yield questions that tell you what you are looking at, from either side.

**If you are waiting: does this person seem not to know yet, or is something else being kept warm?** The first is learning and fails the tests. The second is the Tax. You often cannot see which from outside, and the question is worth asking yourself before you ask them.

**If you are waiting: whose attention is this occupying, and what is it not going to?** The waiter's share is paid in redirected attention: the conversation you let lapse, the set-up you declined. Naming it is the only accounting it will ever get.

**If you are holding: would you hold this if the other person could see all of it?** Disclosure turns the waiter's share into something they can price. If the arrangement only works while it stays undisclosed, the ambiguity is doing the work of not paying. Some holders will answer yes and hold anyway. That is honest optionality, and it is not the Tax.

**If you are holding: what does staying undefined cost you, and what does it cost them?** The two shares are different in kind. A holder who answers "not much" to the first half and "I have no idea" to the second has located the problem.

**About the structure: what does this setting charge for staying undefined, and who collects?** An expiry timer, a "your turn" limit, a friend group that notices, or nothing at all. The answer tells you which game you are probably in.

## 9. Who Collects

Go back to Wednesday night, and to the message about Thursday.

From inside the waiting it felt personal, a verdict being withheld. On Saturday, from the other side, sending "sometime" to someone else felt like nothing at all, which was the point. Both experiences were accurate. The "maybe" was cheap to send because the cost of sending it fell somewhere else: on a slot of attention in someone else's week, a conversation they let lapse, a set-up they declined. Nobody added up those costs, nobody collected them, and nothing sent them back to the person who could have ended them by saying yes or no.

That is the structure we have tried to show. The same changes that made reaching people almost free also made leaving a status undefined cheap, for one of the two people involved. Ambiguity is how the cost gets moved. The market produces more unresolved situations than anyone would choose with the whole bill in view, not because people are careless but because each holder is doing something sensible and the waiter's share never reaches the decision. A thicker market widens the range in which that happens. Whether a given pair is caught in a dilemma, a waiting game or a failure to coordinate decides what could help, and the evidence does not yet say which is most common. Resolution has a cost structure of its own, and the companion essay "What 'Making It Official' Actually Buys" is where that cost becomes the main subject.[^16]

So the question is not why people hold. Each of them has a reason, and most of the reasons are fine. The question is what would have to change, in the structure rather than in anyone's character, for the cost of "maybe" to reach the person who says it.
