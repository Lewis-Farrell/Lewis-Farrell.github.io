It's late, and the evening has reached the point where it could still go several ways. The second bottle is open, nobody has mentioned the time, and a hand has settled somewhere it wasn't an hour ago. Then, almost lightly, they say it: you don't have to be exclusive with me. See other people if you want. Just tell me. It sounds generous. It sounds a little too easy. You're about to answer, and you notice what your mind is actually doing. It isn't deciding whether you want anyone else. It's working out what would happen if you took them at their word: whether the warmth in the room would survive it, whether they would, whether "just tell me" means what it says. You haven't said anything yet.

Now sit in the other chair. You're the one who said it. You meant it, mostly; it is the kind of thing a relaxed, unpossessive person says, and you would like to be that person. Perhaps you chose the moment to say it, too, and now you are watching their face, and you will keep watching what they do with it this week and the weeks after. You haven't told them what you're watching for. You may not have told yourself.

This essay is about the distance between those two chairs. It explains, in order: why some rules work only while the other person doesn't know about them; the sharpest version, where the rule you state out loud is not the rule you actually run; the quieter version, where you state nothing and simply watch; what each one costs the person watching and the person being watched; why people do it, including the reasons that don't flatter them; and the questions that let you work out which of these you are in, from either chair. It is a model and a ledger of costs. No measure of how common any of this is turned up in the research behind it, and the essay says as it goes which parts are the model and which are evidence.

Keep one question in hand while the argument builds, because the essay ends on it and does not settle it for you. If a rule only works while the other person doesn't know about it, what is it measuring, and against whom? Back in that room, you still haven't answered.

## 1. A Rule Has Three Places To Live

Take the offer from that room apart and it turns out to be doing more than one job, and only one of them can be heard.

There is what you want. "I'd rather be with someone who's straight with me about where they are" is a preference said out loud, and it lets two people coordinate; even this can be voiced to position yourself as easily as to inform. There is the rule you state: *see other people if you want, just tell me.* And there is the rule you run, which is what you actually do with what you then see. Nobody hears that one. It shows up only in decisions: whether next month's plans get made, whether you keep investing, whether something starts, quietly, to be held back.

Often the three line up and there is nothing to discuss. This essay is about the two cases where they don't: the rule you run differs from the rule you state, or you run a rule and state nothing. Both are set in an early relationship where exclusivity is the default and leaving is still cheap; where one person depends on the other for housing or money, the stakes rise.

A screen is a rule with a response: some decision of yours depends on what you observe. Without the decision it is attention, which everyone pays and nobody owes an apology for. The subject is what the other person does in front of you or chooses to tell you; reading their messages or tracking where they go is outside the frame.

In 1984, two communication researchers built a typology of "secret tests", the strategies people report using to learn where an opposite-sex relationship stands, from 181 respondents.^1 That shows people describe testing where a relationship stands, in recognisable kinds. The abstract reports nothing on how often it happens, whether it works, what it costs when found, or whether anyone had a decision riding on the answer, and it does not cover either case studied here. Nothing else found for this essay fills those gaps. What follows is the essay's own model, built from standard parts of economic theory, and the text marks which is which.

## 2. The Price They Believe Is Not The Price You Charge

Strip the scene to its working parts. Two people: call them the watcher and the watched. The watcher wants to know how tempted the watched is to sleep with, or get close to, someone else, which the watched won't volunteer. The watched will, sooner or later, get a chance, and whether they take it comes down to one comparison: what they would gain against what they believe it would cost them. How tempted they are varies with who they are and with the evening.

The watcher has two broad options.

They can say the rule and its real price: *if you see someone else, I'm out.* Now the watched knows what it costs. If the price is high enough, everyone complies, both the person who would never have strayed and the person who badly wanted to. Economists call this pooling: different types doing the same thing, so that watching them tells you nothing about which type you have. You have learned that the price worked, not who is in front of you. A laboratory study of trust games reports the same shape: players behaved well when told they would be monitored, and those trusting them took that for trustworthiness.^2 Lab participants are not partners, but the logic may travel.

Or they can make the price look lower. *See other people if you want, just tell me.* Facing what looks like a small cost, the more tempted take the chance and the less tempted mostly don't bother. If temptations do not overlap, and being tempted is a stable trait rather than a mood, behaviour splits along exactly the line the watcher cared about. And if they do tell, as asked, a step that would otherwise have been hidden gets reported.

But look at what moved. The price didn't fall. The response is still in reserve (the cooled plans, the slow withdrawal, the exit), and it costs them what it always would have. What fell was their belief about the price. The information was bought by letting them be wrong about what follows.

Call the distance between what the watched believes will follow and what will actually follow the **belief gap**. It is the only term this essay coins, and the argument hangs on it: a rule that sorts the tempted from the untempted only while it is hidden works only through a belief gap, and closing the gap returns you to the announced rule. The stated rule that isn't the run rule is the sharpest case because a sentence opens the gap: one price is named, another is charged. The word is close to what is ordinarily called deceiving, but it needs no intent and works by omission as well as by statement. ("Fail", below, is the watcher's label for what the watched did after being told it was allowed.)

> The information was bought by letting them be wrong about what follows.
{: .pull}

Announcing does not always destroy the screen. Within this price model it does so only if the real response is severe enough to deter even the most tempted. If the response is mild, a sulk or a cooler week, the tempted act anyway, in the open, and saying the rule costs the watcher little. (Announcing can also ruin a test by letting people perform the standard; the model does not cover that route.) For someone who means "see other people, just tell me" entirely, there is no gap and no screen.

> **The condition, stated once (the essay's own model, built from textbook parts)**
>
> Let the watched take the chance whenever their gain exceeds the cost they believe follows.
>
> - *Announced:* everyone complies, and nothing is learned, only when the real cost is at least the largest gain the more tempted type ever feels.
> - *Hidden:* the rule sorts the two types only if the believed cost sits below the point where the more tempted start taking the chance.
> - Both can hold together only if the believed cost is below the real cost. That shortfall is the belief gap; at zero, the watched acts knowing the price.

Set this beside the textbook one-shot threat, *if this happens, I leave*, said plainly. It uses the same response openly and works by being believed: when it works it is never carried out, and teaches you nothing. The hidden rule is built to be carried out, on exactly the people who take the offer. One response, two disclosure policies: one survives being said and the other cannot.

Vertical axis: the share who take it. Two lines slope down and both reach zero at the real cost, the upper for the more tempted, the lower for the less tempted; label the real cost "Announced: everyone complies, nothing learned." ## 3. Watching In Silence Needs No Lie, And Sometimes No Gap

Now take the offer away. Nobody says anything generous; nobody says anything at all. The watched goes about their life acting on whatever they assume the consequences are, and a decision of yours quietly depends on what they do: how they handle a message from an ex, whether they mention the colleague, whether the weekend away is theirs or yours together.

This is gentler than the stated rule: no false statement is made, and because nothing is offered, nothing is induced. The belief gap is only possibly there. It exists to the extent that the watched's own guess about what follows falls below the response you hold, and if the guess is about right there is none.

Two questions sort the cases. The first is the gap: do they believe the response is smaller than it is? The second diagnoses secrecy: *would the observation measure the same thing if the other person knew?* Visible structure passes it. When something costs a person openly, like meeting each other's friends, which costs the person who is not serious more than the person who is, the wrong person finds it harder to look like the right one whether or not anyone calls it a test: the Mimicry Gap of this publication's companion essay, "Incentive-Compatible Dating".^3 Quiet watching fails the second question, since its value depends on the watched not knowing, and fails the first only where the guess falls short. Where the guess is about right, little is lost; Section 7 has an example.

From the other chair, it feels like this. You can fail an exam you didn't know you were sitting, and you can pass one, and you see neither result. What arrives instead is weather: a relationship that cools, or warms, for reasons you can't locate, judged against a standard nobody stated and nobody can therefore be asked about.

A one-sided version applies the standard to the watched and not the watcher; it measures the same thing, and only who pays changes. In the companion essay's setting, a standard you expect the other person to meet while refusing to meet it yourself is "a power play dressed up in economic language."^3 The cases accumulate without forming a ladder of badness: the one-sided version adds asymmetry to quiet watching, and the stated rule adds a false statement to hiding.

What each case teaches the watcher differs. Visible structure shows who will bear a real cost, which is the companion essay's ground. Quiet watching shows how the watched acts unprompted, though a chance that never came reads as a pass. The stated rule that isn't the run rule shows who takes the chance at that price, including some the offer itself tempted.

This classification is the essay's own; no study sorts real cases this way. Tax authorities use a middle case, announcing that returns are checked while withholding the standards for choosing which, where disclosure would seriously impair enforcement.^4 That is the authority's rationale, not evidence that concealment helps.

## 4. A Rule That Needs Their Ignorance Gets Three Things Wrong

All of this rests on the premise that hiding a check lets it see what announcing would hide. One randomised trial found for this essay tests it, far from romance: across 23 Danish public hospitals, unannounced accreditation surveys were not found better than announced ones at detecting quality problems.^5 That settles nothing about partners, but it makes the premise a hypothesis. The model adds three limits of its own, each landing on someone specific, and all resting on one assumption: that what the watched does at the offer predicts what they would do at the real price. If it reflects the evening more than the person, the screen measures the evening.

First, an offer produces some of what it measures. This limit belongs to offers alone; quiet watching induces nothing. Lowering the believed price raises the share who take the chance in both groups. A generous offer catches more of the tempted, and it also tips over people who would never have taken the chance at the real price: someone whose ordinary temptation is small but whose week has been long. Every one is a fail the screen manufactured.

> **An illustration with invented numbers**
>
> Every figure is invented to show the shape of the argument. Suppose three in ten partners are the more tempted kind. On a 0-to-10 temptation scale, the less tempted feel 0 to 6 on a given occasion and the more tempted 4 to 10, spread evenly, so the ranges overlap. Anyone whose temptation exceeds what the price feels like to them takes the chance. The real response costs 10 and announcing it deters everyone. A chance arises about half the time.
>
> - An offer that makes the price feel like 5: when a chance arises, about five in six of the more tempted take it, and one in six of the less tempted.
> - A fail raises the chance that this partner is the more tempted kind from 30% to about two in three. A pass lowers it only to about one in five (one in twelve, if a chance always arose).
> - Roughly one in three fails is a less-tempted partner the offer tipped over.
> - A cautious offer, making the price feel like 6, tips nobody over and catches two in three of the tempted when a chance arises. The offer is a trade-off.
>
> The structure survives other numbers: once temptations overlap, an offer generous enough to catch most of the tempted also produces fails of its own.

Passes are weaker evidence, and this holds for every hidden rule: a pass might mean low temptation, or that the chance never came up. Where the chance may not arise, a fail condemns more than a pass clears, as the box shows. The people hurt are the ones who would have been fine without the test.

Second, suspicion closes the gap from the other side. Someone who suspects a generous offer might be a test acts as if the real price were in force, and the surer the suspicion, the closer things drift back to universal compliance. It costs the watcher power first, since a pass stops clearing anyone, though it also removes some manufactured fails. The catch is that the dial which makes an offer work is the dial that makes it recognisable: an offer generous enough to tempt is unusual by construction, and one out of keeping with how someone otherwise behaves invites the question. From the watched chair, a too-generous offer says something about the offer before it says anything about you.

Third, the response has to be fixed before the result. A screen measures something only if what you will do on a fail is decided before you see it. Choose afterwards, according to whatever suits you that week, and the outcome tracks your convenience, not their character. Nothing can ever count against such a rule: a fail you didn't want to act on becomes a fail that didn't matter, and a pass you didn't like becomes not really a pass. With no line fixed in advance there are no error rates, not even private ones. Some people simply do not know their response until they feel it, which is the seventh reason in Section 5 and no design at all. Either way, the person hurt here is the one being measured, who carries the uncertainty about a price the other person never settled.

Put the three together and, in the model, the hidden rule has a strange property. It works only on someone who does not suspect it, so whatever else it selects for, it selects in part for not suspecting.

## 5. Seven Reasons To Hide A Rule, Not All Of Them Flattering

Why keep a rule unstated? Seven reasons are set out before any case is placed in them, so that nobody gets to classify a case after seeing where it would land. Six map onto the model's parameters; the seventh sits outside it. The list is not claimed to be exhaustive.

The flattering one comes first, and is flagged as flattering: *saying it would ruin the measurement.* Section 2 showed when that is true: within the price model, only when the real response is severe enough to deter even the most tempted. The cost sits in the same condition. If the response is that severe, whoever fails pays a severe price on a belief you let them hold, so the reason that best justifies hiding the rule is also the condition under which hiding it costs the other person most. Outside theory does not settle it. One treatment of testing and monitoring finds that the best rule depends on the agents: "Incentives need to be concentrated for those whose costs of action are high," with exact requirements announced to some and vaguer standards suiting others.^6 That is about how precisely to state a rule, not whether to hide the response, so this reason stays a hypothesis to check.

The second: *saying it isn't free.* A stated rule can read as distrust, or as an ultimatum, and change the behaviour it was meant to secure. In a laboratory game, when one player imposed a minimum requirement on the other, most of those controlled responded by doing less.^7 Imposed control is not a stated sanction, but announcing can cost something real, and an announced price can itself be coercive.

The third: *asking is a costly bid.* Stating an exclusivity rule early asks for partner-level terms on dating-level standing; it reveals how much you want it, and it risks a no. Concealment moves that risk onto the person who is now measured without knowing. One sign of this reason: you would not have minded at all if they had stated the identical rule first.

Then the reasons nobody volunteers.

The fourth: *no response was ever fixed.* The unstated rule is a free option: enforce it if that suits you, let it slide if it doesn't, and keep it unstated so the option stays out of view. After the fact, this looks exactly like the first reason.

The fifth: *avoiding a conversation that might hurt.* Here no decision depends on the observation at all; the silence is about not hearing an answer, and it spreads, so the same person often isn't saying what they want either. This is where "you may not have told yourself", from the opening, belongs: as one possibility among seven.

The sixth: *stating a rule exposes you to it.* Announce a standard and the reply is "then you too." Unsaid, it keeps your own hands free.

The seventh needs no concealment at all: *I didn't know my own price.* You meant it. "See other people" was sincere, and your response appeared only when you saw what the other person did with it. The sulk, the cooler week, the slow withdrawal arrived as news to you as well. Nothing was hidden and nobody was measuring, yet the watched acted on a price that wasn't the price. The belief gap and the loss survive; the language of measurement does not, and the model has no parameter for the case, since it takes the run rule as known to the person running it.

The seven overlap, and a mixture is likelier than any single one. Self-report is likely to lean towards the first, because it makes whoever ran the rule sound like a careful designer. Several come out as "I couldn't have said it", and introspection cannot separate them. What is offered is a taxonomy, with no ranking.

Two probes follow, to be asked before the usual "what would you have done if they'd failed?" *What does your response actually cost them?* If the honest answer is "a lot", the first reason is at least possible (the comparison is with a temptation you cannot observe), and so is a large cost landing on someone who was not told. If it is "not much, really", the first reason is gone, while the rest stand. And for the seventh: *did you ever check your forecast of your own reaction against anything?* "I'd be fine with it", said confidently and never tested, is its signature.

## 6. The Ledger: What It Costs, And Who Pays

The costs split in two columns, those that arrive whether or not anyone finds out and those that need discovery. A second split cuts across them: some belong only to offers, some to every hidden rule.

The first column is longer than people expect. Two items are the offer's alone: the fails it induced, from Section 4, and deterrence given up on purpose, since an offer works by letting people do the thing you didn't want. The rest apply to quiet watching too. Passes clear too little, because a chance that never arose looks the same as a temptation resisted. The measurement is of one occasion, which a long week distorts most. And there is the watched's loss: they acted on a price that was not the price, and whatever that costs them in time, standing or the relationship itself, they bore without having weighed it. The loss exists even if they never find out. What makes it a loss, and not ordinary ignorance, is that the watcher caused or allowed the wrong belief; in quiet watching it exists only where there is a gap.

The ledger scales with the response. When the real response is mild and costs the other person little, hiding it costs them little too: a belief off by a little, a cooler week they would have shrugged off. Most of the column shrinks, and what remains is closer to discourtesy than to harm. Concealment buys the watcher little, too, since a mild response never deterred the tempted anyway. The case against it is strongest where the response is severe, weaker where it is mild, and weakest where the guess is roughly right.

The second column is the cost of being found, and its data are thin: none turned up on how often hidden rules are discovered or what discovery does. The nearest evidence is by analogy: work on recollections of lies people uncovered^8 and a bargaining experiment in which responders rejected a liar's later offers more often once the lie was exposed.^9 Neither concerns a hidden test; the first is known here only through secondary citations, the second through its abstract. The model adds one prediction: a false stated rule, if found, should damage what the statement was meant to coordinate more than silence would, because it spends a sentence the other person had reason to trust.

Two conditions sit across both columns. A result is usable only while leaving is cheap; information you can no longer act on is information you paid for and cannot use. And the hidden rule wears out as it becomes widely believed in, since the more people suspect such offers, the more behave as if the real price were in force.

So the claim is narrower than "concealment buys better partners". A hidden rule cannot be relied on to sustain cooperation as a deterrent, since deterrence runs through the other person's belief, and whatever it supplies comes from that belief rising toward the real cost, which closes the gap. It can change who stays and what happens when it is found. Whether it improves who stays is unmeasured, and would need a valid screen, a stable trait, a net gain after the fails and an exit you can actually take. The costs are the model's too, derived and unmeasured.

Most hidden rules may never be found, which would leave the second column mostly theoretical. The first column does not wait to be found.

## 7. The Objection: Isn't Watching Just Paying Attention?

The strongest objection goes like this. *Nobody owes anyone a running commentary on their preferences. People watch each other; that is what getting to know someone is. If you notice how someone treats a waiter and you drift away because of it, you haven't run a con. You've used your judgment. If every act of attention with a consequence counts as a screen, this essay has turned ordinary prudence into a charge sheet.*

Most of that should simply be conceded. What you want is yours to disclose or keep. Attention with no decision attached is not a screen. And much attention that does carry a decision is unremarkable for a reason the model supplies: the other person's belief about the consequences is roughly right. Someone who is rude to a waiter probably already knows it counts against them. You are watching without their knowing, but there is no gap about the consequences, so little is lost. The same goes for staying alert, unannounced, to how a new partner handles being told no: watching for safety sits on the weak side of the line, and this essay has no quarrel with it.

So the line the model draws runs through two things together: a response that costs the other person something, and a belief about it that is too low and that you do not correct. It is a matter of degree, since the larger the gap, the larger the loss, and it assumes you can tell roughly what they believe; that is an analytical choice, not a fact. The stated rule that isn't the run rule is plainly over the line, because you named the price. Quiet watching crosses it only where the response is real and the other person's guess falls short. The analysis therefore points against the false stated rule, leaves quiet watching with a real response contested, and finds the case weak where there is no costly response or the guess is about right. A stricter reader might put the line at any decision taken on unspoken grounds, a looser one only at a false statement. What the objection cannot do is make the gap disappear where it exists.

One worry runs the other way: that treating hidden tests as the problem makes disclosure the clean baseline. It does not; demanding every criterion in advance can be control (Section 5, second reason). The claim concerns what a hidden rule needs, and says nothing about the innocence of a stated one.

The hardest case the model leaves open is mutual concealment. When both people run hidden rules, the analysis of one watcher facing a passive partner breaks down. Each is measuring someone who is also measuring, the "just tell me" report becomes a signal that can itself be managed, and each side's suspicion climbs. The model's erosion result predicts where that leads, as a prediction and not a finding: if both expect hidden screens, offers stop sorting and the practice defeats itself. Who pays is then no longer one-way. The essay names this case and does not model it.

## 8. Questions For Whoever Holds The Rule, And Whoever Is Held To It

These questions follow from the mechanism, except the one about applying the standard to yourself, which comes from the companion essay. They are not findings.

If you are the one running a rule, start with the boundary: do they already roughly believe what follows from what they do? If so, there is no gap, and what you have is attention. If not, ask whether it would measure the same thing if they knew. A no means the watching depends on their ignorance, and where their belief about the consequences is also wrong, you are the one holding a belief gap. Then ask whether any decision of yours is actually riding on what you see, and whether you settled the response before the result came in. If not, it is attention, which is fine, or an option you are keeping open, which is the fourth reason, or a response you did not know you had, which is the seventh. Ask what that response costs them, in plain terms, because that is what they stand to lose on a belief you let them hold. Ask whether you would accept the same standard, run the same way, applied to you; that is the companion essay's bilateral rule turned round to face you. And ask whether you could act on the result at all, or whether leaving has already become too expensive for the answer to matter, in which case you are paying for information you cannot use.

From the other chair, what you can see is the offer and what follows it. Does what came after match what the offer implied: did "just tell me" turn out to mean just tell me? What would the offer cost the person making it if you took it literally, and does that fit how they otherwise behave? Is something that has never been said still shaping how they treat you? An offer out of character is either a real change in them or a price that is not the price. The questions cannot tell you which, and a mismatch is a reason to ask, not a finding that a test is under way. What they can tell you is that you are being asked to believe a price.

## Closing

Back in that room, you still haven't answered. The calculation you were running, *what happens if I take them at their word*, was your own attempt to size the belief gap, felt from the inside: was the price you had been quoted the price you would pay? From the other chair, the same gap is something you hold rather than something you feel, and that is why it is so much easier to miss.

So return to the question the essay promised not to settle. If a rule only works while the other person doesn't know about it, what is it measuring? Their belief about the price, certainly. The evening, partly. Whether they suspected, more than the person running it would like to think. And if the response was never fixed, the preferences of the person running it. Or nothing at all, if they did not know their own price. Against whom? Against the person measured without being told: the one who failed a test they would never have set themselves, whose pass proved nothing, and who lost something by acting on a price that was never real, whether or not they ever find out. Where both run hidden rules, against each other.

The hard part is who has to answer. The person best placed to say what their own rule was for is the person least able to, because from the inside several of the seven reasons sound the same: *I couldn't have said it.* For the seventh it is simply true, and the others can sound just like it. What separates them can be seen from outside: what the response costs, whether it was fixed, and whether you would have minded the identical rule stated by them first.

And if you are the one who has been quoted a price, ask the mirror question: what would it cost them if you took them at their word, and does that match the person you know?

Take the rule you run and haven't said: not the one you would defend, but the one you would rather they never heard about. What is it measuring, and who is paying for the measurement?
