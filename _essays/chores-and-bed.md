## 1. The Last Dish

The last thing is the counter. You run the cloth along it once more than it needs, rinse it, and hang it over the tap. The dishwasher has started its first long hum. The bag for the morning is by the door, tomorrow's lunch is in the fridge with a lid on it, and the light in the hall is the only one still on. From the other room, where the lamp is already low, your partner says it is time to come to bed. The words are the ordinary ones. The way they are said is not quite ordinary, and you both know it.

There is a second, before you answer, in which a great deal happens at once. Something in you is already going back over the evening: who cooked, who dealt with the landlord's email, who noticed the bin was full and who walked past it. Or perhaps it is nothing so organised, only a tiredness that sits behind the eyes and makes the hallway look longer than it is. Or perhaps it is the opposite of both, and the sound of that voice has done something the cloth and the counter could not. You do not yet know which. Neither does the person in the other room, who has had an evening of their own: a day at a job you only half see, a phone call they took so that you would not have to, a message from a parent they have not answered yet, a list of their own they may or may not be keeping. Maybe they are counting too. Maybe they stopped counting years ago. From where you stand, drying your hands, you could not say which of you this evening has been harder on.

You have heard the explanations; everyone has. One says the dishes are foreplay, that a partner who does their share will find the bedroom warmer for it. The other runs the opposite way and is less kind: one of you is withholding, or one of you is not pulling their weight, and the bedroom is simply where it shows. Both are said with confidence, sometimes by the same people in the same week. Neither quite fits the second you are standing in. Nothing has been offered over the sink and nothing has been asked. And yet whatever you are about to say is already being shaped by the last six hours of who did what. The light is on under the door. You have not answered yet.

## 2. Three Ways To Follow

The title says that chores follow you into bed. This essay is about what "follow" can mean, and it separates three answers that are usually run together. They differ in how much each has to assume, above all in how far one person's behaviour must be made contingent on the other's, and the essay takes them in that order.

The first is spillover: what the household leaves behind changes what the bedroom costs, with no trade, no intention and nobody needing to notice. That is the claim the essay defends, and it says who it reaches and when it fails. The second is linkage: if both people can see both parts of their shared life and answer a failure in one by pulling back in the other, the two become tied. The essay states what that would require, and does not endorse it. The third is purchase: chores traded for sex at a rate. The essay sets it aside as a general description, and says exactly what would have to be true for it to hold.

Three boundary statements belong up front. Every claim here is a result in a simple model, an assumption stated as one, or a gap stated as a gap. The essay claims a mechanism and its conditions, not a frequency, and does not say how many couples the picture fits or by how much. The record of studies on housework and sexual frequency is mixed in sign and in method, cannot rank the three answers, and is not better evidence for spillover than for the rival explanations that stress, relationship quality, or reverse causality drive both outcomes; the essay returns to this in Section 13. Several of those studies draw on households with dependent children; this essay's model applies to them only under the assumption that children's load is already counted in total effort — a strong assumption, since children change every term in every channel. The scope is narrow in that sense.

Two sister essays in this series own related territory: one handles initiation and desire scripts, the other the long-run drift of wanting. This essay takes those as given and works only on what the evening's share of work can shift.

## 3. The Reading We Set Aside

Say the crude claim at its clearest. Chores are a currency; sex is what the currency buys; a partner who runs short on one is withholding the other, or being withheld from. The academic cousin of this is a theory called sexual economics, in which heterosexual interaction is modelled as a market where one side acquires sex from the other by offering resources, and the resource's value is set by social convention.[^1] The theory is contested; a line of critics argues the evidence pushes the other way, and the authors have been reported in secondary accounts as describing their theory as built to fit known facts rather than to predict them.[^2] This essay does not need to adjudicate the dispute. It cites the theory once, as the most careful version of the folk reading, and leaves it there.

What the essay rejects is not the moral posture of the theory but its mechanism. A trade needs three things to work. It needs the person on the supply side to be able to produce the thing being bought on demand, at a rate that matches the price. It needs both sides to agree on what the rate is. And it needs some way to enforce the trade that does not reduce to making a refusal painful. All three fail on the facts about desire and consent that any decent account of two people in a bedroom has to carry.

Here are the two premises the essay works with throughout, stated as premises, not as findings. **Desire is a state, not a decision.** A person's wanting, on any given night, is simply what it is: it can be moved over time by circumstances, and the household may be one of them, but not on demand and not for a price. Neither partner chooses it in the moment. **Consent is a constraint, not a variable.** A refusal is always available, and no bargain, sulking, chore, allocation rule or price enlarges the set of things a person can be made to want to give. If the first premise were wrong, desire would answer to incentives like any other supply, and prices would clear a market. If the second were wrong, refusing would carry a penalty, which is a refusal with a price. The essay holds both.

With desire as a state and consent as a constraint, the exchange frame fails because its mechanism assumes a supply that cannot be produced on demand and an enforcement that is not available. The essay returns to this later, with arithmetic, when the ranking by contingency pays off. For now, set it aside.

What replaces it is almost banal in its description. The same two people do two things together: they run a household, and they share a bed. The first does not pay the second. But the first leaves things behind that the second has to carry. Working out what those things are, and what carrying them changes, is the rest of this essay.

## 4. Two Games, Same Players

Think of an evening as two games with the same cast. The first is the household. Each of you does some mix of paid work, chores, and the quieter work of noticing what needs doing and remembering when. The division of that total load is partly a choice, partly forced by jobs or illness or the shape of the day. At the end of it, each of you is in a particular state: with some energy left or not much, with a sense of whether the arrangement was fair or not, with a position you would be in if you walked away from the whole thing. The outputs of the game are not only clean counters and folded laundry. They are also what each of you carries in your head and in your limbs when the household part of the evening is over.

The second is the bedroom. At some point, going further is on the table. Each of you arrives with a wanting that is what it is, and a willingness that depends on what going further would cost. Going further has a cost: a getting-started cost, and a rising cost per further step. Each decides how far to go, up to the point where what they still want falls below what the next step would cost them, and no further. If what they want never exceeds the getting-started cost, nothing happens at all. A refusal is always available, and the model carries no penalty for it. Who starts a sentence and whose silence is read how, and how desire changes over years, belong to other essays in this series.

The link between the two games is the thing worth naming. The household game does not pay the bedroom. Nobody hands over a chore and receives a step in return. What the household game produces is a **state**, and the bedroom game feels that state in the cost of going further. Being tireder makes the getting-started cost higher. Feeling you did more than your share makes it higher. Feeling you did less than your share can make it lower. That is state dependence, and it needs no transaction, no intention and nobody noticing. The economic name for what is passing between games is a state variable; the object the essay cares about is the **cost of giving**. Both are the two games' way of being joined in the same two bodies.

One choice the essay makes here will matter later: the unit of account is **total effort**, not household hours. Paid work, housework and the invisible load of running both go into the same pile. Two people who divide the household unevenly but earn their livings evenly, or divide both unevenly in opposite directions, can carry the same total load; and in that case, the household's uneven look is doing nothing to the bedroom. Treating two people this way, as functions with inputs and outputs, is a reduction, and the essay names it as one. A couple is more than two cost functions; the loss is the price of the clarity. The gain, if there is one, is that the ordering of three usually-run-together explanations, by how much each has to assume, becomes visible.

## 5. Slack: What You Have Left In You

The least disputed channel is also the base of the cost of giving. Time and energy are shared inputs. A person who ends the evening more depleted brings a higher getting-started cost into the bedroom. That is the slack channel: a claim that effort over a day has to come from the same body that the bedroom draws on. The essay carries this as an assumption, not a finding, and the reason follows.

Before the counterevidence, one piece of structural work matters. Because the unit of account is total effort, a couple in which one of you does more of the household work and the other does more of the paid work can arrive at the same total load, the same depletion, and the same cost. No term in the model has moved. This is what the essay means by efficient specialisation as a null: not an endorsement of any division of labour, but a derived statement that household hours alone cannot do the work the folk accounts ask of them. The couple in the lede is a spillover case only if the share of total effort is unequal against what the two of them regard as fair for this household. Everything downstream rests on that unit. And here is a prediction that would challenge the claim: if household-hours inequality predicts the cost of giving over and above a total-effort measure and a perceived-fairness measure, the model's unit is wrong.

Now the awkward part. Direct evidence for a time-and-energy mechanism from the housework-and-bed literature is not kind. In one widely cited US dataset, both wives and husbands who spent more hours in housework and paid work reported more frequent sex than those who spent fewer; the simplest hours-of-crunch story predicts the opposite.[^3] The authors read this against the time-crunch hypothesis more generally. There is a plausible third-variable reading, that the kind of person with more energy does more of everything, and the paper does not rule it out. Elsewhere, a two-week diary study of young women found longer sleep associated with higher next-day desire; the authors call their result a pilot.[^4] Neither study measures what the model models — the cost of going a step further — and no study has observed household load and the cost of giving in the same couples at the same time.

So the slack channel is a plausible assumption with direct counterevidence at the level of hours and no direct test of the thing it names. The essay states it as an assumption and uses it.

"I'm just tired" is honest, and it comes with a cost. Being tired is a state any load can move, whoever is depleted; it is the one channel appliances and help can move the other way without a conversation about fairness; and it does not, on its own, explain why identical tiredness carries a different weight in different households. That is what the next channel is for.

## 6. Resentment: The Couple's Own Norm

Two couples do the same work at the same hours and feel differently about it. That is the familiar fact the slack channel cannot touch, and it is the fact the next term is built to carry. The idea is simple: a share of total effort registers against a sense of what was fair for this household, and the gap between the two moves the cost of giving. A person doing more than their norm pays a surcharge in willingness; a person doing less than their norm, under some conditions, pays a discount in guilt.

The word "fair" is slippery enough that it is worth splitting the thing being compared into three. One version compares outcomes: a person dislikes having a worse outcome than the other, in a sense formalised in the literature on inequity-averse preferences.[^5] A second compares intentions: a person rewards or punishes according to whether the other's shortfall seems chosen or forced, in the sense of models of reciprocity that depend on reading the other's kindness.[^6] A third is the one this essay uses: a norm the couple holds, formed by their jobs and histories and expectations, that says what share of total effort each of them regards as fair for this household. **That norm is this essay's own construct.** Neither of the two sourced models contains it, and no study measures it. The essay uses it because it is the thing that explains why identical hours cost differently in different houses; it names it as a construct, not a finding.

The point of this split is not pedantry. It is that "it isn't fair" can mean three different things, and the model's prediction depends on which. The crude unit, hours against hours, is not the right one; the right one is total effort against this household's own norm, read through whether the shortfall looks chosen. Here is one small worked case to show the move, built from invented numbers that illustrate structure, not estimates of anything about real people.

| Case | What each did (total effort) | Norm | k_A (partner doing more) | Cost of giving for the other |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Symmetric, equal norm | 0.60 and 0.60 | half each | 0.56 | 0.56 |
| Unequal, equal norm | 0.80 and 0.40 | half each | 0.81 | 0.39 |
| Unequal, norm equals the share | 0.80 and 0.40 | two-thirds for the doer | 0.68 | 0.44 |
| Unequal, equal norm, shortfall read as forced | 0.80 and 0.40 | half each, but the other's shortfall is read as not chosen | 0.71 | 0.39 |

In row 3, where the norm equals the realised share, no resentment term fires; the doer's higher cost in that row versus the symmetric row is entirely the slack term. Read down the third column. The doer's cost comes out at three different numbers for the same split of work: 0.81 when the norm is an equal split and the shortfall looks chosen, 0.71 when the shortfall looks forced, 0.68 when the norm matches the realised share. The row-two to row-three gap is the resentment term; the remaining distance from the symmetric row is the slack term.

The offsetting term matters as much as the headline one. The person doing less than their own norm has a getting-started cost that falls, not rises. Guilt, or a wish to make up, is in the model as a negative term; the under-contributor's willingness goes up, not down.

The studies consistent with a fairness pathway are mixed. In a German panel, when men perceived their contribution to the household as fair, couples were reported to have more frequent sex a year later and both reported more satisfaction, in press accounts of the study; the published abstract was not retrieved.[^7] In an older US cross-sectional study, perceived household inequity went with lower marital happiness for both spouses and with higher divorce odds for wives.[^8] In a more recent US sample, unequal household labour went with lower desire, but the pathway through perceived unfairness was only weakly supported; a different pathway, through the woman perceiving her partner as dependent on her, carried more weight.[^9] All three are correlational, two know fairness only from one partner's rating, and in the German panel the fairness question bundled household and paid work — which is a strength from the essay's point of view, as press accounts of the study describe it, because it matches the total-effort unit.

These studies are consistent with a fairness pathway; they are not evidence that the resentment channel is operating. The same patterns fit rival accounts — relationship satisfaction driving both, norms driving both, reverse causation. The essay returns to this in Section 13.

"It isn't about the dishes, it's about fairness" is honest; it is also measured against a norm nobody else can see, so neither of you can be told you are wrong about which share was fair, and neither can be told you are right. The under-contributor is not thereby blameless, and the over-contributor is not thereby wronged. The model has two parameters and no villain.

## 7. Dependence: The Channel That Does Not Go Straight Through

Two forces run at once in the dependence channel, and the model cannot sign them without structure — and both points matter for what the bedroom can expect from it.

Time spent on home production is time not spent in the labour market, and in a classic household-production account that erodes the market skills a partner would walk away with.[^10] But home productivity itself is a kind of leverage: a partner good at running a home has something to walk away with, too, in the sense of a measurable value that will be lost by whoever stays alone.[^11] Whether doing more at home shrinks alternatives or grows them depends on which force dominates in a given person's circumstances. In a worked toy that packages both forces into one line, the walk-away position falls with home-production time when market skills depreciate fast and portable home productivity is low, and rises in the other direction; the sign is set by parameters the essay cannot read off the page. The usual version of the claim, that unequal domestic effort shrinks alternatives, states only one of the two forces.

Suppose for the sake of argument that doing more at home does lower the walk-away position for one partner. In the household-bargaining models this series has already used, the split between the two of you over consumption and leisure and effort shifts; the partner with a stronger walk-away position gets a better split.[^12] Under the consent premise of Section 3, the bargaining set is the household's allocation of consumption and leisure and effort; it is not the amount of giving in the bedroom. A shift in walk-away positions moves the household split, and *that* changes who is more depleted and whose share has drifted from their norm. The dependence channel reaches the bedroom through the slack and resentment channels of Sections 5 and 6, not alongside them. It is a channel into channels, with an unsigned effect of its own.

There is one place dependence could enter the bedroom directly, and the essay states it in order to rule it out. If a refusal in the bedroom carried a penalty, observed giving would rise with the refuser's dependence, even though the person's welfare from giving did not change. That is not a model of willingness; it is a model of a refusal that has a cost on it, which the consent premise excludes. The point is a reason the model measures the cost of giving and not the amount of giving: a frequency that is partly a response to the cost of refusing is not a measure of willingness.

> A frequency that is partly a response to the cost of refusing is not a measure of willingness.
{: .pull}

The brief's "three channels" survives as three terms in an account where the third is a channel into the first two. Dependence matters; it matters by changing the share and the depletion.

## 8. Near The Line: Who Is Moved, And Who Is Not

The channels of Sections 5 to 7 can be read as if any household rearrangement produces a bedroom response in any couple. The model does not say so. It says the cost of giving moves; whether the cost moving produces anything observable depends on where a person stands relative to the line between giving and not giving.

Three groups come apart. A person well above the line has wanting high enough that going further costs less than it is worth, by a comfortable margin. When the household moves their cost up by a small amount, they still go further; the household has moved a number inside them without moving what they do. A person well below the line was below the getting-started cost before any household effect, and remains below it after. The household can push the cost up, and nothing changes on the surface. Then there is the person near the line. The household moves them from one side of the getting-started cost to the other. For this group, and only for this group, a change in cost is a change in what happens.

That is a model statement about the structure of the effect, not a claim about which couples are near the threshold. Whether a given couple is near the line cannot be assessed from outside the model — the line is set by unobservable desire and cost parameters that vary by person and by evening. A world in which many people are far from the line will show a muted response to household rearrangements.

The second half of this section is the symmetry the model is built on. The cost of giving is the same function for both partners. Who has lower wanting is a statement about a different parameter from who has a higher cost. The household can raise one of your costs while lowering the other's; both can be true at once. The partner who gives less may be at a corner for a reason unrelated to the household at all, or may be at a corner because the household pushed them past the line; frequency alone will not tell them apart. There is no villain, and no victim.

A small counterintuitive detail falls out of the arithmetic. An appliance that cuts one partner's load lowers that partner's cost and also, by shrinking the other's guilt term, slightly raises the other's cost. The size is negligible at the model's illustrative parameters. The direction is a property of the model, not a finding about people, and it is in here because the implications section names a fix only if it names whose cost the fix changes.

"My partner has lower wanting; the issue is theirs, not the evening's." The model allows this. It allows the other reading too: your cost has risen past the line without the household having touched their wanting at all. Which one the model says is at work depends on which parameter moved, and neither of you can read it off the surface.

## 9. Bed Back To The House

The link is not one-way. The same state logic runs backwards: what the bedroom leaves behind can change what the household game costs in the next round.

Suppose the household game has produced an uneven split and the bedroom game has shown the gap. In the following round, the partner who gave less may respond in one of two ways: by doing more of the household work, out of some mix of guilt and goodwill, or by doing less, out of relief or something less flattering. Whether the response runs in the compensation direction or the other is not something the model can tell you; it is a parameter whose sign the essay does not assert. Both directions are available, and the essay treats them as equal possibilities.

The useful question is a condition, not a pattern. Does the resulting imbalance decay toward the household's resting point, or drift further? That depends on two things against each other: the strength of whatever is pulling the household back toward its norm (outside help, re-balancing, renegotiation), and the response in household effort to the giving gap. With a strong enough restoring force the imbalance decays toward zero over many rounds, whatever the response direction. With a weak restoring force and a compensation response larger than the stability bound (φ > 0, the under-contributor compensates by doing more chores), the imbalance can run: in simulated runs with the model's parameters, φ = −0.1 (no compensation) decays; φ = 0.05 decays slowly over about 40 rounds; φ = 0.15 and 0.3 run to a cap in under 20 rounds. These are theory on invented numbers, not findings about people; the sign of the response is not asserted.

If a given household is in the drift region and the compensation response is running, the cautionary note is this: the partner compensating by doing more at home is already the one doing more of the household work. The two moves that turn the drift are raising the restoring force (visible division, outside help, renegotiation) and understanding what the response in the next round is doing.

## 10. Linkage: When One Failure Is Met In The Other

The second of the three claims is the one most careful readers will think they want. Suppose both of you can see both parts of your shared life, and suppose each of you has a rule that answers a failure in one part by pulling back in the other. The two games are tied: the household's failure meets the bedroom's withdrawal, and the bedroom's value can make the household's cooperation easier to sustain, provided the answer is credible. No price in this; no promise to exchange.

The idea that cross-game contact can pool incentive constraints has a formal home in a theory about firms in repeated competition across markets.[^14] The literature that followed extended the idea beyond markets, with a condition: it holds in supergames where each player's valuation of what they get across games is strictly concave or submodular in material payoffs.[^15] In couple-language: the value of another good evening in one game is smaller when you are already doing well in the other — a night that matters less because everything else is going well. That is a plausible reading of satiable or risk-averse preferences, but it is a reading; the essay cannot motivate it from anything in the record. The mapping of a couple's two games onto this structure is this essay's own step, and no household result is attributed to the firms-collusion papers.

The same result also holds by a different route: when the two games carry unequal slack, a game where cooperation is easy in isolation lends support to one where it is not, even with additive payoffs. The household-specific version is that the bedroom game's value is available as a surplus the household game can draw on, without any assumption about how the two payoffs aggregate.

A withdrawal of giving costs the person doing the withdrawing the value of the giving they forgo. When the withdrawal is free — when the person was not going to give anyway, because the household already raised their cost past their desire — the response is indistinguishable from the spillover effect of Sections 5 and 6. This is the honest limit of claim 2: the data cannot tell a deliberate withdrawal from a state effect.

There is a positive description question that the "not endorsed" framing does not settle. The essay says it does not recommend strategic linkage as household policy. That is a prescriptive claim. Whether many couples are in fact in the linkage state — whether what they describe as "I stopped trying because I felt like I was always doing the dishes" is strategic linkage, spillover, or both — the essay declines to assert. The two are observationally indistinguishable, and the data cannot answer the descriptive question.

One more thing matters. The response rule the essay has described runs only one way: a household failure is met by a withdrawal of giving. The mirror version, where a refusal in the bedroom is met by a penalty at home, is the version the consent premise rules out: it is a refusal with a cost. The essay includes the first as a modelled conditional with low confidence and leaves the second as a case the model's premises exclude.

## 11. Purchase, Precisely

If chores can be done, and desire can be influenced by circumstance, and either partner can propose a trade, why can we not just say chores buy sex at a rate that works for the two of you? The honest answer begins by conceding what is right about the frame. On the interior, when a person already wanted to go further and the question is only how much effort to put into it, a payment of some kind would offset part of the effort cost; the arithmetic of trade on that stretch is not incoherent. A model with a payment on giving produces more giving than a model without one, for the group of people whose desire was positive to start with.

What breaks is the structure at the corner. The condition on consent is that a refusal is always available at no cost to the refuser; the condition on desire is that it is a state, not a decision. Together, these mean that when a person's wanting is at or below the getting-started cost on a particular evening, no payment makes them want to go further; the supply of giving at the corner is zero.

| Wanting | Payment: 0 | 0.3 | 0.6 | 1.0 | 2.0 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Below zero (not wanted) | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| Low but positive | 0 | 0 | 0.1 | 0.5 | 1.5 |
| Clearly positive | 0.1 | 0.4 | 0.7 | 1.1 | 2.1 |

Read the first row. If wanting could be chosen, that row would respond at high enough payments; the market-clearing logic of trade would elicit supply when the payment got large. Under consent as a constraint and desire as a state, the row is flat at zero. The exchange frame fails at exactly the point where a trade frame most needs to deliver. If chores could buy sex, they would buy it precisely when the person on the supply side did not want to give it; that is the row the model's premises hold at zero. This is not an arithmetic incoherence; it is a truncated supply function.

Purchase also needs a rate both sides accept, and a form of enforcement that does not reduce to making a refusal painful. These three things together — truncated supply, a missing rate, and enforcement excluded by the consent premise — give the ordering by contingency the essay has been promising since the preview. Spillover needs no contingency: the response to the household state is automatic. Linkage needs one-way contingency, plus observability and a credible withdrawal. Purchase needs two-way contingency, a rate, and supply that can be elicited by the rate. The three are ordered by how much each has to assume, and purchase has the most.

A test that separates spillover from purchase: pure state effects do not care who paid. An appliance that cuts one partner's load lowers the cost of giving with nobody paying; purchase predicts no response because no payment was made. No outsourcing design testing this prediction exists in the evidence record.

The authors of the most-cited study on housework and sexual frequency read their own result against the exchange frame. In an older US dataset, couples with more traditional divisions reported higher frequency than those with more egalitarian ones; the authors concluded this was consistent with gender display rather than with marital exchange for sex.[^16] The exchange reading of couples' behaviour has no empirical support in the record found here.

## 12. Where The Linkage Fails

Every section of the essay so far has been building up a claim; this section marks the walls.

| # | When the linkage does not hold | What makes it not hold |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | The share matches the couple's own norm | The resentment term is zero; only slack can move |
| 2 | The two games are kept in separate heads | The channels' coefficients are near zero for this couple |
| 3 | The household load is small | The slack term has little room to move |
| 4 | The arrangement is held by non-economic ties | Walk-away positions do not shift materially |
| 5 | Wanting is dominated by something else | The person is far above or far below the line |
| 6 | The arrow runs the other way, or through a third thing | Low desire reshapes who does what; stress drives both |
| 7 | Compensation hides the pattern | The under-contributor does more in the other game |
| 8 | Total effort is equal (efficient specialisation) | No term moves; the division of hours is a different unit |
| 9 | Nobody can see the other game or respond to it | The strategic version of linkage has no purchase |

Three of these are worth saying in prose. **The share matches the norm.** For a couple whose own sense of fair gets what the arrangement is giving them, the main engine of fairness is off. An unequal split in hours can be a fair split in total effort, and both of them can know it is fair without saying so. **Wanting is dominated by something else.** For a person far above the line, no household rearrangement reaches the surface of what they do; for a person far below, the same in reverse. **Efficient specialisation.** When the household work is uneven and the paid work is uneven in the opposite direction, the sum is what matters, and the sum does not move the model's terms.

The rest of the list is one line each. If the two of you keep the games apart, the channels have little room to move. If the load is small, the slack term has little to work with. If the arrangement is held by something other than what each of you would walk away with, dependence does less. If low desire is reshaping who does what, or stress elsewhere is driving both, the record cannot separate them by looking at correlations. If compensation hides the pattern by making the under-contributor do more in the other game, the loop of Section 9 is producing it. If neither of you can see what the other is doing and respond to it, the strategic version of linkage is empty.

The model treats people as functions with inputs and outputs, and that is a reduction. Every parameter is an unmeasured quantity in anyone's actual household. The intentionality reading is exogenous; how a person comes to read a shortfall as chosen or forced is not worked out here. The bedroom game has one margin and one period.

## 13. What The Data Could And Could Not Show

The record of studies on housework and sexual outcomes is mixed in sign, in period, in country, and in what it measures. In an older US survey, couples with more traditional divisions reported more frequent sex than those with more egalitarian ones.[^16] In another US dataset, both partners who spent more hours in housework and paid work had more frequent sex than those who spent fewer.[^3] In a German panel, the amount of housework men did bore no relation to the couple's sexual functioning, but perceived fairness of the man's contribution was associated with more frequent and satisfying sex, in press accounts of the study.[^7] In a US cross-sectional sample of women cohabiting with men and raising young children, unequal household labour went with lower desire; the pathway through perceived unfairness was only weakly supported, and a different pathway, through the woman perceiving her partner as dependent on her, was stronger.[^9] In a more recent set of studies on mixed-gender cohabiting couples, overall housework was not directly linked to women's desire, but larger shares of cleaning, parenting and finances were.[^17] Nearly every study is observational; most are cross-sectional; none observes both games on the same evenings.

Spillover is not better evidenced than these alternatives. It is structurally prior to the tests that would distinguish it — and that is a cost of the model, not a virtue. The mechanism is harder to disprove, not better confirmed. Any negative correlation is consistent with the spillover claim; any absence of correlation is attributed to being far from the threshold, efficient specialisation, or compartmentalisation. A theory that can absorb zero, a positive correlation, and a negative correlation is not being tested — it is being illustrated.

The sign of any aggregate correlation does not sort the three claims. The model predicts a slope whose sign depends entirely on how closely couples' norms track their realised shares. When norms follow the realised shares, the slope is near zero. When norms are unrelated to the shares, the slope is negative. The model never produces a sharply positive slope in this parametrisation; a clearly positive association in a sample needs something outside the model (role-display effects, selection into traditional arrangements, a mix of couples near different corners). The sign is not a test.

The second honest thing about the record is that frequency is a poor proxy for what the model models. The model's object is the cost of giving: what the next step would cost each person at the margin. Frequency is the joint outcome of two partners' wanting and willingness. No study measures the cost of giving directly.

What the data could in principle separate: the fairness-mechanism claim from a role-display account, by cross-country or cohort variation in norms with a fairness measure. The slack claim from the fairness claim, by an exogenous change in household load that is not also a change in perceived fairness. The strategic version of linkage from the state version, by diary data on observability and response timing. None of these designs is in the evidence record. The essay claims a mechanism and its conditions; the record neither refutes the mechanism nor confirms it.

A 2016 US study comparing data from an older period to data from 2006, in which egalitarian and conventional couples were contrasted, is reported differently in its working-paper abstract and in the authors' own press materials: in one, no significant difference; in the other, egalitarian couples with more frequent sex. The published paper would resolve it; the research dossier for this essay could not. The essay does not cite the study in either framing.

## 14. The Serious Objection

> *You have built a model in which a partner's desire is moved by the dishes and called it economics. Couples are not payoff functions; the effect is just stress; some couples are fine with unequal chores; the model is a licence to blame the partner who wants less; and nobody can measure any of the numbers that make the whole thing go.*

It is right that these are payoff functions. Treating two people as inputs and outputs is a reduction, and the model has it everywhere. The reply is not that the reduction is innocent but that the essay's claim is to a mechanism and its conditions, not to a tour of couples. It says what moves what, and under which conditions, and it does not say how often.

On "just stress": stress from the household is the slack channel, which the model names; stress from elsewhere is a rival, which the model names as a rival. The record cannot tell them apart in a correlational study. "It is just stress" is one of the things stress could be doing, and the essay's claim is that one of them has a shape worth designing around.

On "unequal chores are often fair": the model agrees. The resentment term is zero where the share matches the norm; the specialisation null says total effort is the unit, not household hours; the failure conditions in Section 12 name the case.

On "a licence to blame the lower-desire partner": the cost of giving is the same function for both partners, with the signs depending on who did more. The household can raise one partner's cost while lowering the other's; the partner who gives less may be at a corner for reasons the household did not touch, or may be at a corner because the household pushed them past the line. The model says which parameter moved, not which person is at fault.

On "chores really are foreplay for some couples": for them, the household's result is a direct input to wanting, in exactly the sense the model allows. That is an extreme case of spillover, not of purchase. Nothing is being exchanged; the household going well is simply what, for that couple, makes a step more wanted.

On "nobody can measure any of the numbers": that is also right. Every behavioural parameter is unmeasured. The sharper form of this objection is that the channels are unobservable and the threshold moves to absorb null results — a theory that absorbs every outcome is not being tested. The reply is not that the model is confirmed but that it is a map of what would have to be true for the pattern to be the pattern the model names, and that the map is more useful than a correlation plus a label. A couple reading it can run through the walls and ask which they are near. That is a different thing from folklore.

One real cost of the essay deserves conceding. A cross-game account of a household can itself make the household feel like a ledger, which is one of the things the resentment channel measures. The cost of this essay's own existence is that the frame it offers for noticing the mechanism is also the frame that can produce it. The mechanism is a conditional and not a rate; the claim is a shape, not a bill.

## 15. If The Binding Term Is...

The piece is diagnosis, not advice, and this section says so. What follows is model-conditional: if a particular term in a particular household is the one doing the work, the model names what would move it and what would not. No evidence exists on outsourcing, appliances or paid help and sexual outcomes; what the essay gives you is a shape, not a step.

| If the binding term for a couple is | The term moves with | A move that would change it (model-conditional) | A move that would not |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Slack (depletion from total effort) | the over-contributor's total load | appliances, paid help: anything that cuts the load without a transfer between the two of you | a changed reading of fairness alone, if the body is actually tired |
| Resentment (the gap against the norm) | the gap between share and the couple's own norm, and the reading of why the shortfall exists | a change in the share, or in the norm, or in the attribution; making the invisible load visible | more help, if what is wrong is the perceived gap and not the quantity of work |
| Dependence (walk-away positions) | the household split, with unsigned effect | changes in outside options or home productivity shift the split, and reach the bedroom only through the first two terms | any direct move on the bedroom, which the consent premise excludes |
| The compensation loop | the restoring force against the response in the next round | raising the restoring force: outside help, visible division, renegotiation | compensating by doing more of the same at home, if φ > 0 and the household is in the drift region |

Two things to pair with the table. A move that lowers one partner's cost may slightly raise the other's through the guilt term; a fix is a fix on a particular term in a particular partner's cost. And a fix aimed at the wrong channel can fail. More help does not repair a fairness injury: the depletion falls, the gap against the norm does not. An apology does not return lost energy: the fairness term moves, the body does not.

Making the invisible visible deserves a line of its own, as an implication, not advice. Some household work is cognitive: deciding, anticipating, monitoring, noticing when a bin has filled; the qualitative literature on cognitive labour has described it.[^18] Making a household's invisible load visible does not change the hours; what it changes is the intentionality reading. A shortfall that looks chosen when the invisible work is hidden looks forced when the work is seen. The move is on the resentment channel, through the intentionality term, not on the slack channel through the load.

A single sentence of cross-reference: the cost of the act's consequences, who bears the risk and the expenses that follow from going further, is the subject of a sister essay in this series on who pays for contraception; this essay has been about the cost of going further, not of what going further produces.

## 16. Not A Market, Not A Separate Country

What the title promised was that chores follow you into bed. What the essay has worked out is what "follow" can mean. The ranking by contingency gives you three answers, now earned. Spillover is the one that needs almost nothing: the same two people carrying the household's result into the bedroom as a state of their bodies, their reading of what was fair, and the position each would be in alone, with no trade and no intention. Linkage is the one that needs more: a way of seeing both games, a rule for responding across them, and a withdrawal that is credible enough to work and not so cheap that it cannot be told from the state effect; the essay keeps it as a modelled conditional and does not endorse it. Purchase is the one that needs most: a rate, a two-way contingency, and a supply of wanting that could be elicited by a payment; the essay rejects it as a general account, not on moral grounds, but on the shape of the supply at the corner where the premises of desire and consent bite.

The general claim the essay has been building, labelled once as its own synthesis: two things two people do together are separate games with shared state, and what one game leaves in the state is not a payment. The household and the bedroom are not two rooms in the same accounting ledger. They are also not two unrelated countries sharing a border; the first produces conditions the second plays in, without the second being a bill the first is settling.

Return to the counter. The light is still on under the door, and the cloth is still over the tap. You know now what was in the second between "come to bed" and the answer. Tiredness carried over from the whole day's work, your share of it measured against a norm only the two of you hold, a position you would be in without the arrangement that neither of you can see clearly from inside it, and a wanting you did not choose on either side of the door. The answer is still yours.

Whether what you are about to say is a response to the evening or to the person in the other room, and whether, honestly, you could tell them apart, is the harder of the two questions this essay has posed.
