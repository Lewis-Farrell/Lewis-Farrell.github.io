## 1. Before It Resolves

Neither of you has said the sentence yet, but the sentence is in the room. You know what you want. You know, roughly, what the person across from you wants. What you do not know is how much saying it will cost you, and whether the moment you stop keeping it private is the moment you stop having any say over it.

Maybe you are the one who has been waiting to hear the words. Maybe you are the one who has been quietly deciding whether to use them. Either way, there is a quality to the air that does not survive the conversation. Everything is still open. Nothing has been priced. Each of you is holding a hand neither of you is showing, and both of you half-suspect you are showing it anyway, through the thousand small accommodations that have accumulated into the shape of this evening and the next one.

Here is what this article will explain. Before any label is attached, the two of you are already playing a game with real stakes. The label you might be about to reach for ("boyfriend," "girlfriend," "partner," "we're exclusive," "I'm not seeing anyone else") does not add romance to that game, and it does not reduce the game to a transaction. It changes what leaving costs, what staying is worth, and what each of you can ask for.

First, the game you are already in. Then the four levers the label turns, and why the two most common readings of it are each right about a different problem and, in one range, pull against each other. Then a bargaining result that runs against the obvious intuition about who gains and who loses from a label that treats you both identically. Last, when converting an informal arrangement into a formal one is worth it for both of you, and when it costs you both. It will not tell you whether to go official. It will tell you what is on the table when you do.

## 2. The Game Before the Label

Before anyone asks whether you are official, you are already in a structured interaction. Every week, and in some ways every day, each of you decides how much to put in: attention, disclosure, patience, time, the slow accumulation of accommodation that is the actual substance of a relationship. The other person is making the same decision.

Think of it as a one-shot problem first. On any given day there is a gain from holding back. You keep your options open. You protect yourself against the risk that this is not what you think it is. You avoid the particular exposure of being the one who tried harder. If both of you keep protecting yourselves, you end up with a relationship neither of you would have chosen. That is the shape of the payoffs, and it takes no view about anyone's character.

Time turns the one-shot calculation into something more interesting. Once there is something worth protecting (accumulated investment, real surplus, a life that is bigger together than apart) tomorrow starts to matter in its own right. The earlier stage, when you are both still working out whether the other person is who they appear to be, runs on a different logic; the signaling essay in this series handles it.[^1] This article picks up after. You both know, more or less, what you have. The question is how to keep it.

The economist's name for where you now stand is a repeated game: you face the same choice, invest or hold back, over and over. Repetition is what makes cooperation possible. The classical result is that when the relationship has no known last day, the two of you are patient enough, and each can see what the other is doing, cooperation can be sustained at many different arrangements. It is sustained whenever the future you expect together is worth more than the gain from holding back today.[^2] When the future is worth less (because outside options have improved, because your partner has stopped reciprocating, because the whole thing feels close to over) holding back becomes tempting again. Nobody has to be doing arithmetic for this to bite.

Three different things can go wrong, and the label acts on some of them and not others.

| Problem | What it is | What stops it |
|---|---|---|
| **Leaving** | A person walks because their best alternative, after the cost of ending things, beats staying | Nothing needs to be threatened. Staying simply has to be better than leaving. |
| **Coasting, met in kind** | A person stays but stops putting in effort; the other pulls back to match, and both fall to a worse routine for good | The prospect of that worse routine. Whether either of you would really hold the line is the question the companion essay on commitment design takes up.[^3] |
| **Coasting, met with the leave-threat** | A person stays but stops putting in effort; the other says they will go | A threat to leave, which only works if the person making it is willing to carry it out |

The leave-threat means exactly that: one partner telling the other they will go unless things change. It is a different tool from matching withdrawal, and the label treats the two very differently.

One assumption underwrites all of this: each of you can see what the other is doing. Real relationships involve noise, misreading and reasonable doubt about what any silence means. Here we assume you both see straight.

## 3. What the Label Turns

Going official is not one thing. It bundles at least four levers, and they do not all point the same way. The decomposition below is this article's own construction from standard parts of game theory, not a finding from the literature, and the numbers that appear are invented to show structure rather than size. No study we found measures any of these levers in dating couples; the nearest evidence is about marriage, cohabitation and firms, and it is flagged where it appears.

**Entanglement.** The cost that lands on both of you whenever the relationship ends, whoever leaves and for whatever reason. The shared lease. The merged friend group. The holiday with each other's parents that is already booked. The social story that now has to be unspooled in public. Nobody collects this cost; it dissolves into friction, and both of you bear it.

**The conditional bond.** The cost of leaving *without cause*. A bond posted in the classical sense would be paid by the person who walks away unprovoked and handed to the person who stayed. In real relationships the destination is less clear. If you break it off over nothing, your friends may side with the person you left, which is a cost to you and not a payment to them. And "without cause" is a judgement the two of you are least placed to agree on. Real labels contain both entanglement and a bond, in proportions nobody writes down.

**Surplus.** The value of the relationship when both of you are putting in. Part of what going official may do is change that value: removing ambiguity frees up disclosure, planning and investment that declaring too early would make reckless. Whether the label raises surplus is an open hypothesis. Among couples moving in together, constraints rose while relationship quality and dedication were associated with falling.[^4] Rising constraint is not rising surplus.

**Continuation.** The weight each of you puts on tomorrow: some mixture of patience and the chance that the relationship carries on at all. A label may raise it directly, since "we are still here next month" becomes more likely to be true against most shocks. When tomorrow counts for more, every constraint below loosens.

The table shows what each lever does to each problem. Symbols are for the tables only.

| Lever | Protects against leaving | Deters coasting by matching in kind | Sharpens the leave-threat | Kills the leave-threat |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Entanglement (c) | Yes | No | Yes, up to the cliff | Yes, past the cliff |
| Conditional bond (β) | Yes | No | No | No |
| Surplus (v) | Yes | Yes | Yes | No |
| Continuation (δ) | Yes | Yes | Yes | Pushes the cliff further out |

*Direction only; sizes are not claimed.*

The two costs people lump together as "exit costs" are different things. Both protect against leaving. Only entanglement touches the leave-threat, and it touches it twice, in opposite directions.

Say Jo has stopped trying and Sam has said they will go if nothing changes. The more entangled the two are, the more that threat should frighten Jo, because Jo bears the shared cost of the ending too. But the same entanglement makes the threat harder for Sam to carry out. Leaving costs the person making the threat as well. Past a certain point (call it the cliff) Sam would lose more by going than by staying with a partner who has stopped trying, and the threat stops being believable. Two conditions matter here. The coasting has to be believed to be persistent: a one-off lapse does not make leaving credible at any level of entanglement. And Sam's alternative has to be better than the low-effort routine: if it is not, the threat is empty before any cliff is reached. Protection against leaving and discipline against coasting come apart, and they come apart on the same lever.

> Protection against leaving and discipline against coasting come apart, and they come apart on the same lever.
{: .pull}

One result limits how much the leave-threat can do, and it should be said plainly. While leaving is still credible, the threat to leave never deters coasting more than simply pulling back in kind would. At the cliff the two capacities are exactly equal, and the threat never exceeds them. Wherever a pair can match withdrawal with withdrawal, the leave-threat adds nothing. It matters for pairs who cannot or will not do that.

Five arrangements trace what happens as entanglement rises. A pair starts with a surplus of 3 each a year and a fallback of 1 each if both coast. An outside offer worth 3.6 a year appears. A moderate temptation to coast is worth 5, a strong one 9.

| Arrangement | Entanglement | Bond | Surplus | Survives the 3.6 offer? | Matching deters moderate temptation (5)? | Matching deters strong temptation (9)? | Leave-threat deters moderate temptation (5)? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| R0 Unofficial | 0 | 0 | 3 | No | Yes | No | No |
| R1 Bond only | 0 | 4 | 3 | Yes | Yes | No | No |
| R2 Some entanglement | 4 | 0 | 3 | Yes | Yes | No | Yes |
| R3 Deep entanglement | 8 | 0 | 3 | Yes, more so | Yes | No | No: cliff passed |
| R4 Entanglement and higher surplus | 4 | 0 | 3.5 | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes |

*Invented numbers; structure only. Both partners are assumed identical; in a real couple one can be past the cliff while the other is not.*

A bond alone (R1) protects against someone leaving and does nothing for coasting. Moderate entanglement (R2) does both, but only inside a window: enough that the threat bites, not so much that it lapses. Deep entanglement (R3) protects against leaving more strongly and, for a pair whose discipline was the leave-threat, no longer disciplines coasting at all. A strong temptation to coast is handled in this model by routes that do not run through entanglement: higher surplus (R4), a higher weight on tomorrow, or a harsher fallback for mutual coasting. R4 leans on the least-supported lever, since the label's effect on surplus is the open hypothesis above.

In picture form: plotted against entanglement, protection against leaving is a straight rising line. The strength of the leave-threat rises along a second line until the cliff, then drops to zero. A flat line marks what matching in kind can deter, and the leave-threat line touches it at the cliff and never goes above it.

## 4. Two Readings of What the Label Does

There are two accounts of what going official does. The bond reading says the label raises what leaving costs, so it deters leaving. The punishment reading says the label makes the leave-threat credible and so disciplines coasting.

The bond reading is exact about its own constraint. Both entanglement and the conditional bond make leaving less attractive, and no punishment is involved. Each person stays because staying, after the cost of ending things, still beats the best alternative they can see. It makes no claim about what happens when someone stays and coasts.

The punishment reading is exact in a narrower range. It holds where the pair keeps each other honest by the threat to leave and entanglement sits below the cliff, because the one who coasts would bear the shared cost of the ending too. It holds nowhere else. Where the pair matches in kind, entanglement is neutral for effort and only surplus and continuation matter. Past the cliff it fails for the reason entanglement was useful in the first place: leaving has become so expensive that nobody still here will leave over coasting.

So the readings are each right about a different constraint, and the model does not rank them. Which matters more depends on where entanglement sits relative to the cliff and on whether the pair can punish in kind. Neither has been measured.

This is where the comfort cost lives. As security rises, the price of coasting can fall, and nobody's character needs to change for that to happen. In a pair that keeps each other honest by the threat to leave, the comfort cost is not a side effect of the label. It is the same variable seen from the other constraint: entanglement protects against exit and, past the cliff, removes the one threat that was disciplining effort. In a pair that matches in kind, it is not the culprit; surplus and continuation are. There is also a second route. Entanglement can hold a low-effort pair together when staying at low effort still beats leaving, so nobody leaves and nobody improves. This is constraint without dedication, which the cohabitation evidence above is consistent with.[^4] The principal-agent essay in this series works out the effort-decay mechanism.[^5] What the model here adds is the price of coasting as a function of the levers, and where it breaks. It does not say that every change after commitment is bad faith. A pair that stops trying at the margin is not necessarily cheating; the model says only what disciplines them.

A separate question is why a label works at all, and there are three candidate answers that make different predictions.

The **device account** says the label changes payoffs. Exit costs, surplus and the weight on tomorrow shift, and cooperation is sustained or destabilised depending on where the levers land. The formal machinery in this article is device-account machinery; the other two are rivals it does not model.

The **signal account** says going official is costly enough that only people who value the relationship's future will accept it, so the label reveals something about type rather than binding behaviour. It predicts that effects should concentrate where the cost differs sharply between more and less committed people, and that a costless label should do nothing. We found one formal test of device against signal against exogenous payoff, and it concerns legal marriage in the United States; the authors emphasise the commitment-device reading.[^6]

The **focal-point account** says the label coordinates expectations on a cooperative outcome without changing payoffs at all. It predicts that a costless label should work as well as a costly one, and immediately. We found no formal treatment of it for relationship labels and no test.

What would tell them apart? If coasting falls as entanglement rises to the cliff and then jumps back up past it (an inverted U in effort: rising, then collapsing), the leave-threat is doing the work. If coasting is flat or rising in entanglement, the bond reading fits. If effort tracks surplus and expected duration but not entanglement, the matching-in-kind or surplus channel is doing it. If the label works equally well whatever it costs, and at once, the focal-point account is strongest. How exit responds to a better outside offer will not discriminate, since all the readings agree that it falls. None of these tests has been run on dating-stage couples that we could find.

## 5. Who Gets What

The label affects whether cooperation can be sustained. It also affects how the value of that cooperation is split between you. These are different questions with different answers.

The split depends on two things: how patient each of you is, and what each of you can walk away to. The classical results are Nash's (1950) and Rubinstein's (1982).[[^7]][[^8]]

**Patience.** The person who minds waiting less gets more. In Rubinstein's model two people make alternating offers over a surplus, each discounting the future, and the equilibrium is immediate agreement in which the more patient party takes the larger share. Willard Waller put forward a similar idea in the 1930s as the principle of least interest: the partner who cares less about the relationship's continuation has leverage over the one who cares more.[^9] The two echo each other. Treating patience as the bargaining counterpart of Waller's "interest" is this article's own mapping, not an established equivalence.

**Walk-away options.** Here the result is sharper than intuition. Suppose, for a moment, that value can be traded between you without friction, so a gain to one is a loss to the other. Nash's formula then gives each person their walk-away value plus half of whatever is left. If both walk-aways fall by the same amount, as they would under a symmetric cost that is pure deadweight, the split does not move. Both of you pay more to leave, and the distribution between you changes by exactly nothing.

That runs against the natural reading, which says a label that raises exit costs for both must weaken whoever is more locked in. Under Nash, only a *difference* in the change moves the split. A sharper version comes from the strategic bargaining literature: an outside option moves the split only when it binds, meaning it is better than what bargaining would give you anyway.[^10] If your option is weak, it does nothing. If it is strong, it sets your payoff and your partner gets the rest.

Here is that in numbers. Say the relationship produces 6 a year between Sam and Jo. If neither had any alternative and they were equally patient, they would split it 3 and 3. Sam has a good alternative worth 3.2 a year. Jo's is worth 2.0.

*Under Nash,* each gets their alternative plus half of what is left: Sam 3.2, Jo 2.0, leaving 0.8, so Sam gets 3.6 and Jo 2.4.

*Under the outside-option rule,* Sam's 3.2 beats the even split of 3, so it binds: Sam will not take less than 3.2. Jo's 2.0 is below 3 and changes nothing. So Sam gets 3.2 and Jo gets the rest, 2.8.

Now the label arrives and treats them identically. Each takes on an entanglement cost of 4. At the weight they place on tomorrow, a cost of 4 on ending lowers each person's yearly walk-away by 0.8. Sam's falls to 2.4 and Jo's to 1.2.

*Under Nash,* Sam gets 2.4 plus half of 2.4, which is 3.6, and Jo gets 1.2 plus half of 2.4, which is 2.4. Nothing has moved.

*Under the outside-option rule,* Sam's 2.4 is now below the even split. It no longer binds, and the split drifts to 3 and 3. Sam has lost 0.2 and Jo has gained 0.2, from a label that treated both of them the same.

| | Walk-aways (Sam, Jo) | Nash split | Outside-option split |
|---|---|---|---|
| Before the label | 3.2, 2.0 | 3.6, 2.4 | 3.2, 2.8 |
| After a symmetric label | 2.4, 1.2 | 3.6, 2.4 | 3.0, 3.0 |

*Invented numbers; structure only.*

The two rules disagree about who resists the label. Nash says neither has a structural reason to. The outside-option rule says Sam does, because Sam's stronger alternative is the one the label dissolves. The experimental evidence on outside options is on the outside-option side, but that experiment is on strangers bargaining over money, and we do not pick a winner here; the test for couples has not been run.

Three assumptions sit under these numbers. Utility must be transferable, so the 6 can be divided any way without losing value; intimate payoffs are not cash, and the comparison across people is illustrative only. The baseline is an equal split, which holds when the two are equally patient; with unequal patience the baseline moves and so does the threshold at which an option binds. And the option is assumed to be exercised in place of a counter-offer; if it can be used at a different point in the bargaining, the threshold shifts. Change any of the three and the size of the contrast changes. The structure, that equal costs leave Nash unmoved and can dissolve a binding option, does not.

Bargaining does not pick any point it likes. Cooperation survives only at splits where neither person would rather leave and neither would rather grab the gain from coasting. Each person therefore has a floor: the least they will accept. For Sam the floor is the walk-away, 3.2. For Jo it is the lowest share at which Jo still prefers to keep investing over coasting and being met in kind, which in this example is 2.25, higher than Jo's walk-away. Sam's share can therefore run from 3.2 up to 3.75 (6 minus Jo's floor), and any split in that range can be sustained. This is the folk theorem made concrete: a whole range of splits is available, and nothing in the repeated game picks among them. The label changes the range. Sam's floor falls from 3.2 to 2.4, so Sam can be held to a smaller share before leaving becomes worth it, while the ceiling stays at 3.75. And if a bargaining rule would hand Sam more than 3.75, Jo would rather coast, so the outcome stops at the ceiling; the repeated game constrains the bargain from outside.

**Why revealing what you want can cost you.** If each of you knew exactly what the other would pay to stay, the results above would be exact. Once something is hidden, there is a different effect.

Suppose staying with this person is worth either 10 a year to you over your alternatives, or only 4, and they think there is a 30 percent chance it is 10. They can make one take-it-or-leave-it ask. Asking 4 is safe: you accept either way. Asking 10 only works if you really are the high type. At 30 percent they ask 4, and if you are the high type you keep a rent of 6 because they did not know. If they learn the truth, they ask 10 and you keep nothing. Revealing a high valuation is a gift to the person who can ask for more. This holds in a deliberately simple setup with one ask. If they cannot commit to a single ask and can try again after a delay, the rent shrinks (from 6 to 4.8 in the same example) but survives.

The same setup has an efficiency cost. If they believe it is 60 percent likely that you are the high type, the gamble on 10 pays for them, and the low type is refused and the relationship never forms. A quarter of the joint value disappears.[^11] The exclusion is a real loss and not a transfer.

The person with a low want can say the same words: "I'm not that fussed, honestly." When anyone with an incentive to mimic would say it, hearing it tells the listener little. This is the cheap-talk problem: a statement that costs nothing to make cannot reliably carry information when the speaker gains from being believed. What can carry information is a costly action: delay, refusal of the label, a visible alternative, the kind of behaviour that someone with a genuine outside option can afford and someone without one cannot. One candidate reading of what people call strategic ambiguity is this. The person who delays the conversation may be buying information before they lock in, or may be signalling the strength of their position. From the outside the two look identical, and the person doing it may not be able to tell either. A close formal cousin is bargaining in which patience, not valuation, is concealed.[^12]

**Two divergences that need to be kept apart.** People talk about the conflict between what is good for the two of you and what is good for me as one thing. It is two.

The *distributional* divergence is not a loss. Wherever more than one split can be sustained, and for patient enough players with observable actions the folk theorem says that is the normal case, any gain to one person along the range is a loss to the other. Nothing is destroyed; there is a fight over the share of a fixed total.

The *efficiency* divergence is different. It arises when strategic behaviour shrinks the total. Private information, as above, produces deals that fail to form or form only after costly delay. Hold-up discourages investments that would benefit both of you. Lock-in keeps people in matches that were worth leaving. These are losses, and the next section takes up the first one that bears on the label.

The division of chores is a distributional conflict. The investment one of you did not make because you knew you would not recoup it is an efficiency loss. They have different solutions.

## 6. When Converting Pays

You have built something in this relationship that is worth a lot to the two of you and close to nothing anywhere else. The time, the mutual calibration, the knowledge that took a year to accumulate about how the other person needs to be told something difficult. It is relationship-specific. You cannot carry it with you.

That specificity creates a structural problem. Once you have sunk something that only pays off inside this relationship, your position in any negotiation over who gets what weakens. You cannot credibly threaten to walk out and start over without paying the full cost of starting over. The partner who invested less, or whose accumulated understanding is more portable, holds the stronger walk-away inside the bargain.

This is the hold-up problem. The classical example is between firms: a supplier builds equipment that is useful only to one buyer, and afterwards the buyer can renegotiate the price downward because the supplier cannot credibly walk away.[^13] By analogy, the same dynamic lands on anyone in a relationship who has invested more than their partner in something that will not survive an ending: the career accommodations, the emotional labour, the geographic move, the trust that took years to earn and would take years to reproduce.

Two cases matter. Take an investment that costs the investor 4 and adds 10 a year to what the pair can produce, worth nothing outside this relationship.

**Case I: the partner's alternative would leave the investor short.** Say the partner's walk-away is 7 of the 10. The investor would get 3, less than the 4 it cost, so the investment goes unmade and the pair loses the 6 it would have added. The investment pays whenever the partner's walk-away is at or below the gain minus the cost, here 6, and 7 is above it. Going official, by raising what leaving costs the partner, can lower the partner's walk-away to 5, an even split. The investor then gets 5 and invests. The label has to cost less than the 6 it unlocks. Suppose it costs the pair 0.6 in lost freedom to leave a match that turns out badly, and 1.0 in lost pressure to keep trying. The pair gains 4.4 jointly. If the two share the costs evenly, both come out ahead, the partner because the investment now exists.

**Case II: the partner's alternative is already weak.** Say it is worth 3. The investor already gets 5 and invests anyway. The label changes no split and costs 1.6. If the costs are shared evenly, both lose.

There is a case in between. At a partner's walk-away of 5.5 the investor already invests, the label shifts 0.5 toward the investor, and the pair still loses 1.6 overall. The partner is worse off by 1.3 and the investor by 0.3. A conversion can be a gain for the pair and a loss for one of them, depending on who bears the costs.

The two bargaining rules diverge sharply on Case I. Under Nash, an equal-cost label does not relieve hold-up at all: the symmetric cost leaves the split unchanged and the investor is still short. Under the outside-option rule it can, by dissolving the partner's binding option. Whether going official does the job hold-up logic assigns to it depends on which bargaining account is right. A formal qualification also applies: if the pair can renegotiate after the investment, a label may not be renegotiation-proof, and the relief may be partial.[^13]

The closest field evidence for hold-up logic in couples is from divorce law. A study of US states that adopted unilateral divorce found that making divorce easier reduced investment in several kinds of marriage-specific capital, including spouses' education, children and household specialisation.[^14] That is a change in exit cost at the level of legal regimes for married couples, the evidence on the estimates is contested, and it is not a dating-stage label.

One more cost deserves plain statement. While the arrangement is informal, exit is cheap. If the match turns out worse than expected, because character shows differently once tested or circumstances change, leaving costs less. Going official forecloses some of that. The likelier a bad turn, the higher the price of formalising. This is a cost of converting, not a reason against it.[^15]

## 7. The Objection

The strongest objection to all of this is not that the model is wrong about relationships. It is that it is wrong about people.

"Nobody actually does this," the objection goes. "No one in the moment of asking 'are we official?' is solving a participation constraint or thinking about a credibility cliff. People love each other, or they do not. They decide on feeling, or on fear, not on cost structures."

That is right, and it misses the point. The model describes a cost structure, and a cost structure operates whether or not anyone does the arithmetic. If leaving genuinely costs more after going official, the rate at which people leave should change, not because they ran a calculation but because the incentive moved. In laboratory repeated games among strangers, cooperation moves in the direction the theory predicts when the continuation probability rises, though being an equilibrium does not guarantee that most subjects cooperate.[^16] The subjects were not solving equations, which is our inference and not something those studies test. This evidence supports the exit-protection channel: behaviour responds to what leaving costs. The cliff, a non-monotone, credibility-dependent effect on coasting, is pure model. Nobody has tested it.

Two shorter objections follow.

*"Love is not bargaining."* The split between two people is almost never negotiated aloud. It is settled by who accommodates whom, who gives up what, whose preferences quietly dominate over time, whose weekends get used for whose family. That is the bargain, conducted implicitly. A strand of household economics has treated the distribution inside a couple as a bargained outcome for decades, using walk-aways and threat points, not romantic feeling, to explain why sharing shifts when outside rules change.[^17] The model keeps the mechanism in front. It says what the incentives are. It does not say who is good or bad, or that anyone deploys them on purpose.

*"Going official changes feelings."* That is correct. The model treats the label as changing payoffs, what each of you gets, and not preferences, what each of you values. Commitment is associated with how people represent themselves and their partners: more shared pronouns, more overlap between self and partner, more of identity organised around the relationship.[^18] "Payoffs not preferences" is a convenience and not a fact. What the model still delivers is the cost-structure account of what is happening before preferences reorganise, and the structural prediction about who is exposed to what. Whether commitment also rewrites the preferences by which it would be judged stays open.

## 8. Practical Questions

This article is diagnostic, not prescriptive. The model does not tell you whether to go official. It yields five classification questions, each tied to a mechanism above.

**Which problem is the label acting on, leaving or coasting?** Is the worry that someone will go, or that someone has stopped trying while staying? These need different tools. Entanglement addresses the first. For the second, it helps only below the cliff and only if the pair's discipline is the threat to leave; past the cliff it can disable the one threat that had been working.

**Does the cost of ending fall on both of you, or only on whoever leaves?** Entanglement is deadweight both bear. A conditional bond falls on whoever walks away without cause. If what you are building looks mostly like a conditional bond (reputational, and costly mainly to the leaver) the cliff does not appear in the same way. If it looks mostly like entanglement (shared life, merged friends, a public identity whose unspooling would hit both of you) the cliff is real.

**Whose outside option is currently the strong one, and would a symmetric label dissolve it?** If the outside-option reading is right, the person whose option binds is the one with reason to be ambivalent about a symmetric label, because it dissolves the option that was setting their share. That is a structural prediction and nothing more. Hesitation has many causes the model does not cover, and the prediction is untested.

**Is what has been built specific to this relationship, and is the investor's return currently blocked by the partner's walk-away?** This is the hold-up test. If both answers are yes, the label may protect an investment that would otherwise go unmade or unrecouped. If either answer is no, the label costs without paying.

**What does the label do to both your walk-aways, and can you tell whether it is wanted for that?** A label that protects you against your partner leaving also lowers your partner's walk-away, and yours. The structure cannot show intent, and from the inside the two can be hard to separate. The question is whether you can tell them apart in yours.

These questions classify the situation. They do not say what to do next. What makes any of this credible, and what a pair can do when it is not, is the subject of the companion essay on commitment design.

---

## 9. Who Was It For

The repeated game makes a whole set of splits sustainable. It does not choose. Which split gets chosen depends on each person's patience and on any walk-away option good enough to bind, and it lasts only as long as the threats behind it stay credible.

That last clause belongs to the companion essay on commitment design. What this article has suggested, in a stylised model, is narrower. The label protects the relationship against exit, and it does so without punishing anyone: it raises what leaving costs, and it lowers both your walk-aways by the same amount. Whether it also protects against the thing most people worry about more, the slow withdrawal of effort inside a relationship that technically continues, depends on the levers. A label that sharpens the threat to leave below the cliff can disable it above, and a pair that matches withdrawal with withdrawal never needed the threat at all.

Return to the room you started in. Neither of you has said the sentence yet, but now there is something behind it that was not there before: a sense of what is on the table. The label treats you the same. Its effects on you may not be the same. If the outside-option reading is right, and one of you had the stronger alternative, that person's share can fall while the other's rises. If the Nash reading is right, nothing moves between you. The model cannot say which holds in your relationship.

What it can do is sharpen the question you were already asking. The label protects each of you against the other leaving, and it narrows what each of you could carry away if you did. So before the sentence is said: which of you does it bind more, and which of you does it protect more?
