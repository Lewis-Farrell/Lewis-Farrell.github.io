## 1. The Moment, Then The Puzzle

The date is going well — better than well. There's a third drink on the table, and eye contact has shifted from polite and easy to charged. It is your first date, and you can feel yourself getting a little smitten: not just with how they look, but with their charm, their potential, the flattering possibility that this person might actually make your life larger and more interesting. Then something small happens — a touch that lingers, a kiss that almost turns into another one — and suddenly you are not just enjoying yourself. You are deciding whether to go home with them. Whether to have sex tonight. And if you do, what that will say about you — to them, and about them, to you.

That is the strange pressure inside modern dating. Every move feels like a message, and sex feels like one of the loudest. This article is about why people treat the decision to sleep with someone as a readout of that person's intentions — why they are often wrong to — and how to judge seriousness using patterns of behaviour rather than one loaded evening. You have probably been told, in one form or another, that if you are serious about someone you should wait. But wait for what, exactly? To prove restraint? To screen out the people who would leave after getting what they came for? To look like the kind of person worth pursuing?

Economists have a name for this kind of situation. It is a signaling game: one person knows more than the other about what they want, how serious they are, and how long they plan to stick around, while the other person has to infer all that from visible behaviour. Early dating is full of these tiny acts of mutual interpretation — who texts first, who holds back, who pushes forward, who makes the expensive gesture, who keeps their options open. The useful question is not "what does sleeping with someone early say about your character?" It is: under what conditions does sexual timing actually tell you something reliable about a person's intentions?

## 2. Hidden Type, Costly Signals, And Why Words Alone Are Weak

The core problem in early dating is not attraction. It is information. You are sitting across from someone whose intentions are invisible. They might want to build something with you. They might want a warm body for the winter. They might want to feel chosen for an evening without any of the weight that comes after. You cannot observe their type directly. All you can do is watch what they do and try to infer.

Early dating is an information problem, and the economist Michael Spence shared the 2001 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for formalising how these problems work. The basic logic: when one party knows something the other cares about, the informed party can sometimes reveal that knowledge through costly observable actions. The classic example is education — a job candidate gets a degree not necessarily because it teaches useful skills, but because completing a degree is harder for low-ability candidates than high-ability ones. The degree separates types.

In dating, one piece of private knowledge that matters is your time horizon. Are you someone who values future relationship payoffs — willing to invest now for something that compounds later? Or are you weighting immediate returns more heavily? Economists call this a difference in discount factor: future-oriented types value the long run; short-term types prioritise the present.

Notice, too, that dating is rarely symmetric. One person is usually pursuing harder than the other — initiating more, investing more visibly, taking more reputational risk. The pursuer's signals are read differently from the pursued's. A person who waits while being actively courted is in a different strategic position from a person who waits while doing the courting. The model applies to both, but the costs land differently depending on which role you occupy.

The first thing to notice is that words alone are weak signals in early dating. "I'm looking for something serious" is free to say. Both types say it — the person who means it and the person who has learned that saying it opens doors. Since the claim costs nothing to produce and both types produce it equally, the listener cannot update their beliefs much. In game-theory terms, this kind of generic, unverifiable claim is cheap talk: low-cost and therefore low-information.

That does not mean all speech is useless. Specific plans, explicit boundaries, and statements that become falsifiable within weeks — "I've deleted my apps," "I'd like you to meet my friends next weekend" — carry more weight because they create reputational exposure. The person making the claim has now committed to something checkable. But the vague, early-stage assertion of seriousness? Both types produce that in roughly equal measure, which is why people instinctively feel that actions matter more than declarations in dating. They are informally recognising the cheap-talk problem.

The question is whether the actions people fixate on — particularly whether and when you have sex with someone — actually carry the information they think they do.

## 3. Why Waiting Can Separate Types, Sometimes

Under the right conditions, waiting for sex really can be informative. The logic is straightforward: if delaying physical intimacy is genuinely more costly for someone who prioritises short-term gratification than for someone who values a long-term outcome, then a willingness to wait credibly signals the long-term type. The short-term type finds the wait too expensive relative to the payoff of being perceived as serious, so they drop out or move on. The signal separates.

> **The separation condition.** A signal separates types when two things hold. First, the signal must cost more for one type than the other. Second, the benefit of being correctly identified must fall between those two costs — worth it for the future-oriented type, not worth it for the short-term type. When this inequality holds, a separating equilibrium exists. When it does not, both types send the same signal and the observer learns nothing.

Couples who delay sex tend to report higher relationship satisfaction later. Longitudinal work suggests that the context matters at least as much as the timing — sex within a developing relationship predicts different outcomes than sex in a one-night encounter. But these are associations, not clean causal tests. One explanation consistent with the signaling model is that patient, future-oriented people both wait longer *and* have better relationships — the signal and the type are correlated, which is what the theory would predict. Yet the evidence cannot rule out simpler rivals: assortative matching on conscientiousness, shared religiosity, higher self-control, or the stabilising effect of relationship context itself. The honest position is that the data fit the signaling story without confirming it over the alternatives.

The honest version of the positive case is this: waiting can function as a costly signal of seriousness under specific incentive conditions. It is not a universal rule, and treating it as one is where most folk wisdom goes wrong.

Consider the symmetry. A genuinely future-oriented person who sleeps with someone on a first date has not revealed a short time horizon — they may simply have felt strong desire, real connection, or comfort. Their seriousness becomes legible later, through consistency, not through that one evening. Conversely, a short-term-oriented person can wait three months if the social norm demands it, because waiting carries its own rewards: the appearance of patience, the flattery of being perceived as serious, the social approval of playing the long game. The signal only works when the cost differential is real. When it is not, both types wait, and the act tells you nothing.

## 4. Pooling: When Both Types Do The Same Thing

Even when types *could* theoretically separate, sometimes they do not. Both types converge on the same behaviour, and the signal collapses into noise. Game theorists call this a pooling equilibrium.

Suppose social norms create a strong expectation that serious people wait. If the norm is powerful enough, even someone with no long-term intentions will wait — not because it is costly, but because violating the norm carries its own penalties. Being seen as "too eager" has a social cost that has nothing to do with actual patience. When both types prefer to send the same signal for reputational or social reasons, the signal stops separating them. Waiting tells you nothing about type because everyone is waiting.

The clearest formal illustration of this is the Beer-Quiche game (Cho and Kreps, 1987). The setup: there are two types of player — call them tough and weak. There is an observer who will challenge the player only if they believe the player is weak. Before the observer decides, the player chooses a publicly visible action: order beer or order quiche. In the model's logic, tough types genuinely prefer beer, weak types prefer quiche. But here is the problem: the weak type *also* knows that the observer associates beer with toughness. So the weak type orders beer too — not because they like it, but because being seen as tough (and therefore not challenged) is worth more than eating what they actually want.

> Waiting tells you nothing about type because everyone is waiting.
{: .pull}

The result: both types order beer. The observer sees beer, learns nothing, and cannot tell the types apart. The signal has pooled. The action that was supposed to reveal type instead conceals it, because the cost of mimicry (eating something you dislike) is lower than the cost of being correctly identified (getting challenged).

Translate this back to dating. "Waiting" is the beer. When cultural norms make waiting the expected move for serious people, the short-term type waits too — not because patience is natural to them, but because the social cost of being seen as eager outweighs the cost of a few more dates without sex. Both types wait. The screener sees patience, learns nothing, and is exactly where they started.

People on dating apps already behave this way with their profiles — presenting selectively, sanding down edges, promising a version of themselves they hope can survive first contact. The calibration is itself a sign of how thoroughly people manage their signals. When both types learn the expected move, the move stops sorting them.

## 5. Why Dating Apps Make It Worse

If pooling happens when mimicry is cheap, then anything that reduces the cost of mimicry should increase pooling. Dating apps create exactly these conditions.

First, apps lower search costs. When alternative prospects are abundant and accessible with a swipe, the opportunity cost of "waiting" with any single person drops. A short-term type can afford to be patient with you because they are simultaneously pursuing three other people. The cost of the signal falls, and with it, its informativeness.

Second, apps produce mixed-motive pools. Users often arrive unsure what they want — something casual, something serious, something in between — and that ambiguity shifts week to week. You are not just trying to separate "serious" from "casual." You are trying to interpret behaviour from someone who may not have resolved their own type yet.

Third, app-first dating strips away the contextual information that used to help corroborate signals. When two people do not share a wider social network — no mutual friends, no overlapping communities, no reputational trail — the screener has less outside evidence to challenge or confirm what the signaler presents. The signal has to do more work, and it is doing that work in a noisier environment.

Large-scale analyses of dating-app behaviour show that stated preferences and actual contact decisions frequently diverge — people say they want one thing and choose another. Broader reviews find that expanded access widens options while simultaneously reducing commitment and encouraging comparison shopping. When search costs fall and outside options multiply, one-shot behavioural signals become less informative.

This does not mean dating apps have destroyed the possibility of honest signaling. It means the *kind* of signal that actually separates types needs to shift.

## 6. Why One Date Cannot Tell You What Six Months Can

The previous sections established that early dating often looks like a one-shot test — people read a single decision (did they sleep with me? did they wait? did they text the next day?) as though it reveals a stable type. The model says that test is weak. So what does work?

Real dating is not a one-shot interaction. It unfolds over time. Each date, each conversation, each small decision provides new information. And in a repeated game, a different kind of signal becomes available: costly consistency.

A single grand gesture — an expensive dinner, a dramatic declaration, a conspicuous wait — is easy to mimic. One night of patience proves little. But sustained investment over time is harder to fake, because the costs compound. A future-oriented type finds the ongoing investment worthwhile because they genuinely value what is being built. A short-term type faces escalating costs for maintaining a deception that yields diminishing returns. The longer the sequence continues, the more expensive the bluff becomes.

Studies tracking dating relationships over time find that fluctuations in perceived partner commitment predict later breakup better than any initial read of seriousness. It is not the level of commitment at the start that matters most — it is the consistency. Stable signals of dedication predict stability. Volatile signals predict dissolution.

There is, however, a crucial distinction the model requires you to make: the difference between dedication and constraint. Not everything that looks like commitment reflects a genuine long-term orientation. Some relationships persist because of accumulated constraints — a shared lease, intertwined finances, mutual friends who expect you to stay together, the sheer administrative hassle of breaking up. These constraints can mimic dedication without reflecting it.

Dedication — the active choice to invest in and prioritise the relationship — predicts satisfaction and stability. Constraint — the practical difficulty of leaving — predicts persistence but not necessarily happiness. A relationship can look committed from the outside because it is sticky, not because it is chosen. The screener's job is to tell the difference.

Genuine commitment devices work by deliberately restricting your own future options in ways that are visible and voluntary: going public with the relationship, introducing a partner to friends or family, closing the dating app, making concrete plans that would be costly to break. These actions raise the cost of defection. They are credible precisely because they are hard to reverse and clearly chosen. But sliding into cohabitation because the lease was expiring, or staying intertwined because untangling shared finances feels exhausting — those are constraints masquerading as commitment. The difference matters for what happens next.

## 7. Not Everything Intimate Is Strategic

There is a real objection here, and it would be dishonest to wave it away. Treating sex as a signal is reductive. People sleep together for reasons that have nothing to do with strategy: desire, loneliness, curiosity, alcohol, boredom, the particular alchemy of a Thursday night in April. Not every intimate act is a move in a game, and not every person who sleeps with someone early is "signaling" anything at all.

The signaling framework isolates one mechanism — the transmission and interpretation of information under uncertainty — but it does not claim to be a complete theory of human intimacy. People are not always strategic. They are not always coherent. Young-adult relationships in particular are marked by volatility that resists clean equilibrium stories: breakup-reconcile cycles, continued sex after breakups, on-again off-again patterns that look less like calculated moves and more like people figuring themselves out in real time.

A harder version of this objection says the model may be systematically weakest where people most want help — in situations driven not by clean strategic types but by ambivalence, vulnerability, and conflicting motives. That is a fair charge. The model does not handle mixed feelings elegantly; it works best when types are relatively stable and the main problem is inference rather than internal confusion.

The model earns its keep not by capturing everything, but by capturing something people routinely get wrong: the gap between what a signal *could* tell you in theory and what it actually tells you in practice. Most of the folk wisdom about sexual timing treats the channel as clean — as if one decision reliably reveals one type. The economics says the channel is noisy, context-dependent, and often pooled. That is a genuinely useful correction, even if it is not the whole story.

## 8. Better Screening, Clearer Signaling

If the model is right that one-shot signals are weak, early behaviour is noisy, and consistency matters more than any single move, what follows?

**If you are trying to read someone else:**

Stop overweighting isolated moments. A single sexual decision — whether early or late — is a weak signal because too many types can produce it. Instead, pay attention to clusters of behaviour that are costly to sustain over time. Are they consistently available, or do they disappear for days? Do they invest time and effort that has no immediate payoff for them? Have they foreclosed other options in ways you can verify, or are they keeping the field open? The question is not "did they wait?" It is "what would it cost the wrong person to keep doing what they're doing?"

Think of seriousness as something that emerges over time rather than something you decode from a single evening. Notice whether words and actions align across weeks. Observe how someone behaves when there is no audience and no immediate reward. People who are alert to the possibility of misrepresentation tend to respond not by reading signals harder, but by gathering outside evidence and testing claims over time.

And distinguish dedication from inertia. If someone is still around six months in, ask whether they are staying because they are choosing this, or because leaving has become inconvenient. The difference matters enormously for what happens next.

**If you are trying to be understood:**

Recognise that ambiguity preserves your options but weakens your legibility. If you want to be read as serious, pair your words with actions that cost you something — consistency, exclusivity, time, foregone alternatives. One gesture is easy to mimic. A pattern is harder to fake.

Timing affects interpretation. Saying "I want something real with you" before you have slept together lands differently from saying it after — not because the words change, but because the listener's interpretive frame shifts. That asymmetry is worth knowing even if it is not a reason to choreograph every move.

And if you are genuinely uncertain about what you want, the most useful thing you can do is say so clearly. Not because ambiguity is a strategic move, but because performing certainty you do not feel builds exactly the kind of noise that makes dating harder for everyone — including you. Honesty about uncertainty is not a signal in the game-theory sense. It is just honest, and in a landscape full of curated confidence, that tends to stand out.

---

The question you started with — sitting across from someone, deciding whether to go home with them, and what the choice will say — does not have a clean answer. That is actually the point. The folk wisdom promises clarity: wait and you will be taken seriously, rush and you will not. The economics says the signal is noisier than that. Sexual timing can separate types under specific conditions, but in most modern dating contexts — particularly app-mediated ones, where mimicry is cheap and motives are mixed — one decision tells you less than you hope.

The useful question is not "what does this moment mean?" It is "what pattern is emerging, and what would it cost the wrong person to fake it for long?" That reframe will not make the third-drink decision any less charged. But it might stop you from reading a novel into a single page.
