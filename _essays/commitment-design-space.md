## The Decision Inside The Moment

Something didn't quite fit.

It might have been a message that arrived late with an explanation that was too clean. A night described vaguely. A name mentioned once and then not again. Or nothing like that at all: a week where the attention moved somewhere else, a conversation that ended before it should have, a warmth with a different texture from the week before. You're not sure. That is the point. You are sitting with a piece of evidence that could be nothing.

The question in front of you isn't whether to trust. You probably already do, or you are past that. The question is what your response implies. At some point you said something, or acted in a way that implied something, and it set a rule: if this happens, I will do X. Now something has happened that might be this, and the question inside the question is whether you will actually do X.

The person on the other side is in the same position from the other angle. If they did something, they are wondering whether you will find out and what you will do if you do. If they did nothing, if this is your anxious reading of a difficult week, they are carrying the weight of a response they didn't earn, set off by a rule they may not fully know.

That sounds like a question about loyalty. It is closer to a question about whether the rule you implied can be carried out at all. This article explains why, for the part of a relationship where enforcement is the question, the answer depends less on character than on what the rule requires.

---

## Ways Of Treating A Partner Are Strategies

How forgiving you are, how proportionate your responses are, how hard a line you draw: these are, among other things, strategies. That word sounds cold, so hold it lightly. A strategy is a rule for how you respond to what happens. Forgiving often is a strategy. Matching the other person move for move is a strategy. Drawing a line and meaning it is a strategy. None of them is noble or calculating in itself, and all of them can be run well or badly.

Under the right conditions, the economics of repeated relationships says, any of several such rules can sustain cooperation in a long relationship. When tomorrow matters enough, the threat of losing the future outweighs the temptation to take something today.[^1] Article 1 in this series set out that foundation. The question it left open, and the one this article takes up, is what each particular rule needs in order to work. The rules need different things, and the things they need are not always available.

There are three: patience (the future has to matter enough that losing it is a real cost), detection (you have to be able to see what happened, and tell it from bad luck), and credibility (the response has to be something the person threatening it would actually carry out). Each of the three is established in the literature. Running them rule by rule, and pairing relationship devices with the condition each helps supply, is this article's own synthesis, not a textbook result.[^2]

One concession belongs here, at the start. Most people may stay faithful because they want to, and for them nothing needs enforcing. The conditions below describe the margin: the cases where later wants are expected to pull away from earlier promises, or where the question has already become one of enforcement. How large that margin is, this article cannot say.

The argument runs from the most forgiving rule to the harshest. Each rule is tested against the same three conditions, and each ends with what gives out first. The conclusion, which the article has to earn, is the weak form of a stronger claim: mild rules can mostly enforce themselves, at a price. Harsh rules, in which both people go cold for good, cannot. In the model, the rule that needs the least patience on paper is the one that does worst once the world is imperfect.

## The Game Underneath

Start with a bare version of the choice. Each period, each partner either holds up their end (attention, honesty, effort, fidelity) or holds back. The payoffs below are invented to show structure, not size.

| | Partner holds up their end | Partner holds back |
|---|---|---|
| **You hold up your end** | 3 each | You get 0, they get 5 |
| **You hold back** | You get 5, they get 0 | 1 each |

Both holding up their end beats both holding back. But in any single period, holding back pays more whatever the other does. Take the cell where you give and they take: they get 5, you get 0. That is the temptation, and the person on the wrong end of it gets the worst result in the table. Economists call that last payoff, with a straight face, the sucker payoff.

One period, taken alone, ends in both holding back. What changes it is that relationships continue. Today's choice shapes tomorrow's: the partner responds, the relationship shifts, and the future, which looked like a smooth continuation, turns out to be contingent. That is the shadow of the future. Cooperation can hold, not because people are angels, but because both prefer the continuing good outcome to the quick grab.[^1] It can hold in many ways, which is why the question is which rule, and what it needs.

**Patience** is the first condition. The grab is a one-period gain. Against it stands everything the grabber loses afterwards. If the future matters a lot, the comparison favours cooperation. If it matters little (the relationship is new and uncertain, both are half thinking about leaving), the grab wins even when the response is real.

**Detection** is the second. It is not only whether a lapse gets discovered, but whether any lapse can be told apart from bad luck. A missed dinner may be deliberate or unavoidable. A week of distraction may mean something or may be a bad week at work. Every rule has to decide how to respond to ambiguous signals. Punish noise and you punish the innocent. Forgive noise and you cover for the guilty. At a given level of noise and patience, no setting removes that trade-off.

A rough picture of what noise looks like from the receiving side: in two studies of dating couples, people whose sense of their partner's commitment kept fluctuating were more likely to see the relationship end, including in recently started relationships regardless of where the sense started.[^3] These studies are observational and describe the perceiver's reading, not the partner's behaviour. Doubt may be a symptom of a relationship already failing, not a cause. The finding does not identify anyone's strategy; a volatile signal fits a noisy cooperator, a partner reacting to you, or someone mixing in lapses. It shows only that ambiguous signals are real and costly.

**Credibility** is the hard one. Detection and patience ask whether a lapse is seen and costly. Credibility asks whether the threatened response will actually happen, because the response costs something too. "I'll leave if you cheat" makes sense in advance. At the moment it would have to be carried out, it costs the one who says it. Whether a threat can be believed depends on whether carrying it out will make sense later, and often it will not.

## Three Rules, Run Against The Conditions

The rules below come from the literature on repeated games, where they were developed as formal strategies for the game above.[^4] Treat them as aids for classifying what a response rule requires, not as a map of how people consciously behave. No study has shown that couples deliberately run them, and the experiments that exist involve strangers playing for money. What a rule shares with a formal strategy is its structure, and so what it needs in order to hold.

### Generous Tit-For-Tat: Forgiveness As Feature And Bug

The rule: cooperate first. After a lapse, answer in kind, but not every time; forgive some fraction of them, say one in three.

*Detection.* This is where the rule earns its place. Because it forgives some apparent lapses, a false alarm does not set off a spiral. In a simple simulation, it stays close to full cooperation even when a few percent of moves go wrong.[^5] In one prominent laboratory study of noisy repeated games, the strategies that did well were lenient toward a first apparent defection and tried to restore cooperation afterwards, though the subjects were strangers playing for money, and other forgiving rules, such as win-stay lose-shift, did about as well.[^6]

*Patience.* Here the price appears. Forgiveness weakens the deterrent. If a lapse is punished only some of the time, the grab pays more than it would against a rule that always answers, and the future has to matter more to offset it. Under perfect monitoring, the more generous the rule, the more patience it needs; push the generosity high enough and the rule stops deterring at all.[^7] Its strength under noise is bought with patience.

*Credibility.* The response is mild and temporary: a stretch of withholding, a cooling-off, a proportionate pullback. It does not require leaving or doing anything drastic. If both people would be a little worse off while it lasts, they are not much worse off than if they ended it, so there is little for renegotiation to undo.

*What fails first: exploitability.* A forgiveness rate is also a tolerance rate. A partner who knows you forgive about a third of lapses can calibrate, and use ambiguity as cover. In the model, the rule cannot tell a genuine bad week from a strategic one. This is the same trade-off as the patience cost, seen from the grabber's side. It is not an argument against being forgiving. It is an argument for being honest about what forgiveness costs.

### Tit-For-Tat: The Clean Mirror

The rule: cooperate first, then do whatever the partner did last time. One lapse earns one lapse. One kindness earns one kindness.

In Axelrod's two computer tournaments, the first with about fourteen entries and the second with about sixty, tit-for-tat won both.[^8] It is nice (starts by cooperating), provocable (answers a lapse at once), forgiving (returns to cooperation the moment the partner does) and clear (always predictable). Those tournaments had no noise.

*Patience.* Using the payoffs above, tit-for-tat needs more patience than grim trigger, the harsh rule below: roughly a two-in-three chance of the relationship continuing, against one in two. The binding constraint is the fear of being the one who gives while the other takes, so this threshold depends on how bad the worst payoff is, not only on how severe the punishment feels.

*Detection.* Here the rule starts to show its limits. In a world with no noise, it is elegant. With noise, it generates echoes. You make a mistake, a cooperative move that looks like a lapse. Your partner answers it. You answer the answer.

> Round 1: both cooperate. A's kindness is misread as a lapse.
> Round 2: B answers the "lapse" by holding back. A, seeing B hold back, holds back too.
> Round 3: B, seeing A hold back, holds back again.
> The pattern continues until a chance misread breaks it.

No step is irrational. Each person is doing what the rule prescribes. The joint result is sustained mutual punishment over a signal that may have been false, and in a simple simulation the long-run payoff settles well below full cooperation.[^5]

*Credibility.* The punishment is brief and proportionate, so it does not ask either person to give up the relationship. The echo is a punishment phase that is worse for both than cooperating, so renegotiation bites here too. The difference is how much is left to renegotiate: a one-period penalty costs little to forgo, and little rides on it.

*What fails first: detection under noise.* The rule is clear and cheap to carry out; what it cannot survive is the world's imperfection. A false signal sets off a retaliation neither party wanted and neither can easily stop.

### Grim Trigger: The Harshest Rule

Grim trigger needs its definition stated plainly, because it is often confused with a hidden grudge. It is a known, common-understanding rule that cooperation ends for good after a single lapse. Its deterrence depends on being known and on both people being willing to carry it out once it applies. A concealed version is not a grim trigger. This is the definition this series uses, not the only one in the literature.

The logic is attractive. If one lapse ends everything permanently, the cost of any lapse is total.

*Patience.* On paper, grim trigger needs the least patience of the three, a one-in-two chance of continuation with the payoffs above (the Friedman result, in a perfect-monitoring game).[^9] The deterrence is so severe that a relationship with modest forward-looking patience could, in theory, be held together by it. It looks like a point in its favour. It is where the problem starts.

*Detection.* Any noise is dangerous to it. At a one percent chance that a move is flipped, with both partners exposed, the expected wait before the first false alarm is on the order of fifty periods; the chance of surviving a hundred periods without one is about one in eight. Over a long relationship, a false alarm is close to certain, and under grim trigger it is permanent. In a simple simulation of the long-run average payoff, the rule spends nearly all its time in mutual holding back.[^5] This is a toy calculation, not a finding about couples, and it concerns average payoffs over a very long run. Whether grim trigger can still be an equilibrium under noise is a separate question; with enough patience, it can. What it cannot do is perform.

Experiments with noisy repeated games point the same way: leniency toward a first apparent defection, and a willingness to try to restore cooperation, do well.[^6] Grim trigger offers neither.

The falsely suspected partner bears the worst of it: a permanent punishment for something they did not do. The rule has no provision for appeal.

*Credibility: two problems, not one.* The word hides two separate failures, and they need different fixes.

The first concerns the threat to leave. Leaving is not a move inside the relationship; it ends the relationship, and with it everything you were trying to protect. It is rational only if what you get outside, less the cost of getting out, beats what you get by staying. At high enough entanglement, it does not. That is a structural fact about the price of exit, not a failure of nerve.

The second concerns going cold. Suppose you do not leave but simply stop giving, and you stay in the relationship to enforce it. If your partner has gone permanently cold, going cold back is the sensible reply at every step. So the reversion passes one test. But a frozen standoff is worse for both of you than starting again, and both of you know it. When the punishment arrives, both would rather drop it. If you can talk, you will. Economists call a threat that survives this temptation renegotiation-proof, and symmetric permanent reversion usually does not.[^10] A threat that both people know will be talked away deters nothing before the lapse.

> A threat that both people know will be talked away deters nothing before the lapse.
{: .pull}

| Problem | What fails | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Threat to leave | Not worth carrying out | Leaving costs the leaver the whole relationship, above some exit price |
| Permanent reversion | Both would rather restart | The punishment is worse for both than cooperating |

Neither is a question of courage. Both are features of what the rule asks.

*What fails first: detection and credibility, together.* With perfect monitoring, the weak point is renegotiation. With noise, the rule collapses from false alarms before renegotiation arises.

*Does it enforce itself?* Not reliably. With perfect monitoring and enough patience, grim trigger is a consistent plan. It breaks only if communication is cheap and effective enough for renegotiation to happen, and where communication is hard, as in long separation or estrangement, it bites less. But "harsh" here means symmetric, permanent mutual reversion. Theory allows harsher-seeming punishments that survive renegotiation, such as a penance in which the offender is punished while the other side keeps cooperating, though they need the offender's acceptance.[^10] The weak form of the claim is about the symmetric kind: it asks both people to do something neither will want to do when the moment comes.

### The Rules Side By Side

| Rule | Patience needed (perfect monitoring, payoffs above) | Under noise | What the punishment asks | What fails first |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Generous tit-for-tat | More than tit-for-tat, rising with forgiveness | Good | A brief pullback | Exploitability |
| Tit-for-tat | About two in three | Echo cycles | One period of holding back | Detection |
| Grim trigger | One in two | Collapses on average | Permanent mutual coldness | Credibility and detection |

The table inverts the naive ordering. The harsher the rule, the less patience it needs, and the worse it does when the world is imperfect. The milder the rule, the more patience it needs, and the better it handles noise. Mild rules can mostly enforce themselves because what they ask is cheap to carry out and leaves little to renegotiate. The price is that they are the most exploitable and demand the most patience. Harsh rules are the ones that need something that has already changed what both people will want when the moment arrives.

## What Can Supply Credibility From Outside The Couple

Relationships deepen in familiar steps: moving in together, sharing money, getting engaged, being folded into each other's families, becoming public as a couple, marrying. Each step changes the conditions, and three kinds of structure can supply what a harsh rule cannot supply itself. The mapping below is the author's reading, built from the literature but not itself an empirical result.

**Exit cost.** As a relationship collects shared things (a lease, shared finances, a joint social life, a public identity), leaving costs more. At low levels, "I'll leave" is a cheap and so believable threat, but it does not hurt the cheater much. As the cost rises, the threat hurts the cheater more: there is more to lose. It also hurts the leaver more. Past a point, which Article 1 calls the credibility cliff, carrying out the threat costs the leaver more than staying does, and the threat is empty.[^11] The same entanglement that sharpens the threat for the person threatened takes it away from the person making it.

Each step affects the three conditions differently. Cohabitation adds shared space and shared observers, which may help detection. Joint finances add financial monitoring and shared stakes. Engagement makes the relationship's status public, creating reputational costs for breaking what has been announced. Devices that signal that a relationship is meant to last bear on patience by raising the expected continuation, a different channel from exit cost.

A cost side deserves equal time. In a study of about 1,200 young unmarried adults in opposite-sex relationships, followed for eight months, material constraints such as a joint lease, shared account or shared pet were associated with staying together. The sense of being trapped, felt constraint, predicted breakup.[^12] Constraints can both stabilise a relationship and be felt as a trap. Being unable to leave is not the same as wanting to stay. And the study measured staying together. It did not measure whether anything deterred anyone from cheating.

Marriage is the limit case, and the case where it is easiest to say too much. The law sets the terms of exit: it divides property and sets procedure. In most settings it does not punish infidelity or enforce fidelity as such. The evidence is that exit rules change behaviour inside marriages, though some of it, such as the effect of unilateral divorce on divorce rates, is contested.[^13] By the cliff logic, marriage's high exit cost weakens the spouse's threat to leave. What marriage supplies is less a punishment than a durable shared future, which bears on patience. Past the cliff, what remains is withdrawal in kind, a sustained change in how much you give, which is the milder rule from the preceding section.

**Community enforcement.** The second source is older and less visible. If the people around you know a relationship's rules, and would respond to a breach, then your cost of defecting includes their response.

The formal version is Kandori's. In a large community of strangers who meet at random, cooperation can be sustained, because a defection spreads as a chain of refusals to cooperate with the defector.[^14] A historical illustration is Greif's account of the Maghribi traders, merchants who, he argued, enforced agreements across long distances through coalition reputation.[^15] The analogy is rough, and Edwards and Ogilvie contest whether such a coalition existed as described. Use it as a model of how reputation can enforce, not as established history.[^15]

Carrying the logic to a couple is this article's own step, and two gaps open. First, in Kandori's model, third parties punish out of self-interest, because they will meet the defector. A couple's friends and family do not meet your partner in a random draw. They may disapprove, but whether they would bear costs to act is exactly what the approval literature does not show. Second, community punishment is costly to the punishers and falls under the same renegotiation problem as the couple's own; Kandori's contagion version is cooperative but fragile, much as grim trigger is at scale. At best, community enforcement spreads the cost and adds information. It moves the problem rather than dissolving it.

The cost is real as well. Delegated enforcement means losing discretion over how enforcement happens. The family that disapproves of the relationship is not just enforcing the breach. Third parties have interests of their own.

**Emotional commitment.** The third source is the hard case for the argument. Frank argued that emotions can work as commitment devices: a person whose response to betrayal is involuntary, and visible to their partner, has committed in a way calculation could not.[^16] It partly sidesteps the renegotiation problem, because it is a disposition, not a negotiation. It works only if the partner knows about it, and feelings fade.

The limit is just as real. Credibility without calculation is credibility without calibration. A response that makes punishment automatic also makes it fire on a false alarm. The partner whose response is involuntary is credible, and also unpredictable in the worst moment, when something ambiguous has happened and both are trying to work out what.

The same fork applies to internalised norms. If a person's rule about infidelity is part of who they are, the response is automatic without either emotion or outside structure. If internalised norms count as structure outside the pair, the claim that credibility needs such structure is true almost by definition. If they do not, a sufficiently internalised norm supplies credibility entirely from inside, and the claim is false. This article does not resolve the fork. It names it.

| Device | May help with | Key limit |
|---|---|---|
| Cohabitation | Detection (shared observation) | Past the cliff, the threat to leave is empty |
| Joint finances | Detection (shared financial view) | Monitoring is a cost borne by the monitored |
| Engagement | Credibility (reputational stakes); patience | Only if others would act on a breach, not just know of it |
| Family integration | Credibility (third parties) | Family has its own interests; may enforce what neither partner wants |
| Public integration | Credibility (social standing) | The community must be willing to act, not just to approve |
| Marriage | Patience (durable shared future) | Does not guarantee detection; raises exit cost, weakening the threat to leave |

This table is the author's reading, not a finding. The implication holds only in the model: a mild rule needs neither state nor community. A harsh rule needs outside support, and even then faces the noise that no device removes.

## When The End Is In Sight

All of this assumes an indefinite future. What if both people know the end is coming?

Work backward from the last day, which has no future to protect: the grab wins. So on the second-to-last day each knows the other will grab tomorrow, and cooperating today earns the sucker payoff. By the same logic, applied back to the beginning, cooperation unravels entirely.

Real subjects cooperate more than that logic predicts, and the cooperation fades gradually rather than collapsing all at once.[^17] A standard explanation is that if there is even a small chance that the other person is the kind of player who reciprocates, cooperation near the end can still be rational.[^18] And most relationships do not have a known end date, which keeps the backward logic from starting.

A limited search found no study of relationships with known end dates, so the endgame applies by structural logic, not by direct evidence. It is a stress test on patience. Every rule gets harder to hold as the horizon shortens, and harsher rules, which rest on the future being valuable, have the most to lose. Read it as a stress test, not a prediction.

## If You Cheat, I'll Leave

Apply this to the specific threat the article opened with. People say it, or imply it, in many forms, and plenty never say it at all: the line is drawn by how they behave. In the strict sense, it is a threat to leave, which is an exit. Going cold would be reversion, the other problem. The two are different, and each fails differently.

*The threat to leave.* Leaving costs the leaver. After years, with shared finances, friends, spaces and perhaps children, the question at the moment of discovery is whether staying or leaving is worth more, net of exit costs. At high enough entanglement, and if the breach is read as a one-off after which the partner will return to cooperating, staying wins. The person who cannot leave is not weak; they have built something real. But the same logic has a price for them: the partner learns what a lapse costs, and learns it is less than was said. This is the exit cost doing what it was built to do, except that the thing it was meant to protect against has now happened.

What makes the threat believable is a low exit cost, a good outside option, or a different reading of the breach.

*Going cold.* This is the other threat. The relationship continues, but in the degraded state that follows a known, unaddressed betrayal. As established, it passes one test and fails the other: both would prefer to go back, and if they can talk, they will. What would make it believable is a punishment that cannot be talked away: third parties who would act, a social network that would respond without being asked, an emotional commitment deep enough that going back is no longer what the punisher wants.[^10][^16] A couple rarely supplies that for itself. Two people across a table, both preferring to cooperate, will cooperate.

*The cheater's side.* One more piece of the model comes from the person who cheats. Imperfect monitoring is usually treated as noise that comes from the world. But an informed person can add noise: explaining an absence away, being extra attentive on the days that will be checked, making an ambiguous week look like a normal one. Economists call this signal jamming, after a model of predatory pricing in which a firm clouds a rival's inference.[^19] Carrying it to a relationship is this article's own step. The point is that the cheater is working to make the partner's detection condition harder to meet, and the partner's tolerance of noise is exactly what that work exploits. The falsely suspected partner faces the mirror image: if the channel has been muddied, a cooperative person cannot clear their name.

*A third possibility.* There is a reading of "I'll leave" that involves no punishment at all. A long panel study of married Americans found that infidelity and marital unhappiness feed each other: unhappiness predicts later cheating, and cheating predicts later unhappiness.[^20] That fits a reading, which is the article's interpretation and not the study's finding, in which a lapse reveals a kind of person, not a decision made in a moment. Leaving after discovery is then not a punishment under a rule but a response to new information. And it changes the first failure above. The leave-threat looks empty only if the breach is read as a one-off. If it is read as revealing a pattern, staying is worth less, and leaving can be rational at any level of entanglement.

Punishment and learning look the same from outside. Both end in leaving after a discovery. Punishment is backward-looking: a rule fixed before the breach and applied to it. Learning is forward-looking: a revision of belief after it. Knowing which is operating changes what the response implies, and what the falsely suspected partner can do about it. The article cannot tell you which applies in a given case.

## The Strongest Objection

The strongest objection to everything above is also the simplest. Most people are faithful because they want to be.

Where a person wants to keep the promise, nothing needs enforcing. No threat has to be credible and no exit cost calibrated. Someone who never wants to cheat is not deterred by anything. They are simply not tempted. If most faithful relationships rest on dedication, the machinery of conditions, rules and devices describes a margin, not the ordinary. This may well be true. Nothing in the evidence here can rank the two. A study of dating couples found that dedication and material constraints each independently predicted staying together,[^12] which shows they can coexist and says nothing about how common either is. The model applies where dedication has not formed, or later wants are expected to diverge from earlier promises, or circumstances have changed.

There is a second form of the objection. Cheating is betrayal. Calling it a defection, and describing a partner as a counterparty to be deterred, drains the moral weight from what happened. The model describes the structure of enforcement, not the moral status of the act; it does not excuse anyone by modelling them. But the objection has a further edge: thinking in terms of strategies, threats and exit costs can change how a person treats the one they are thinking about, and a frame can become self-fulfilling. That is a real limit. Using enforcement logic where dedication is the right description mischaracterises the relationship, and may harm it. The model is for cases where enforcement is already the question.

Within that scope, the framework yields claims that could be wrong. If it is right, severity of response should track support: harsher, permanent responses should be found more where third parties know and would act, and milder ones where they do not. And the link between entanglement and cheating should be non-monotone: falling as exit costs rise, then rising once the threat to leave is empty. Neither has been tested on couples.

The framework is diagnostic, not prescriptive. This article does not tell you what rule to run or how to make a threat believable. For the cases where enforcement is the question, the three-condition classification (which is missing, what would supply it, what it costs) gives more precise language than "I trusted you" or "you should have known."

## Five Questions About Your Own Response

Here are five questions the framework generates. None tells you what to do; each tells you what you are looking at.

**Which rule does the response resemble, and what would it need to be believed?** A harsh rule needs near-perfect detection and either a commitment against renegotiation or outside enforcement. A mild rule needs enough patience, and some tolerance for the noise problem. Naming which condition is missing changes the analysis of whether the response is believable.

**Is this a punishment, or what you would do anyway if you knew what you know?** Punishment answers a lapse under a rule that existed before it. Learning answers information. They look alike and mean different things. A punishing response is committed to consistency; a learning one is revising a picture of who the partner is.

**Who besides the two of you could act on a breach, and do you want them to?** Community enforcement works only if the community knows the rules and would respond. If yes, the relationship has borrowed credibility from its network, at the cost of discretion. If no, enforcement stays between the two of you, which is the hardest place to sustain it.

**How noisy is what either of you can observe, and what does each of you do on a false alarm?** A rule that punishes noise punishes the innocent. A rule that forgives noise covers for the guilty. Where does yours land? And when you have punished on a false alarm, and almost everyone has, at some point, what happened?

**From the other seat: what does the response imply about the rule being applied?** If you are the one whose action is being read, carrying the weight of a response you may not have earned, the question is what rule the other person appears to be running and what it would need to be believed. The same rule can look different in different settings. The echo cycle that tit-for-tat generates on a false alarm is not a failure of the rule. It is the rule working exactly as designed, on bad inputs.

## Is That The Relationship You Wanted?

Return to the moment the article opened with. Something didn't quite fit. The question is not whether to trust. It is what your response implies, and whether you will carry it out.

A harsh rule, a line in the sand with a permanent consequence, implies near-perfect detection and a commitment that neither of you will want to honour once the moment comes. In the model, what makes such a rule believable is something outside the two of you that has already changed what you will do: a family that knows and would act, a legal process that sets the terms of exit, an emotional commitment so deep the response is not a choice. When those structures exist, they supply what bilateral negotiation cannot. They make the threat real by making the moment of carrying it out less a moment of choice.

Removing discretion has a cost. The family that would act on a breach might also act in ways neither partner wanted. A legal structure that makes exit expensive does so when the moment calls for repair as well as when it calls for departure. An emotional commitment that makes punishment automatic fires on false alarms too. Structures that make a harsh rule believable tend to be ones you cannot easily forgive your way out of.

None of that is the only route. A mild rule, with its limits, can mostly stand on its own: forgiving, proportionate, exploitable, and dependent on patience. It asks less of the people who run it and less from outside.

So the question has a conditional shape. If you want a harsh rule to be believed, the structures that supply belief tend to remove the discretion to forgive. Is that the relationship you wanted?
