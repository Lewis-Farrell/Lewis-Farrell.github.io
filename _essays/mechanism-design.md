You have been here before. Not with this particular person — with this
particular feeling. Three weeks in, maybe five. The texts come steadily. The
dates have a rhythm. There is enough warmth to keep you leaning forward and
enough vagueness to keep you from knowing where you stand. You are not being
mistreated. You are not being pursued with any special urgency, either. You are
suspended in something that has all the textures of early romance — the
lingering eye contact, the Saturday nights, the "I had a great time" — without
any of the clarity.

So you do what most advice tells you to do. You communicate. You raise the
question. And you get back something that sounds like an answer without quite
being one: *I really like spending time with you. I'm just not great with
labels. Let's see where this goes.*

That sentence is not a lie, exactly. But it is also not information. It costs
nothing to say. It forecloses nothing. And you have heard some version of it
enough times now that the problem no longer feels like a few bad calls. It
feels like a system that keeps producing the same result: ambiguity sustained
at low cost, just enough warmth to delay your exit, and a slow bleed of
investment from the person who wanted clarity toward the person who could
afford to wait.

This article is about why that keeps happening — and what you can do about it
that goes deeper than "raise your standards" or "trust the process." The
argument is that many of modern dating's most persistent frustrations are not
failures of judgment. They are failures of **mechanism design**: the structure
of rules, defaults, costs, and stage gates that determine what behavior gets
rewarded, what information gets revealed, and how quickly aligned and
misaligned people separate from each other.

By the end, you should have a sharper question than "is this person right for
me?" The question is: **what is the structure I am dating inside, what
behavior does it reward, and how should I redesign it if I want better
information?**

## 1. This Is Not Just A Choice Problem

The default way people think about dating failure is personal. You picked the
wrong person. You ignored the red flags. You moved too fast, or you were too
guarded, or you did not communicate clearly enough. All of these can be true in
any given case. But they share an assumption: that the outcome was mainly
determined by the quality of your individual decisions.

Mechanism design starts from a different premise. It asks not "what should this
person have chosen?" but "what rules produced this outcome?" The field earned
the 2007 Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences for formalizing an idea that sounds
obvious once you hear it: when people hold private information — about their
intentions, their priorities, their willingness to commit — the structure they
operate inside determines what gets revealed and what stays hidden.[^1] A
well-designed mechanism makes it in each person's interest to act on their true
preferences. A badly designed one lets people profit from strategic
misrepresentation.

That distinction matters in dating because most of the information you actually
need is private. You cannot directly observe whether someone wants a
relationship or just enjoys the attention cycle. You cannot see whether their
vision of the future includes you or whether you are a pleasant placeholder.
You cannot verify that "I'm not ready for a label" means genuine uncertainty
rather than comfortable access without obligation. The words sound the same
either way.

The question mechanism design asks is not "how do I read this person better?"
It is "what would have to be true about the structure of our interaction for
their real preferences to show up in their behavior?" It moves the problem from
reading signals to designing conditions under which signals become reliable.

There is one important caveat, though. A mechanism does not have to be
complicated to work. In fact, the opposite is usually true. Recent work on
mechanism simplicity shows that if people cannot understand why a structure
works in their interest, they will resist it or game it regardless of how
elegant the design is on paper.[^2] An incentive-compatible mechanism that
nobody trusts is just a theory. The same applies to dating: if your
"relationship rules" feel like a compliance exam, they will repel the people
you want and attract the ones who enjoy performing. The best romantic
mechanisms are simple enough that both people can see what they reward.

## 2. What This Logic Assumes

Before going further, it is worth being honest about when this lens helps and
when it does not — because it does not apply to everyone equally, or always.

Mechanism design works best in one specific case: when someone already knows
what they want and the only open question is whether the structure makes them
reveal it. A person who wants low-commitment company and says "let's see where
this goes" to keep a good thing convenient is sitting on stable private
information. That is the case the rest of this article is mostly about, and it
is the case the framework handles well.

There is a second case it handles only with humility. Often the information
does not exist yet. Two people genuinely do not know whether attraction will
deepen, whether their lives have room for this, whether this particular person
will turn out to be worth rearranging things for. Intentions there are not
hidden facts waiting to be exposed; they are partly formed by the interaction
itself. The staged, low-cost approach this article recommends is still the
right tool — it is exactly how you learn something that is not yet knowable —
but it is learning, not unmasking. The other person is not always concealing a
type. Sometimes you are both still becoming one.

And there is a third case where the framework does not apply at all. Not
everyone is optimizing for clarity, exclusivity, and escalating commitment.
Someone who openly wants a deliberately loose, non-escalating connection — and
whose partner knows it — is not a "misaligned type." They have a different
objective. This piece is not an argument that clarity and commitment are what
everyone should want. It is an argument that when two people nominally want the
same thing, some structures reveal whether that is true far better than others.
The failure this article is about is ambiguity that quietly persists on one
side while the other believes the goal is shared — not ambiguity that two
people have chosen with open eyes.

One more piece of honesty: the advice that follows assumes a kind of design
freedom not everyone has. Setting your own pace, introducing a checkpoint,
leaving before dependence gets high — these are easier in a thick dating
market, with time, money, safety, and social options, than in a thin one, or
under age pressure, financial dependence, a small community, or unequal
personal risk. The framework describes what better structures look like. It
does not pretend everyone is equally free to build one.

With that scoped: the four tools in this piece — the Mimicry Gap, the
Revelation Ladder, the Optionality Tax, and Alignment Escrow — are not laws of
dating or established terms of art. They are a vocabulary built from existing
work in mechanism design and relationship science, organized so that you can
actually use it.

## 3. The Pooling Problem: Why Different People Look The Same

Imagine two people who both want to date you. One wants what you want: a real
relationship built on mutual effort, growing clarity, and eventual commitment.
The other wants something less than that — attention, validation, easy
intimacy, the comfort of someone who is interested in them — but is not
prepared to match the cost that a real relationship would eventually require.

In a well-designed dating environment, these two people would behave
differently. The structure would impose costs or create checkpoints where
the second person's unwillingness to invest becomes visible.

In a badly designed one, they can look identical for weeks or months. Both
text you back. Both show up on Saturday night. Both say things that sound like
interest. Both sleep with you. Both enjoy the momentum of early romance without
anyone having to answer the question that would separate them: *what are you
willing to give up for this?*

And there is a third person, easy to forget precisely because the structure
hides them too: someone who also says "let's see where this goes" and means it
honestly. They are not extracting anything. They genuinely do not know yet —
about you, about their own readiness, about their timing. They are not the
problem. But where staying vague costs nothing, you cannot tell them apart from
the other two, and that is the entire point. The failure is not that vague
people are bad. It is that the environment makes the honestly unsure person,
the genuinely aligned person, and the person enjoying low-cost access all
produce the same sentence.

This is what economists call a **pooling equilibrium**. Different types — in
this case, people with meaningfully different intentions and time horizons —
end up in the same behavioral pool because the environment does not impose
enough differential cost to sort them. The person who genuinely wants to build
something and the person who is happy collecting warmth on their own terms can
both say "let's see where this goes" and mean completely different things. The
sentence is **cheap talk**: costless to produce, identical regardless of type,
and therefore almost informationally worthless.[^3]

The reason pooling persists is not that people are stupid. It is that the
structure permits it. When the cost of ambiguity is low — when you can enjoy
someone's time, attention, affection, and body without ever having to
commit, plan visibly, sacrifice an alternative, or even define what you are
doing — then staying vague is not a character flaw. It is the rational
response to a mechanism that does not charge anything for it.

The damage shows up in asymmetry. Longitudinal research on commitment finds
that relationships where partners are unequally committed are consistently
lower in quality and more likely to dissolve.[^4] Asymmetrical commitment is
not just emotionally painful — it is structurally predictive of failure. And
it thrives in environments that let one person invest heavily while the other
retains full optionality at minimal cost.

Meanwhile, the research on what actually builds trust points in the opposite
direction. Trust does not grow from warm words alone. It grows more reliably
when someone visibly departs from their own immediate self-interest for the
relationship's benefit — and the other person notices.[^5] Willingness to
sacrifice, even in small ways, is associated with relationship quality and
persistence.[^6] The mechanism is specific: it is not that sacrifice feels
romantic. It is that sacrifice carries information. It tells you something
about the other person's priorities that a pleasant text cannot.

So the pooling problem has a clear structure. Bad mechanisms let different
types produce the same cheap behavior. Good mechanisms create conditions where
type-revealing behavior — the kind that costs more if you do not actually mean
it — shows up earlier.

## 4. The Mimicry Gap

If pooling is the disease, the **Mimicry Gap** is the diagnostic. It answers a
simple question: at any given stage of dating, how much harder is it for the
wrong person to keep looking like the right one?

Think of it this way. Every stage of a developing relationship imposes some
cost of continuation — time, effort, emotional exposure, foregone alternatives,
or visible commitment. For someone who actually wants what the relationship is
becoming, those costs are acceptable. The expected payoff exceeds the price.
For someone who wants something less — access without obligation, intimacy
without trajectory, attention without reciprocal sacrifice — the same costs
start to bite differently. Clarity is expensive when you do not want to be
pinned down. Exclusivity is expensive when you want to keep browsing.
Introducing someone to your friends is expensive when you are not sure you want
this person in your real life.

The Mimicry Gap is the difference between those two costs. When the gap is
wide, the structure is working: aligned people find it natural to continue,
and misaligned people start to resist, withdraw, or deflect. When the gap is
narrow — when both types can keep going at roughly the same price — the
structure is failing, and you are stuck in a pool where intention remains
invisible.

> **A more formal version**
>
> At stage *k*, the aligned type faces a continuation cost that is lower than
> the value they expect from the relationship progressing. The misaligned
> type faces a cost that starts to exceed the value they are getting, because
> what the next stage demands — clarity, sacrifice, exclusivity, visible
> planning — is precisely what they do not want to provide.
>
> A dating structure is well-designed when each successive stage widens this
> gap enough to separate types, without imposing so much deadweight cost that
> even aligned people opt out.

This is why vague early dating is so reliably frustrating. The Mimicry Gap in
the first few weeks is almost always narrow. Texting back promptly, showing up
for a drink, being charming, expressing interest — none of these cost
meaningfully more for someone who is not serious than for someone who is.
The behavioral signal is the same; the underlying intention may be completely
different.

The practical implication is that **early signals are almost always
over-weighted**. People agonize over what a first-week text pattern "means," or
whether the third date going well is a real indicator, when the honest answer
is: at that stage, the mechanism has not yet imposed enough differential cost
to tell you much. The signal is not fake — it is just cheap enough that both
types can produce it.

The Mimicry Gap only starts to widen when the structure demands something that
is differentially costly: planning ahead, showing up consistently over weeks
rather than in bursts of enthusiasm, being willing to define the relationship
to others, choosing this person over a visible alternative, adjusting a
schedule rather than fitting the other person into leftover time. These are
not arbitrary "tests." They are moments where the cost of continuing to mimic
seriousness rises for someone who is not actually serious.

The question to carry forward is not "is this person serious?" — at least not
as a binary read on their character. The better question is: **what has the
structure of our interaction asked of them so far, and would a non-serious
person have found it easy or hard to do the same thing?**

## 5. The Revelation Ladder

The Mimicry Gap tells you what to look for. The **Revelation Ladder** tells
you how to sequence it.

The core insight is simple: you cannot screen for everything on the first date,
and you should not try. Dating is an information-acquisition process under
genuine uncertainty. People learn about their compatibility as a couple by
spending time together, and that learning trades off against continued search
— every week invested in one person is a week not spent meeting others.[^7]
There is real cost on both sides of the ledger: screen too aggressively and you
lose good matches to false negatives; screen too loosely and you waste months
in relationships that were never going to work.

The Revelation Ladder is a staged sequence that resolves this tradeoff by
increasing the cost of continuation in calibrated increments, each rung buying
better information while keeping error costs manageable. It is not a rigid
protocol, and it is not a script to run on someone. It is a design principle —
learn in stages, with rising cost, before dependence gets too high — and the
four rungs below are kinds of cost-escalation, not a numbered ritual to march a
person through.

**Low-cost interaction for baseline fit.** Early dates, initial texting, first
impressions. The Mimicry Gap here is narrow, and that is fine. You are not
trying to determine long-term compatibility. You are testing for baseline ease,
attraction, and whether the person can hold a conversation. The appropriate
cost at this stage is low: a couple of hours, a drink, a walk. If it does not
work, both sides have lost very little.

**Repeated follow-through for consistency.** This is where the first real
information appears. Not from any single impressive gesture, but from a
pattern. Do they follow up? Do plans materialize or dissolve into vague "let's
hang out soon"? Is the effort roughly mutual, or is one person consistently
initiating while the other passively receives? Consistency across multiple
low-cost interactions is more diagnostic than one high-effort display. A
pattern is harder to sustain than a moment — that is the article's inference
from the costly-signaling logic, not a separate measured finding.[^8]

**Social legibility and reciprocal planning.** At some point, a developing
relationship has to become visible — to friends, to schedules, to the small
public of each person's actual life. This is where the Mimicry Gap widens
meaningfully. Introducing someone to your social world, making plans that
require coordination, adjusting routines to include them — these acts cost more
if you are not genuinely invested. Research on couple identity finds that
clarity about "who we are together" is itself associated with higher commitment
and lower dissolution risk.[^9] Clarity is not merely an emotional preference.
It is structurally informative.

**Higher-friction contexts for dedication under cost.** The later rungs involve
situations where effort, sacrifice, or inconvenience become real. Navigating a
disagreement without bolting. Showing up when it is not convenient. Choosing
this relationship over a concrete alternative. These moments test dedication —
not just attraction, not just compatibility, but willingness to bear a cost for
the relationship's sake; the sacrifice and trust research speaks to dedication
in general, not to this staging in particular.[^10] They are the stages where
more thoughtful, explicit decision-making is associated with higher
satisfaction, stronger dedication, and fewer extradyadic involvements than
vague sliding.[^11]

The enemy of good screening is not spontaneity — it is structurelessness. It
is a dating process where intimacy, access, and emotional investment can climb
steeply while clarity and reciprocity remain flat.

Online daters already do a version of this. In a qualitative study of fifteen
online daters, participants used progressive, personalized filtering steps —
text, photographs, chat, and webcam contact — before deciding whether to meet
in person.[^12] The Revelation Ladder makes that logic explicit and extends it
beyond the first meeting into the months that follow.

## 6. The Optionality Tax

Picture the version of this you have probably lived. Two months in. The texts
are still warm and still prompt. But every plan lands inside the next
seventy-two hours, you have met no one who matters to them, and not once has
the structure required them to choose you over anything. Nothing is wrong.
Nothing is moving, either. The environment has made staying undefined almost
free — and free things get overproduced.

This is the structural story of app-era dating, though it is not a single
story. The most careful recent economics research paints a picture more
complex than either cheerful disruption or cultural catastrophe. When Tinder
rolled out across U.S. college campuses, it sharply and persistently increased
sexual activity — but had little corresponding effect on long-term relationship
formation or relationship quality.[^13] A separate analysis, in an NBER working
paper on broader online-dating adoption, found significant effects on marriage,
divorce, and matching patterns, with desktop-era and mobile-era platforms
producing meaningfully different results.[^14] Low-friction markets do not move
everyone the same way: the same architecture that lets one person keep their
options open indefinitely lets another surface a dealbreaker, a faith, or a
geography in the first ten minutes. The point is not that apps ruined dating.
It is that the structure of the market changed what behavior was cheap and what
was costly — unevenly.

Call this effect the **Optionality Tax**. In any market — romantic or otherwise
— if participants can retain maximum optionality at near-zero cost, the system
will systematically overproduce ambiguity and underproduce commitment. Not
because people are bad, but because the mechanism does not charge anything for
keeping your options open while someone else is narrowing theirs.

The tax is not literal. It is an opportunity cost. The question is whether the
dating structure imposes it or waives it:

- **Does continued access require increasing clarity?** Or can someone enjoy
  Friday nights indefinitely without ever defining what this is?
- **Does deeper intimacy pair with any reciprocal commitment?** Or can one
  person receive relationship-level investment while contributing
  dating-level effort?
- **Is ambiguity itself costly?** Or is it a free default that only the person
  who wants clarity has to pay to disrupt?

When the Optionality Tax is near zero — when someone can swipe, match, text,
date, sleep with you, and still keep every option open without ever having to
declare or sacrifice anything — the Mimicry Gap collapses. The cost of
looking serious drops so low that nearly anyone can do it, because
"seriousness" in that structure requires nothing that could not also be
produced by someone who simply enjoys the arrangement.

This is not an argument against dating apps specifically. It is an argument
about what any low-friction, high-option environment does to revelation. The
same logic applies to social scenes, friend groups, or any context where
access to intimacy and attention is abundant and the cost of remaining
undefined is essentially zero. The Optionality Tax is not about technology.
It is about what the structure charges for ambiguity.

The practical point: if you are dating inside a mechanism where optionality is
free, do not expect early signals to carry much weight. The useful response is
not moral outrage. It is structure — not punishing anyone, but making your own
pace legible and letting closeness travel with clarity rather than far ahead of
it. Ask for the things that separate someone who is choosing you from someone
who is merely not leaving yet.

## 7. Alignment Escrow

The Mimicry Gap explains why separation is hard. The Revelation Ladder explains
how to sequence it. The Optionality Tax explains why modern environments make
it worse. But the question most readers actually live with is more immediate:
*how do I keep from getting hurt in the meantime?*

**Alignment Escrow** borrows an answer from a familiar financial structure. In
ordinary escrow, one party does not release the full asset until the other
party has posted credible performance. The buyer does not hand over the money
until the seller delivers the goods; the seller does not deliver until the
money is secured. Neither side bears full exposure before the other has skin in
the game.

In romantic terms, the principle is: **do not let one person move far out ahead
— in time given, in priority, in emotional reliance, in sexual exclusivity, in
a life quietly rearranged around the other — while the other has shown no
comparable willingness to expose themselves in return.**

This is not emotional withholding. It is not "playing hard to get." It is a
matching rule:

- **More time and prioritization** should pair with **more reliability.** If
  you are rearranging your week around someone, they should be showing up
  consistently, not fitting you into whatever is convenient.

- **More intimacy** should pair with **more legibility.** If you are sleeping
  together regularly, the relationship should be getting clearer, not more
  ambiguous. Sex is not a reward to be withheld — but it is not
  informationally neutral either. When sex deepens while clarity stays frozen,
  two people are sharing the same bed with very different understandings of
  what it means, and usually only one of them knows it.

- **More sacrifice** should pair with **visible reciprocity.** If you are
  the one consistently adjusting plans, absorbing inconvenience, or deferring
  your preferences, and the other person is not doing anything remotely
  comparable, the structure is broken. You are the only one putting anything
  at risk.

There is a fourth match the bullet form flattens, and it is the one that does
the most damage when it fails: more emotional dependence should pair with more
chosen commitment. If you find yourself relying on someone for comfort,
support, and stability, there should be evidence that they have chosen that
role — not merely drifted into it through convenience and proximity. Commitment
research distinguishes chosen dedication from several kinds of constraint; some
constraints can keep a couple together, but felt constraint — the sense of
being trapped — is a different signal from dedication that was actually
chosen.[^15] One is chosen. The other is stuck.

The reason Alignment Escrow matters is that most dating damage does not come
from a single dramatic betrayal. It comes from a slow accumulation of
one-sided investment — one person gradually becoming more emotionally exposed,
more logistically entangled, more sexually bonded, more future-oriented, while
the other person retains full freedom and low accountability. By the time the
gap becomes undeniable, the person who invested more has significant sunk costs
and the exit price has become painful.

But sunk costs and future plans are not the same thing.[^16] The fact that you
have already invested heavily does not mean the other person is investing
alongside you. Alignment Escrow works by keeping the question live throughout:
**is the other person's demonstrated behavior roughly tracking the level of
access and dependence I am granting?** If yes, continue. If the gap is
widening, that is information — and it is better to have it at Stage 3 than
at Stage 12.

The bilateral frame matters. Alignment Escrow is not a one-sided screening
script where you test the other person while giving nothing. It is a mutual
structure. Both people should be increasing exposure and reciprocating cost
at roughly matched rates. If you expect the other person to post evidence of
seriousness but refuse to do the same yourself, you are not running Alignment
Escrow. You are running a power play dressed up in economic language.

## 8. The Objection: Romance Is Too Messy For This

The strongest version of the objection is not "this is cold." It is "this is
too clean."

Real dating is volatile. In one young-adult sample, about half reported at
least one reconciliation in a present or most recent relationship, and 57% of
those who had reconciled reported sex with that ex.[^17] Relationships churn.
People contradict themselves. Chemistry distorts judgment. Loneliness overrides
strategy. Someone who looked misaligned in February may genuinely change by
June. Someone who looked perfect on the Revelation Ladder may turn out to be
performing consistency rather than living it.

That is real, and the article does not claim otherwise. Mechanism design does
not eliminate uncertainty. It does not manufacture chemistry. It does not turn
dating into a spreadsheet with a guaranteed output. And even formally elegant
mechanisms can fail in practice if the people inside them do not understand the
structure or respond to its incentives as designed — a point the behavioral
economics literature makes forcefully.[^18]

There is a harder version of the objection, worth stating plainly because it
attacks the remedy rather than the tone. Sometimes added friction does not
reveal the truth — it changes what is being measured. A checkpoint can produce
a relationship that looks clear because one person conceded before they
understood what they wanted. Pressure can manufacture a yes. Performance can
clear a gate that was supposed to test for sincerity. This is real, and it sets
the limit precisely: the goal is information, not compliance. A checkpoint that
extracts a concession has not passed anyone — it has produced a false positive.
Good design is therefore not about making things harder. It is about making
them harder to fake, which is a narrower and more demanding thing. Friction
that reveals is mundane, repeated, and low-stakes. Friction that mostly
produces pressure tells you about the pressure, not the person.

But the narrower claim is still strong: **some structures produce cleaner
information and less one-sided risk than others.** You cannot design away
heartbreak. But you can design away a significant portion of unnecessary
ambiguity — the kind that exists not because the situation is genuinely
uncertain, but because the mechanism does not impose any cost on remaining
vague.

The model earns its keep not by explaining everything, but by improving the
question. Instead of "why did this person hurt me?" — which often leads to
character assassination or self-blame — you get "what did the structure of our
interaction make easy, and what did it make hard?" That is a question with
better answers and more actionable implications.

The limit condition should be explicit: good mechanisms improve revelation and
filtering **at the margin**. They make it more likely that you learn what you
need to know before you are too invested, and less likely that you spend months
in a structure that rewards the person who cares less. They do not promise that
the person who survives every stage gate is your soulmate. They promise that
the information environment is less rigged against you.

That is not everything. But set against the alternative — the months of
ambiguity, the clarity questions that never quite get answered — it is not
nothing.

## 9. How To Design Dating For Better Outcomes

If the article has done its job, you now have a different frame. The question
is no longer only "is this person right for me?" It is "what is this structure
rewarding, and is it rewarding the behavior I actually need to see?" What
follows is less a set of tests to run on someone than a set of choices about
the structure you are willing to be in.

**Read the arrangement, not just the words.** Words are cheap. Structure is
expensive. If someone says they want a relationship but is dating you inside an
arrangement where nothing requires either of you to act like it — no plans
further than three days out, no visibility to anyone in their life, no movement
toward clarity after weeks of intimacy — the structure is telling you something
the words are not. This does not mean talking is worthless; it is the opposite.
An explicit, direct conversation that creates a real expectation — one that
later behavior can confirm or contradict — is among the cheapest honest
mechanisms you have. Structure is what makes that conversation informative. It
is not a substitute for having it.

Stop trying to figure out everything on the first date. Early dates test for
baseline ease and attraction — that is all they can do, and that is fine. What
matters is that what you ask of the relationship grows as your own investment
grows: from "is this pleasant?" to "is this person following through?" to "are
we both willing to give something up for this?" This is not a covert escalation
of demands. It is your own pacing, made legible, instead of a default of
silence quietly doing the deciding.

**Make honesty easier than performance — for both of you.** The default in most
modern dating is ambiguity. Nothing forces a definition; nothing requires a
conversation about trajectory; the relationship can slide forward on momentum
alone. The antidote is not an ultimatum. It is saying what you actually want
early enough that silence stops doing the deciding for you, and letting the
next level of closeness travel with a shared definition rather than out ahead
of one. "We have been seeing each other for two months; I want to know where we
stand" is not clingy. It is the conversation a good structure makes normal.

Watch the balance from the start rather than auditing it once you are already
deep in. Is planning roughly shared? Is initiation roughly shared? Is emotional
availability roughly shared? Small, occasional imbalances are normal and not a
verdict. The signal is a gap that is consistent and widening — observed over
weeks rather than days, and after you have actually said what you want, not
inferred from silence. A real imbalance is far easier to act on at Week 4 than
at Month 8, which is the other half of the point: keep leaving cheap while it
is still cheap. If you build a life-entangled, deeply intimate, half-cohabiting
relationship with someone whose commitment you have never actually verified,
the structure has failed — not because the person is bad, but because it let
dependence outrun evidence. That is not pessimism. It is the most respectful
way to treat your own time and capacity.

**Test with cooperative friction, not manufactured difficulty.** The best
later-stage screen is not a staged loyalty trial. It is an ordinary situation
that requires cooperation under mild difficulty: planning something that takes
effort, navigating a small disagreement, being reliable when it is
inconvenient. And calibrate what you read from it. The friction that tells you
something is the mundane, low-glory, repeated kind — sustained reliability when
no one is watching — not dramatic early sacrifice, which is the easiest thing
in the world to perform for exactly as long as it serves someone. A grand
gesture in week two is weak evidence. Quiet follow-through across two months is
strong evidence. If someone is wonderful in restaurants and absent in
logistics, that gap is information about what they are actually offering versus
what you thought you were building.

## Closing

You are back where you started: three weeks in, maybe five, sitting with the
same warm, vague, structurally ambiguous thing you have been through before.
The dates are fine. The texts are fine. Everything is fine enough to continue
and unclear enough to worry about.

But this time, the question is different. Not "is this person serious?" — you
cannot know that yet, and neither can they, entirely. The question is: **what
would seriousness cost them here?** What has the structure you are both inside
actually required anyone to do? If clarity is free, if exclusivity is
optional, if the whole thing can keep running on pleasant momentum and
noncommittal warmth — then you are not learning anything about their
intentions. You are learning that the mechanism has not yet asked.

People do not get better dating outcomes simply by wanting them harder. They do
not get them by refining their taste, memorizing red flags, or manifesting the
right energy. They get them by building — or insisting on — structures where
truth, effort, and alignment cost less than their opposites. Where ambiguity
is not the free default but a choice with a visible price. Where intimacy and
obligation rise together rather than one running far ahead of the other.

You cannot design certainty. You cannot engineer away the part of love that
is genuinely unknowable. But you can stop participating in mechanisms that
reward the wrong behavior and then wondering why you keep getting the wrong
result.

The answer was never just the person. It was always also the game.
