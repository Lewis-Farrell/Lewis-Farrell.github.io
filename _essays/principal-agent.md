## 1. They Can Like You And Still Be Badly Aligned

You are not on a first date. You are past that. They text every day, sometimes
before you are fully awake. You stay over on weeknights now. They tell you
about work stress and fights with their mother and the thing their ex said that
still bothers them. Their toothbrush is in your bathroom. They have a drawer.

You are giving this person something that functions, in practice, like
partner-level access: scheduling priority, emotional bandwidth, sex, domestic
comfort, the low-grade vulnerability of letting someone see what your apartment
looks like at its worst. You are getting enough warmth back to keep the whole
thing feeling plausible.

Then the question comes up. Where is this going? Maybe you ask it. Maybe a
friend asks on your behalf. Maybe it arrives as your own late-night inventory
of what you are actually building here. The answer is warm but slippery:
something about not wanting to rush, or not being good with labels, or really
enjoying what this is right now. The affection does not vanish. But clarity
does not arrive either.

The puzzle is no longer whether they like you. Of course they do, in some
sense. They keep showing up. They keep reaching for you. The puzzle is why the
arrangement still seems to reward vagueness.

Early dating begins with one question: what does this behavior reveal about the
other person's type? This piece is about the harder question that comes after
access has already been granted: what does the arrangement now reward? That is
the turn that matters. Someone can genuinely like you and still be badly
incentivized to offer clarity, consistency, exclusivity, or real investment
once those things start costing them something.

So the argument here is not that romance is a firm, or that every ambiguous
relationship hides a villain. It is narrower and sharper. Courtship starts as a
screening problem under uncertainty. Some relationships then become incentive
misalignment problems: one person enjoys many of the benefits of closeness
while staying poorly positioned, or poorly motivated, to provide what the other
person actually needs. That is why "Do they like me?" is often the wrong
question. The more useful one is: what does good treatment cost them, and what
does this structure reward?

## 2. The First Problem Is Screening, Not Mind-Reading

Before you can judge alignment, you face a simpler problem: you cannot directly
observe what the other person wants.

Early dating is an information problem. The person across from you knows more
than you do about their own intentions, patience, outside options, and
willingness to bear costs for a shared future. You do not get to inspect any of
that directly. You watch behavior and infer.

That is a screening problem.[^1] Screening is not mind-reading. It is the
practice of noticing or creating conditions that make different types behave
differently. If two kinds of people respond the same way under the current
conditions, you still do not know much.

This is why so much early-dating language is weak evidence. "I really like
you." "I want something serious." "I have never felt this way before." Cheap
talk matters socially, but it does not reliably separate types, because it is
easy for aligned and misaligned people to say the same thing.[^2]

Some statements carry more weight than others. "I deleted my apps." "Come meet
my friends on Saturday." "I told my parents about you." Those claims are either
checkable, reputationally costly, or tied to a near-term commitment that can
fail publicly. They are stronger not because they are verbal, but because they
become expensive to fake.[^3]

The deepest evidence is usually behavioral. What relationship researchers call
pro-relationship acts and willingness to sacrifice tell you more than chemistry
does, because they ask the person to choose the relationship over immediate
convenience.[^4] [^5] Rearranging plans, accepting inconvenience, giving up a more
fun option, showing up when the conversation is uncomfortable: these actions are
informative precisely because they are annoying for someone who is only
dabbling.

This is screening, not therapy. Stop asking only whether they feel something.
Start asking what they have given up, what they have made legible, and what
good treatment has cost them so far. If the answer is "almost nothing," you do
not have a clear signal yet.

## 3. The Trouble Starts When Access Arrives Before Alignment

Now the question changes. It is no longer only, "What does their behavior
reveal?" It becomes, "What does this arrangement reward?"

That shift marks the boundary between ordinary uncertainty and something more
structural. Early on, both people are often unsure. The problem is mutual.
Later, the symmetry can break. One person begins receiving what most people
would reasonably experience as partner-level benefits, while the other person
starts paying partner-level costs, and the arrangement has never been
renegotiated to match that reality.

You know the pattern. One person is getting companionship, sex, emotional
support, scheduling priority, domestic comfort, the warm architecture of
someone else's care. The other person is providing all of that and getting
something back, but not the corresponding clarity or security that would make
the investment safer. Alternatives remain open. Labels remain deferrable. The
future remains conveniently blurry.

That is not just a communication problem. It is an incentive-divergence
problem.

From the vantage of the person enjoying the access, the current structure may
be privately efficient. They get warmth, intimacy, and support while preserving
optionality. Offering clarity would close off alternatives. Making the
relationship public would create accountability. Naming it would turn a soft
arrangement into a legible one with social and emotional consequences. If the
status quo gives them many of the benefits already, ambiguity is not a bug in
the structure. It may be one of the rewards.

> Constraint can hold two people together. Dedication keeps two people actively choosing each other.
{: .pull}

That helps explain why asymmetrically committed relationships are so fragile.
Relationships where one person is meaningfully more invested than the other are
lower in quality and more breakup-prone than relationships where commitment is
more balanced, and the less-committed position is associated with better
perceived alternatives and more ambiguous relationship formation.[^8]

Clarity about the relationship itself matters too. Couples with a clearer sense
of who "we" are report stronger commitment and lower dissolution risk.[^9]
Vagueness is not only a feeling. It is a structural condition, and it predicts
something.

So the arrangement becomes evidence. If it rewards access without clarity,
warmth without legibility, and intimacy without foreclosure, someone is
benefiting from that design. The point is not to moralize it. The point is to
notice that liking you and being aligned with your interests are separate
questions.

## 4. Once Reliance Starts, The Principal-Agent Analogy Earns Its Place

The principal-agent language belongs here, not at the beginning.

Dating partners do not literally delegate authority to each other, so this is
an analogy, not a strict classification. But once one person is relying on the
other for treatment that affects their welfare, the structure becomes close
enough to be useful. One person wants an outcome: clarity, steady effort,
continued choosing, reciprocal care. The other person has discretion over the
quality of that behavior, private knowledge about their motives, and room to
coast in ways that are only partly observable.[^6] [^7]

That is the bounded principal-agent turn. The earlier question was about hidden
type. This one is about hidden action.

Moral hazard enters when the cost of low effort falls after security rises. The
article does not need a cynical monocausal story here. Effort can change after
commitment for many reasons: novelty fades, life gets heavier, desire settles
into routine, people get tired. The mechanism this frame adds is incentive
drift. Once the relationship is stable enough, coasting can become privately
attractive for some people even if they still value the bond.

The insurance analogy is useful only if you map it cleanly. In insurance, the
downside of risk gets shifted onto someone else, behavior becomes imperfectly
observable, and penalties for small hidden deteriorations are blunt. In a
relationship, the more invested partner absorbs more of the downside when
effort declines. What gets shifted is not money but the cost of reduced care,
reduced clarity, reduced initiative, reduced follow-through. Monitoring is poor
because you cannot directly inspect the other person's internal quality of
choosing. You see fragments. You infer the rest.

This is why the dedication-versus-constraint distinction matters so much.
Dedication is active, chosen commitment: I am here because I want this, and I
keep acting like I want this. Constraint is different. Constraint is what makes
leaving costly: a shared apartment, mutual friends, emotional dependence,
children, reputation, time already sunk, a life too interlocked to dismantle
easily.[^10]

Both can keep a relationship going. Only one reliably keeps the treatment good.

That is the real warning. Persistence is not proof. Someone can stay because
they are deeply committed to building a life with you. They can also stay
because leaving has become more inconvenient than continuing. From the outside,
both arrangements can look stable. Inside them, they feel very different.

## 5. Stability Is Not The Same As Dedication

If staying is ambiguous evidence, what is better?

Consistency over time.

People who experience their partner's commitment as volatile are more likely to
end up in breakup than people who experience it as steady.[^11] The issue is not
simply whether the commitment level is high on average. It is whether the
signal is anchored. Intense affection followed by distance, sudden certainty
followed by retreat, bursts of effort followed by long troughs of passivity:
that volatility is information.

Future orientation matters too. Past investments are sunk. Shared trips, years
already spent, previous sacrifices, emotional labor already burned into the
relationship: none of that guarantees anything about what comes next. What
predicts persistence better is orientation toward shared future plans rather
than mere attachment to shared history.[^12] Backward-looking commitment can keep
people in place. Forward-looking commitment keeps them building.

When dedication is real, it usually looks something like this:

> - alternatives get closed that could easily have been kept open
> - clarity is offered before repeated pressure makes it unavoidable
> - sacrifice appears in ordinary life, not just in dramatic speeches
> - future plans become concrete rather than permanently deferred
> - effort continues even after the relationship is stable enough to coast

Constraint can hold two people together. Dedication keeps two people actively
choosing each other.

That is why patterns beat moments. One grand gesture can be theater. A stable
pattern of costly, partner-favoring behavior is harder to fake, and more useful
to live with.

## 6. Not Every Ambiguity Is Incentive Misalignment

The strongest rival explanation deserves a real hearing.

Not every unclear relationship is organized around bad incentives. Some are
organized around uncertainty, mismatch, immaturity, stress, or attachment
patterns that predate the current relationship. Avoidant attachment matters
here because it can produce almost the same surface behavior as incentive
misalignment: resistance to labels, intermittent withdrawal, verbal warmth
paired with behavioral hesitation, discomfort when the relationship starts
asking for legibility.

That overlap matters because the interventions differ. Incentive misalignment
calls for changing the structure: reducing unreciprocated access, demanding
clarity, raising the cost of ambiguity. Avoidant attachment often calls for the
opposite: less pressure, more pacing, more security, fewer forced declarations.
Use the wrong intervention on the wrong problem and you make the situation
worse.

So how do you tell them apart?

The cleanest first cut is whether the pattern is relationship-specific or
cross-relational. If someone has a long history of resisting commitment with
everyone, even when outside options are weak and ambiguity is not buying them
much, attachment theory earns the presumption. If someone has committed
elsewhere, can be legible when it suits them, and is specifically resisting
clarity with you while still enjoying the same benefits, the incentive lens
earns its place.

Another clue is what exactly seems costly to them. If pressure itself triggers
shutdown regardless of who is applying it, that points one way. If the costly
part is closure of alternatives, public accountability, or loss of optionality,
that points another. The first pattern says, "closeness dysregulates me." The
second says, "ambiguity still serves me."

The model here is not a universal theory of romantic confusion. It is a tool
for the narrower case where one person's incentive structure is materially
better served by keeping the other person in an underdefined arrangement. When
that is the structure, the incentive lens explains something attachment talk
often leaves blurry: why warmth can coexist with treatment that never becomes
reliably safe to build on.

## 7. The Screen: Ask What The Arrangement Rewards

This screen is not for the third date. It is not a machine for pathologizing
ordinary asymmetry. Use it when three conditions are already in place: the
relationship has been running long enough for expectations to become
reasonable, one person is already receiving substantial partner-level benefits,
and clarity has been sought or made relevant more than once and consistently
deflected.

Under those conditions, the central question is:

**What does this arrangement reward, and what does aligned behavior cost them?**

Break that into five smaller questions.

**What are they getting from the current structure right now?**

List it plainly: companionship, sex, emotional support, scheduling priority,
domestic comfort, status, validation, the steadying effect of being cared for.
If they are receiving most of the functional benefits of partnership already,
the arrangement is working for them somehow. The question is whether it is
working for you on comparable terms.

**What would clarity, exclusivity, or public legibility cost them?**

This is the most directly revealing test, especially when the answer repeats
across multiple moments rather than one charged conversation. If labels,
exclusivity, visibility, or accountability would barely change their life and
they still resist them, the resistance itself is telling you something.
Ambiguity is serving a purpose.

**Which options have they actually closed, not just described?**

Words about exclusivity are still cheap unless visible foreclosure follows.
Have they removed themselves from dating platforms? Changed routines that
preserved optionality? Accepted social consequences that are annoying to
reverse? The signal is not in the declaration alone but in the structure of
what they have given up. Signals that leave full optionality intact carry far
less weight than signals that close real options.[^2] [^3]

**Are you seeing dedication, or just accumulating constraints?**

Shared logistics, routine, social entanglement, and rising exit costs can keep
people together without keeping them generous. Ask whether the relationship is
being actively chosen or merely continued because departure has become costly.
Constraint predicts persistence. Dedication predicts the quality of the
continuing treatment.

**What happens when good treatment requires inconvenience, sacrifice, or
foregone alternatives?**

This is the deepest test. Aligned people reveal themselves most clearly when
the right thing is mildly expensive. They rearrange plans. They offer clarity
that narrows their own options. They choose transparency over comfort. They
show up for the hard conversation instead of soothing the moment and escaping
the issue.

Misaligned people often reveal themselves in exactly the same moments, only in
reverse. The affection remains, but the costly behavior does not follow. They
like you in the ways that are free and resist you in the ways that cost
something.

> **The alignment screen in summary**
>
> 1. Map the benefits: who is getting what from the current structure?
> 2. Price clarity: what would legibility, exclusivity, or commitment cost?
> 3. Check foreclosure: which options have actually been closed?
> 4. Distinguish dedication from constraint: are they choosing, or just staying?
> 5. Watch the costly moments: what happens when good treatment becomes
>    inconvenient?

The screen also runs both ways. If you want an aligned partner, you cannot ask
the other person for costly legibility while keeping your own intentions cheap.
You have to make your own alignment easy to verify: close options you would
want them to close, offer clarity before being cornered into it, and accept the
small inconveniences that make your seriousness visible.

That matters for another reason too. Sometimes both people are quietly using
ambiguity. One wants access without obligation; the other wants emotional
security without the risk of an explicit ask. In that case, the structure is
not hiding one exploiter and one innocent reader. It is stabilizing mutual
optionality. The screen is still useful. It just indicts both sides.

## Closing

Come back to the arrangement you started with: the texts, the sleepovers, the
drawer, the small possessions migrating across apartments. The person who likes
you enough to keep you close, and vaguely enough to keep the future open.

The useful question was never simply whether they like you. Liking is abundant.
Liking starts arrangements. It does not tell you whether the arrangement is
safe to build a life inside.

The sharper question is structural. What does this arrangement reward? What
does good treatment cost them? Which options have they closed, and which have
they kept carefully open? Are they choosing you, or have they merely stopped
choosing against you?

Attraction explains why the arrangement started. Alignment explains whether it
deserves to continue.
