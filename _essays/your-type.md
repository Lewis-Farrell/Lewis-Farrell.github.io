You are across a table from someone new, and something familiar has already
arrived. Not their face - a quality. The way they deflect a compliment with a
half-smile that dares you to try harder. The slight performative edge in how
they hold your attention, as if they are deciding, in real time, whether you
are worth the effort of being charming. You recognize this. You have been here
before. You know how this kind of evening accelerates - the conspiratorial
lean-in, the joke that tests a boundary, the point where the conversation stops
being about what either of you is saying and starts being about whether you are
both going to admit what is happening. You lean in anyway.

This is not attraction finding its target. It is a prior finding a match - not
in the strict Bayesian sense, but in the ordinary sense that an old template
has reached your attention before the evidence has. Your "type" is not just a
list of people you happen to like. It is a bundle of cues that tells you who to
lean toward before you know the slower things that decide whether life with
them will actually work.

This article is about what happens when that template becomes hard to update.
The argument is narrower than "everyone dates the wrong people." It is about
the cases where a type is built mostly from fast cues and familiar intensity,
where the search keeps circling those cues, and where each failure updates the
story about the person more readily than the pattern itself. The useful
question is not whether you should abandon your type. It is whether you are
treating it as a rough hypothesis to test or a preference to defend.

---

### 1. There Are Two Kinds of Type

What people call a type often mixes two different things. One is the fast
archetypal type: a look, a social rhythm, an emotional temperature, a way of
making attention feel scarce. The other is the substantive assortative type:
religion, geography, family plans, education, class background, or a shared way
of organizing life. Those latter traits are not trivial. They can be crude but
useful screens for real compatibility.

That distinction matters because this article is mostly about the first case,
and about the second only when it is being used as a noisy proxy for the first.
If you keep dating teachers because you want a partner with a school schedule
and a similar view of family life, that may be rational sorting. If you keep
dating the same emotionally withholding flirt because the tension feels like
chemistry, that is a different problem.

Some "types" are also social inheritances rather than innocent heuristics. A
person's recurring attractions can encode community fit, but also class habit,
status aspiration, or prejudice dressed up as taste. The point here is not to
resolve that moral terrain. It is to notice that the word "type" covers too
many things, and that only some of them create the pattern this article is
trying to explain.

Even so, people really do carry stable templates. Gerlach and colleagues found
that ideal-partner preferences prospectively predicted characteristics of later
partners.^1 Types are not fantasy. They do some real sorting work.

But they do narrower work than people often imagine. Eastwick, Finkel, and
Eagly found that ideal-partner matching helped predict romantic interest when
people were evaluating profiles in the abstract, but the effect disappeared
after live interaction.^2 Fast cues predict attraction more readily than they
predict the slower traits that matter for compatibility. They get you to the
table. They do not tell you nearly enough about what happens once you are
actually there.

The useful economic idea is simple: dating is a learning process, not a string
of isolated taste expressions.^3 Each flirtation, date, and relationship is a
chance to collect information. The real question is whether your search is
producing new information or just rewarding what already feels legible.

### 2. How the Template Gets Set

One source is obvious: a few early relationships matter far more than they
deserve to. Not always your first relationship, but often one of the first that
felt like this - the first time intensity arrived wearing a particular face and
a particular emotional rhythm. Dugan and colleagues, using data that followed
participants from childhood into adulthood, found that early interpersonal
experience had enduring links to adult attachment orientations.^4 Fraley and
Roisman supply the necessary correction: those effects are real, but weaker and
less deterministic than pop attachment talk usually suggests.^5

That is exactly why the template can be misleading. It is not that one early
relationship permanently wires you. It is that important impressions often get
set on absurdly little evidence. The first relationship that felt electrifying,
or costly, or hard to win can become the benchmark for what a relationship is
supposed to feel like long before you know whether that feeling predicts
stability, generosity, or ease.

Another plausible source is simple market feedback. Some kinds of people reply
to you more. Some social worlds keep putting you in front of the same kind of
person. Some versions of you - your profile, your clothes, your jokes, your
friend group, the places you go - make you easier for certain people to read
and reward. After enough repetitions, familiarity starts to feel like truth.

> Fast cues predict attraction more readily than they predict the slower traits that matter for compatibility.
{: .pull}

This is one reason types become sticky even without any grand theory. You get
smoother with what you have already practiced. You know how to flirt with this
kind of person, how to read the pauses, what usually comes next. The type is no
longer just a filter on who seems attractive. It is a playbook. And playbooks
are hard to give up because competence feels a lot like evidence.

The point is not destiny. It is path dependence. A type can start from a small
number of salient experiences, then get reinforced by the slice of the market
you keep encountering and the social competence you accumulate inside it.

### 3. Why the Pattern Under-Updates

Once the template exists, the problem is not that it never gets challenged. It
is that the challenge is often weak, late, or too easy to explain away.

Start with sampling. If you mostly swipe on the same cues, respond to the same
energy, and pursue the same kind of early charge, you are not running a neutral
search. You are repeatedly drawing from a narrow part of the distribution and
then treating the outcome as if it told you something general.

That narrowing does not happen only at the moment of choice. Your profile and
the contexts where you meet people help shape which slice of the market you
encounter in the first place. This is a smaller claim than "you designed the
whole market," but it is large enough to matter. If you foreground polish,
status, and ironic detachment, you will hear back from people who like polish,
status, and ironic detachment. If you spend your romantic life in the same app
ecology, the same neighborhood scenes, and the same social performance, the
candidate pool will start to look repetitive before you even start selecting
from it.

Self-awareness does not solve this by itself. Whyte and Torgler found a
measurable gap between stated preferences and actual contact behavior in online
dating.^6 People can describe their pattern and still keep enacting it. Anyone
who has ever announced, with complete sincerity, that they are "trying
something different this year" and then felt their attention lock onto the same
familiar profile understands the problem.

The last part is more inferential, but it matters. Fincham and Bradbury found
that how partners explain relationship difficulties was longitudinally related
to relationship satisfaction.^7 That does not prove that people preserve their
type by blaming each failed relationship on the individual. It does suggest why
this kind of updating is hard. When a familiar relationship fails, the easiest
story is usually "wrong person," not "wrong pattern." The person absorbs the
blame. The template survives mostly intact.

That is how repetition can feel like evidence even when it is doing very little
real learning. The search keeps overweighting what it already knows how to see.
The disconfirming evidence arrives slowly, and even then it often lands on the
person, not the pattern.

### 4. Why Apps Can Make It Worse

App dating did not invent types. It did make visible cues easier to sort on and
easier to perform at scale. Pew's recent reporting shows that online dating is
now a mainstream channel in the United States.^11 That matters because the more
of your search happens through profiles, the more power accrues to whatever can
be read quickly from a profile.

And people do manipulate exactly those surfaces. Toma, Hancock, and Ellison
found that online-dating profile inaccuracies were common but usually small:
men were more likely to overstate height, women to understate weight, and
photographs were among the least accurate parts of the profile.^8 Toma and
Hancock later found that lower-attractiveness users were especially likely to
enhance visible physical cues rather than unrelated traits like income or
occupation.^9 People lie where your type looks first.

Ellison, Hancock, and Toma offer the right frame for this: a dating profile is
better understood as a promise to be tested later than as a complete factual
document.^10 That does not mean every promising profile is deceptive. It means
that if your type leans heavily on visible cues, app environments make it
easier for those cues to pull your attention before the harder evidence arrives.

This is the adverse-selection wrinkle in its bounded form. The claim is not
that apps have ruined dating or that legible types always attract worse
matches. It is that a highly visible type becomes easier to mimic. Someone who
fits your template may genuinely fit the deeper thing you want. They may also
be especially good at presenting the visible version of it. Those are not the
same.

There is an important counterpoint. For people in thin markets - small towns,
niche communities, minority religious scenes, queer subcultures with limited
offline density, or geographies where local options are sparse - larger pools
and clearer filters can genuinely help. The point is not that app legibility is
bad in every case. It is that when your romantic mistakes are already driven by
fast, familiar cues, apps can hand those cues even more leverage on both sides
of the screen.

### 5. The Strongest Objection

The strongest objection is not "types are fake." It is that repeated same-type
dating may be rational. If your recurring type bundles religion, ambition
tempo, education, class fit, location, humor, and desire for children, then of
course you keep ending up with similar people. Efficient search often looks
like repetition from the outside.

That objection is good, and the article should concede it directly. Gerlach's
findings are exactly what you would expect if many people do carry useful
templates.^1 The right contrast is not "type bad, randomness good." It is
between a template that keeps being tested against new evidence and one that
keeps getting protected by familiar cues.

The rough check I am proposing is within-type variance, and it should be read
as a heuristic, not as a validated diagnostic instrument. Small samples distort
it. Life stages change. You are one of the variables. Still, it can reveal
something your stories about individual exes often hide.

If your type keeps generating meaningfully different outcomes - some easy, some
hard, some healthy, some obviously not - then the template may be tracking
something real, even if imperfectly. But if the names, jobs, and cities change
while the structure keeps staying the same - rush, ambiguity, instability,
collapse - then familiarity may be doing more work than compatibility.

Most people never run the check because they experience each relationship as a
separate drama. The stories are separate. The structure often is not.

---

### 6. What To Do With That

None of this requires abandoning your type or forcing yourself to date people
you do not want. It requires producing better evidence.

First, read your history as a cluster, not just as a string of individuals. If
the same cues keep leading to the same ending, stop treating each ending as a
totally fresh surprise. That is not cynicism. It is pattern recognition.

Second, change the evidence before you change the opinion. If your search keeps
surfacing the same kind of person, the answer is not just to swear, once again,
that you will "be open-minded." Change where you meet people. Change what you
foreground. Change the kinds of first impressions you reward. Sampling
different people is the only way to find out whether your old template was
tracking something deep or just something familiar.

Third, stop mistaking intensity for signal. Arriaga and colleagues found that
fluctuations in perceived partner commitment predicted breakup better than
commitment level alone.^12 The rollercoaster is not proof that something
important is happening. It may be proof that something unstable is happening.
Familiar tension can feel rich because you already know how to play it.

Fourth, notice whether the relationship is being chosen or merely carried
forward by momentum. Rhoades, Stanley, and Markman distinguish dedication from
constraint: relationships can persist through both, but persistence is not the
same as satisfaction or freely chosen commitment.^13 A relationship can survive
because it is good. It can also survive because leaving is expensive, socially
messy, or humiliating. Those are not the same achievement.

This is why the closing advice is plainer than "expand your horizons." Ask a
better question. Not "is this my type?" and not even "am I excited?" Ask: what
kind of evidence would actually change my mind about this person, and am I
putting myself in a position to receive it?

That recognition across the table is not meaningless. It may be genuine
information. It is just early information, and early information is exactly
where your type has always been strongest.

Your type may be right. But if your search keeps rewarding the same visible
cues, the same familiar intensity, and the same easy explanations after it goes
wrong, you will keep learning the same lesson in slightly different outfits.
