## 1. "Yes, Fine"

The question comes sideways, the way it usually does, somewhere between the second glass and the point where the evening stops being about the evening. Maybe you are the one who asks whether it's taken care of. Maybe you are the one asked. Or maybe nobody asks at all, and one of you says *don't worry, it's sorted*, in the voice people use for having booked the table. There is a pause about the length of a breath. Then the answer: *yes, fine.* It comes out easily. Perhaps a little too easily, or a little too fast; you could not say which, and you are not going to check. What is being handled in that breath is not small. It is the chance of a pregnancy neither of you is planning, and for a second it is in the room with you both. Then it isn't, and nobody brings it back.

The word *fine* is doing a lot of work, and it does different work on each side of the bed. If you said it, it has a tail you can already feel: a prescription to renew, a reminder on your phone, an appointment to fit around a working week, something paid at a counter, and whatever the arrangement is like to live with, which is known from the inside and nowhere else. If you heard it, *fine* is a relief and also a door gently closed. You may not have stopped to ask what it costs, partly because it sounded settled and partly because asking can sound like doubting. Neither of you said anything untrue. Neither of you was pushed.

If anyone ever asks how the two of you landed here, the account will be short, and it will be accurate: *we both agreed.* Nobody objected. Nobody walked out. And yet it is not obvious what the agreement covered. Whether the counter, the calendar and the appointments came with it, or only the method. Whether the things only one of you can feel were part of the deal, or simply never mentioned. Whether *fine* meant *I would choose this anyway* or *this is what was on offer*. Whether it will ever come up again. You both said yes. What, exactly, did you say yes to?

## 2. What This Essay Will Show

This essay takes *we both agreed* at its word and asks what it proves. Read as economics, an agreement is evidence, but evidence of something narrower than it sounds, and the gap between the two is the subject here.

Four things follow. First, what an agreement certifies, and what it cannot. Second, the costs of prevention sort by two questions: could a payment from the other partner make up for this cost, and can the other partner even see it? That sorting decides whether a lopsided arrangement can be repaired. Third, when the split is the cheapest way to carry the burden, when it is fair, when it is both and when it is neither — kept as two separate tests throughout. Fourth, why the partner with more to lose can end up bearing more than the other even when the other would have borne it more cheaply.

Three boundaries, each stated here only. This is a model, not a measurement: every claim below is a result in a simple model, an assumption stated as one, or a gap in the evidence. The model case is a pair in which one partner can become pregnant and the other cannot; other pairs are outside the model. The partners are called X and Y — roles, not sexes; what matters is who bears which cost and who stands to lose what. A pregnancy neither partner planned enters only as a cost to each of them if prevention fails; the essay takes no position on what that cost should be or on what anyone should do about it, and it says nothing about which method anyone should use. The examples in this essay are illustrative and could run the other way: the structure is symmetric even when the example is not.

## 3. The Bill

Start with the bill, before any theory, because the theory needs to know what is on it.

Preventing a pregnancy nobody is planning costs something, and in the model's terms the costs come in five kinds. There is money: whatever is paid at a counter or a clinic. There is the body: whatever a method does to the person using it, from side effects to procedures. There is medical time: appointments, check-ups, being assessed, since whether a method suits a given person depends on their health and is decided clinically. There is the cognitive part: remembering, tracking, refilling, noticing that the supply runs out on a Sunday. And there is risk, the exposure left over after prevention, because no method eliminates it.

A list like that invites a tour, and the tour would teach nothing. What does the work is two questions put to each item. Could the other partner make up for this cost with a payment? And can the other partner see it?

Money passes both. A payment repays a payment at par, and a receipt is visible to anyone who asks. The appointment shows in the calendar, and the travel and the time off can be covered, at least in part.

The body fails the first question and mostly the second. Nobody can be paid to have not had a side effect, and how a method feels is known from the inside. The cognitive cost fails both: the work of keeping track is done in one head, and a gift does not hand it back. Risk sits apart from the others. It is not a cost of prevention but the reason anyone bears the rest, and each partner's share of it is that partner's *stake*: what each stands to lose if prevention fails.

So the bill sorts into three piles that the rest of the essay uses. Costs a payment can repay. Costs it cannot. And costs the other partner cannot see. The same item can sit in two piles at once, and the interesting items mostly do.

Who receives the bill? Of the methods the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention lists, all but two, the male condom and vasectomy, are used by or act on the partner who can become pregnant[^1]. That is a statement about the list, not about any couple, and it says nothing about who pays the money part, who remembers, or who holds the stake. The partner who bears the method and the partner exposed to more of the residual risk can be the same person or different people. The model keeps the two roles apart.

The evidence behind this ledger is thin and uneven: no authoritative figure for what prevention costs couples in money, no validated couple-level measure of the cognitive load, no estimate of how lopsided the burden is on average, and the research behind this essay looked mostly at hormonal methods. None of that needs a number to do its job. The question is not how heavy the bill is but whether, once it has landed, a payment could move it, and whether the other partner can read it.

## 4. The World Where "Agreed" Is Enough

Imagine, for one section, a couple for whom *we both agreed* settles everything.

Two partners share one recurring burden: the bill from the last section. Each would lose something if prevention failed, and they would not lose the same amount. Now give them two powers couples do not normally have. Each can see exactly what the other bears, side effects and mental bookkeeping included. And either can pay the other exactly, in something both value one for one: money, favours, other jobs in a shared life, anything that moves value across without loss.

What would they do? They would find the cheapest way to carry the burden, because any other arrangement leaves value on the table that both could share. Whoever finds the burden lighter bears it, and the other pays enough to make the deal worth it to both. What decides who bears it is not who has more to lose. Who has more to lose decides who pays whom, and how much. Stakes move money. They do not move the burden.

This is the benchmark economists associate with Ronald Coase: when bargaining is costless and rights are clear, the parties reach a cost-minimising outcome whatever the starting point, and the starting point decides only who ends up better off[^2]. Coase's own emphasis ran the other way. In the world he cared about, the costs of striking and enforcing bargains are positive and they matter, so the frictionless case is a foil, not his description of how things go. That is how it is used here. What each partner could do instead — leave, find a different arrangement, rely on a different method — is what economists call the walk-away or disagreement point, and it determines who pays whom once who bears it is settled.

The cost-minimising choice of bearer also has a name from law: what Calabresi called the cheapest avoider of accident costs[^3], now commonly the "cheapest cost avoider." Calabresi framed it as a rule for assigning liability, with a court to do the assigning. Here it is an analogy, and a narrow one. It says who bears at least cost. It says nothing about who ought to.

A small example makes the claim concrete. The numbers in this essay are abstract units, chosen to show the structure of the argument; none is an estimate of anything. Suppose bearing the whole burden costs X 8 units and Y 6, so Y is the cheaper bearer. The table varies how much prevention is worth to each partner over and above what each could walk away to:

| Worth to X over walk-away | Worth to Y over walk-away | Who bears | Payment | Each ends with (X, Y) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 10 | 4 | Y, all of it | X pays Y 6 | 4, 4 |
| 4 | 10 | Y, all of it | none | 4, 4 |
| 20 | 1 | Y, all of it | X pays Y 12.5 | 7.5, 7.5 |
| 6 | 6 | Y, all of it | X pays Y 3 | 3, 3 |

Y bears in every row. The rows differ only in the payment, and where X has far more at stake, X pays far more, in money. The payment here splits the saving equally; any split both would accept leaves the bearer unchanged.

The example works only because of five conditions that are jointly demanding, and each is a door the rest of the essay opens:

**What the benchmark needs.** (1) Payments are possible and enforceable at whatever size the deal requires. (2) Each partner can see the other's costs. (3) A payment makes up for a cost one for one. (4) The arrangement can be revisited at no cost. (5) Each partner's walk-away is known to both. Conditions 1 and 3 give way in Section 5; condition 2 in Section 7; condition 4 in Section 8; Section 6 is about a different limit — what an outsider can read off the agreement when all five conditions hold for the partners themselves.

Only where all five hold does *we both agreed* certify that the burden landed where it costs least. Whether any real couple meets them nobody can say: there are no data on payments within couples for this good, so the benchmark cannot be tested here. It is used for what benchmarks are good for: naming exactly what has to fail.

## 5. When a Payment Cannot Make Up for It

Take the payments away, and the deal with them, and ask the simplest question each partner can ask alone: is protection worth its burden to me if I bear it myself?

If the answer is yes for one partner and no for the other, the first bears it and the second benefits. That is the whole outcome, and it has an awkward property. The bearer is whoever's stake exceeds that partner's own burden, not whoever finds the burden lighter. Suppose prevention is worth 10 to X and 0.8 to Y, and bearing costs X 8 and Y 1. X bears it, because 10 beats 8. Y would not, because 0.8 does not beat 1. The pair ends up with 2.8 units of value where Y bearing would have produced 9.8. The difference is extra summed burden — what the essay calls waste, using the commensurability assumption of Section 4's third condition — and only a payment from X to Y could recover it.

The structure has a textbook name. Each partner's choice ignores the benefit it gives the other, which makes protection a positive externality: external to each partner's decision, though internal to the pair. It is a joint cost allocation problem inside one couple. The term describes a structure, not a charge. The same arrangement arises with no ill will on either side, and the model is silent on intent.

So let them talk. They bargain over how to share the burden, how often each bears it or how its parts are split, under one realistic restriction: part of the burden cannot be repaid by a payment. A payment does not cancel a side effect, and a gift does not hand back the habit of keeping track. The payer may be unable, or the bearer unwilling, to settle a body's costs in money.

What split do they reach? This essay borrows a result rather than deriving it: in Nash's bargaining solution, worked through in this series' essay on making a relationship official, the split depends on what each partner could walk away to[^4]. Applied here with no payments at all, it gives a clean prediction. The share a partner bears rises with that partner's net gain from prevention, measured from the walk-away, and falls with how heavy that partner finds the burden. The partner with more to lose concedes more of the burden to secure the protection. Both choose freely. The result holds in the model, in two versions that follow.

If the model's self-interest assumption is too stark — if each partner partly internalises the other's burden, which the model does not capture — the gap between "agreed" and "cheapest" narrows even without a payment. What survives even then is the identification limit (Section 6) and the hidden-cost layer (Section 7), which require only that costs differ in observability, not that partners are indifferent to each other.

Return to the first row of the table: bearing costs X 8 and Y 6; prevention is worth 10 to X and 4 to Y. With free payments, Y bears all of it and X pays. With no payments, X ends up bearing about 79 per cent of the burden, as the more expensive bearer, and 1.58 units of extra summed burden are lost that a payment would have saved. Every split in the no-payment case is on the efficient frontier in the usual sense — no change helps one partner without hurting the other — so the 1.58 is not a mutual gain left unclaimed. It is waste only relative to the cost-minimising corner. The partner with the larger loss rationally bears more, and the rational arrangement is the wasteful one.

> The partner with the larger loss rationally bears more, and the rational arrangement is the wasteful one.
{: .pull}

Each unit of payment cap moves about 15 percentage points of the burden from the expensive bearer to the cheap one, until the corner is reached. Once the cap covers the needed payment, nothing is wasted.

Two labels belong to this result. Economists call value that cannot be passed across one for one *non-transferable utility*, and the distinction is standard in the economics of marriage and the family[^5]. Applying it to a bodily and cognitive burden is this essay's own step: no source was found applying it to contraception or to any bodily cost. That bodily and cognitive costs carry a low payment cap is an assumption, and there is no evidence on how low.

This is not a claim that couples leave mutual gains lying around. Household economists assume couples reach the efficient frontier, and every split here is on it. The question is which point. The efficient standard, in the cost-minimising sense, is the cheapest-bearer corner, and reaching it requires a payment. That is the distinction the essay rests on.

A correction, because readers often bring a different mechanism. Within expected-utility reasoning, aversion to risk only makes the pregnancy cost more in that partner's own terms: a larger stake, not a separate force. Roth and Rothblum showed that when an agreement can include a gamble that may leave a player worse off than disagreement, a more risk-averse player may need to be offered better terms[^6]. The mechanism here is the ratio of net gain to burden, plus the walk-away. Nerves enter only as part of the stake; in this model, curvature in the cost of bearing would add a separate effect that is not modelled.

## 6. Same Observation, Four Readings

Now stand outside a couple. You see who bears the burden, and you know both agreed. How much of the story can you recover?

Start with what *agreed* certifies. In the running example, every share for X from about a third of the burden up to all of it is one both partners would accept over walking away. Agreeing to any point in that range is a statement that each preferred it to the alternative each saw. It is not a statement that the point is the cheapest, or that the burdens are equal, or that they track the benefits. The range is wide, and *we both agreed* places the couple somewhere inside it without saying where.

Take two couples in which X bears everything and both agreed:

*Couple A.* Bearing costs X 6 and Y 8; prevention is worth 10 to X and 4 to Y. X is the cheaper bearer, and the arrangement is the cheapest one. Nothing is wasted.

*Couple B.* Bearing costs X 8 and Y 6; prevention is worth 10 to X but only 1.5 to Y over what Y could walk away to. Y is the cheaper bearer, but without a payment Y would accept at most a quarter of the burden, so the cheaper arrangement was never on the table. Two units are wasted.

From outside, A and B are the same couple. The pattern "the partner with more at stake bears more, and both agreed" is consistent with four readings. The cheaper bearer bears. Or the partner who stands to lose more bears. Or the partner with the worse alternative bears. Or the partner who wants to bear does. All four run through one quantity: each partner's net gain from prevention relative to their burden, compared across the two. The observation pins down that comparison at the corner, and nothing behind it. So the partner who bears the method can say *I insisted*, the other can say *I was never asked*, and both can be accurate reports of a free choice that say nothing about whether the split was the cheapest or the fair one.

Beyond those four model-internal accounts, there are alternatives that require no bargaining at all: the technology supplies few options for one partner, so the arrangement is settled before the couple meets; social norms or clinical practice direct one partner toward managing it; a method was already in place before the relationship began. The model cannot distinguish these from the preference account (H6) any more than it can separate the four model-internal readings. They are a limit on what the observational data can show.

What would tell the stories apart is three numbers, not one: the cost each partner bears, the stake each holds, and the walk-away each has. The observable alone cannot supply them. A model can say which number matters for which question, and Section 12 turns them into questions a couple can ask.

Some of the rival accounts could in principle be separated by data. Hidden costs have one experimental analogue, in Zambia (Section 7). Whether first arrangements simply persist would need couples followed over time, and no such study was found. Whether the asymmetry is smaller than it looks would need a full accounting of costs that does not exist. So the essay offers no ranking of the accounts, and no estimate of how often each holds.

## 7. What Only the Bearer Can See

The partner who bears the burden knows how heavy it is. The other partner does not, and cannot check. Suppose acknowledgement, or help, follows what the bearer reports. What can the report carry?

Less than it should. A cost that can be claimed but not verified can be claimed by anyone, so the report carries only as much information as misreporting costs. At zero cost this is cheap talk in the technical sense: saying "this is hard" costs nothing to say falsely, so a bearer for whom the burden is light and one for whom it is heavy say the same thing. At positive misreporting cost the problem is the same with a price on exaggeration: the premium a heavier report earns is capped by the cost of claiming it falsely. Only when faking would cost as much as the burden itself does the report carry the full weight. The limits of this kind of communication are worked through in this series' essay on partners' friends[^10].

The result is narrower than it sounds. It is a statement about who is short-changed, not about kindness: the other partner can be generous and fair-minded and still have no way to hear the difference. On average the listener is right, unless the listener assumes the burden is lighter than it is; only that bias — which is assumed here and has not been measured — turns the result into a shortfall on average. And the shortfall is in acknowledgement, not in contraception. The burden is still borne. What goes missing is the recognition of how much it weighs, and with it the case for any payment that might move it.

One study speaks to the general point that who can see contraceptive use matters. Ashraf, Field and Lee posit that fertility decisions involve moral hazard because most contraception can be perfectly observed only by the woman; in this essay's roles, by the partner who uses it. In a field experiment in Zambia, women given contraceptive access with their husbands used concealable methods less and gave birth more often than women given access alone, and the women given access alone reported lower well-being[^9]. That is one setting, with a different question at its centre, and it is cited here for the narrow point that private observability has consequences; the study is about privately observed use, a question this essay keeps out of scope.

This section is held at low confidence. The cost types are unmeasured, there is no validated couple-level measure of the mental load of contraception, and dividing bearers into two types is a simplification made to show the mechanism, not a description of anyone.

## 8. The Arrangement Nobody Revisited

The first arrangement is made with whatever the partners knew at the time. Then things change: a side effect appears, a job moves, the risk starts to feel different. Raising the arrangement again costs whoever raises it something: a hard conversation, the risk of sounding dissatisfied. Each partner raises it only when what they would gain exceeds that cost.

An existing split moves only under a credible threat to walk away, and a threat is credible only when someone would be better off leaving. With no payments, any change to the split that helps one partner costs the other the same amount, so no change is good for both. A band around the fresh optimum goes unchallenged: the band is wider when raising the subject is costly and narrower when the burden is heavy. In the running example, a cost of raising it of half a unit to each partner leaves unchallenged everything from about 71 to 85 per cent of the burden on X. If the cost of raising it falls mainly on the partner who bears the burden, the band leans against that partner. That is an assumption, with no evidence either way. And if cross-domain payments are available — other things the partners trade in the relationship — that is a separate channel from the bodily payment cap, and it would need its own assumption to be limited in the same way.

The starting point for any of this is often just the obvious first arrangement, what game theorists call a focal point: the option both parties coordinate on without a prior agreement[^11]. The general pull of the status quo is documented in individual choices of health and retirement plans, and in questionnaire scenarios of which a replication supported three of four[^12]. No study of first-method persistence in couples was found; the forced-re-solve assumption (that a challenge resets the split to what the partners would choose today) is strong.

This section, like the last, is held at low confidence.

## 9. Where the Argument Fails

The argument has edges, and they can be stated as conditions under which *we both agreed* is good enough after all:

| # | Condition | What it does to the argument | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Comparable total costs | No asymmetry to explain; the essay's point has no bite | None on how lopsided burdens are on average |
| 2 | Payable and visible costs | The Section 4 benchmark applies; who bears affects distribution, not the cost-minimising assignment | No data on payments within couples |
| 3 | A real outside option | Agreement is a good revealed-preference signal | No measure |
| 4 | Bearing is partly a benefit | Net cost of bearing falls; bearing may be cheapest and preferred | Direction supported for the pill in one US survey[^14]; size unknown |
| 5 | Revisited arrangement | The band of Section 8 closes | No couple evidence |
| 6 | Lighter or less lopsided costs | Every effect shrinks with the burden | Method- and jurisdiction-dependent; gap |
| 7 | A shared-cost method | The model applies at the margin | Methods borne by the other partner exist on the CDC list[^1] |

Three of these matter most.

The bearer may get something from bearing. Control over the risk is worth something; so is a reliability one trusts, and, for some methods, benefits that have nothing to do with contraception. A US survey of pill users found that more than half take it at least partly for reasons other than contraception[^14]. If that benefit is large enough, bearing is both the cheapest arrangement and the preferred one. How large it is, nobody has measured, and from outside it looks exactly like a lower cost, a larger stake, or a weaker walk-away (Section 6).

The bearer may have had a real choice. If the partner who bears could have said no at little cost and still said yes, the agreement is good evidence, and the essay's point has no bite. That is said plainly.

The costs may be payable and visible, or comparable. Then who bears is a question about who ends up better off rather than about waste, and the essay's thesis has no bite either. The essay does not claim the burden is always misplaced. It claims that agreement alone cannot tell you whether it is.

The model has limits of its own, stated once. Costs rise in a straight line with the share borne. Shares are fixed, not settled by a coin toss, which would bring in different effects. The game is played once. Everything is known to both except in Section 7. Each partner's burden is treated as one number, which presumes money, body and attention can be compared across two people — a limit named once and not repeated. Pairs where neither partner faces pregnancy risk are outside the model. The model says nothing on method, medical safety, abortion or parenting. Concealed or coerced use is a different problem from bargaining.

One escape route needs closing: "it is just their preference, so none of this applies." Preference is possible; it is one of the four readings in Section 6. But from outside it is indistinguishable from a weak walk-away, and a preference formed by the arrangement cannot be told from one formed freely.

Two falsifiers, for the reader who wants the model to be challengeable: if the share borne is uncorrelated with stakes once the cost of bearing is held constant, Result 3 fails to describe the couple. If acknowledgement of private costs is no more reliable when misreporting is costly (verifiable, observable markers), Result 7 fails. Both falsifiers would require data that do not currently exist.

## 10. The Serious Objection

Some people prefer the arrangement. The partner exposed to the risk has a claim to decide how it is managed, and may want control over the method rather than a share of the burden. An agreement between adults can be informed and fair. And economics should not be pricing a body at all.

Most of this is right, and the model already contains it. The exposure argument is not a rebuttal of the model; it is the mechanism of Section 5. If the exposed partner has the larger stake, that partner has the stronger reason to bear, and the model says so.

On informed and fair agreements: yes. Section 9 lists the conditions under which agreement is enough, and the benefit of bearing is one of them. Nothing here says that a burden implies an injustice. The claim is narrower: agreement certifies that each preferred the deal to walking away, and nothing more, unless the conditions hold.

On the right to decide the method: the essay takes no side on it, and does not need to. Grant it in full. A different question remains, and it is the one the model asks: whether the other partner then owes a share of the other costs — the money, the appointments, the acknowledgement of what only the bearer can see. Deciding the method and bearing its costs are separate questions. Having settled the first can make the second harder to raise: *it was your choice* stops the bearing partner asking, and *it was my choice* stops the other offering. That is where the menu in the next section applies.

On money fixing it: for the part of the burden a payment can repay, it does, and Sections 4 and 5 say exactly that. What the model adds is what is left over when the payable part is limited, and why no payment closes that remainder.

On pricing a body: the model prices nothing in money. It compares how much each partner gains with how much each bears, and asks when an agreement carries information. The alternative to naming the limit is not a model without the limit — it is *we both agreed* left to mean whatever each partner needs it to mean.

What the model cannot do should be stated as plainly. It cannot tell a preference formed freely from one formed by years of being the partner who handles it. The observation is the same, and so is the report. That question is left open, not answered.

So the objection does not dissolve the argument. It locates it. What survives is not the claim that lopsided arrangements are unfair. It is the claim that agreement, by itself, cannot say whether they are.

## 11. A Menu of Fair

"Fair" is not an economic concept, and the model does not pick a standard of fairness. What it can do is work out the split each standard requires from the same facts, so the standards can be compared on something firmer than intuition.

Five standards are commonly applied to joint burdens. Equal burden: each partner bears the same weight. Equal net gain: each ends up equally well off from the arrangement. Proportional to net gain: each bears in proportion to what they gain from prevention, measured over their walk-away — equal to exposure only when neither partner's walk-away exceeds staying unprotected. Ability to pay: the money part split by means. And cheapest bearer, then compensate: this is the essay's cost-minimising benchmark (the efficient standard, included here for comparison) — the cheaper partner bears, and the other pays.

On the running example in table order, which is not a ranking:

| Standard | X's share | Burdens (X, Y) | Each ends with (X, Y) | Extra burden |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Equal burden | 43% | 3.43, 3.43 | 6.57, 0.57 | 0.86 |
| Equal net gain | 86% | 6.86, 0.86 | 3.14, 3.14 | 1.71 |
| Proportional to net gain | 65% | 5.22, 2.09 | 4.78, 1.91 | 1.30 |
| Ability to pay | money part only | needs incomes, not computed | depends on incomes | n/a |
| Cheapest bearer, then compensate (efficiency standard) | 0% | 0, 6 | 4, 4 after X pays Y 6 | 0 |
| *For comparison: the no-payment agreement (Section 5)* | *79%* | *6.33, 1.25* | *3.67, 2.75* | *1.58* |

Four things follow.

The standards disagree on the same facts. Without payments, X's share runs from 43 per cent under equal burden, through 65 per cent under the proportional standard, to 86 per cent under equal net gain, and falls to nothing under the cheapest-bearer efficiency standard. Being fair is not one target.

"More at stake bears more" is required by equal net gain and by the proportional standard, and violated by equal burden. So the very pattern Section 6 could not decode is fair under two standards and unfair under a third. The pattern is not a verdict, in either direction.

Only the cheapest-bearer standard minimises the total burden, and it requires the payment to be large enough: here, six units. Without the payment, the cheaper bearer is left worse off than walking away and would not agree. The efficient standard is the one least often available, and where it is not, the choice is among the others.

Equal burden and equal net gain compare amounts across two people, which requires a common unit: the commensurability assumption of Section 4's third condition. The proportional standard and the Nash split do not require it. So the "extra burden" column is meaningful only under the common-unit assumption, and two of the five standards are immune to that limit.

Procedural and rights-based standards — was the process free, informed and revisable; who holds the decision right over the method — are not listed here because the model cannot derive a split from them. Their absence is a limit of the model, not a verdict that they are less valid.

Choosing among the listed standards is a normative choice, outside the model. Declining to choose does not make them agree. What the menu removes is the option of hearing *it's fair* without asking: fair by which of these?

## 12. Three Questions

The model needed three numbers that no outsider can see. A couple can at least ask about them, from either side of the arrangement.

**Who bears which cost?** Not just who uses the method, but who pays, who books, who remembers, and how it is to live with. Then sort the answers: which of these could a payment make up for, and which can the other partner actually see?

**Who holds the residual risk?** What each partner stands to lose if prevention fails. And does the partner who holds more of it also bear more of the burden, or do those come apart?

**What would each of you have done if you could have said no?** The walk-away is the number that sits behind *fine*. Both partners can ask it; from the bearing partner's seat it asks what the agreement was really worth, and from the other's seat it asks what the alternative was. Neither version implies what anyone should do.

Go back to the breath before *yes, fine.* It could have been the cheapest arrangement two people could have found, landed on without effort. It could have been a free preference, for control or for something else the arrangement gives. It could have been a concession by the partner with the worse alternative. Or it could have been an arrangement made once, years ago, that nobody has had a reason worth the trouble to reopen. The words are the same in all four. From outside, and sometimes from inside, the four cannot be told apart.

The general claim, which is this essay's own: an agreement is good evidence that the deal is the cheapest one when the costs could be seen and repaid and the arrangement stayed open to revision. Where those conditions hold, *we both agreed* is a good answer. Where they do not, it is still true, and it is still the answer. The question it leaves is the one it started with: what, exactly, did the *fine* cover?
