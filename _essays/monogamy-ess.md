## 1. Nobody Would Ever Know

It is late. You are in a city where nobody knows your partner, and the person across the table has stopped pretending this is about work. Your phone is face-down. The conversation has slipped into the register where every sentence can be read two ways, and both of you keep choosing the second reading. Nobody has touched anybody. Nobody needs to yet. The question is sitting on the table between the glasses, and you notice your mind doing something it would rather you didn't see. It isn't asking whether you want to. It is working out who would find out.

*Nobody would ever know.* That is the sentence. Not "I've fallen out of love," not "I deserve this." Just the private calculation that the room has no witnesses in it.

Hold that moment, because it contains an argument evolutionary biologists have been running for decades and that almost nobody applies to the question it fits best. Multiply your moment across a population and across generations and it comes out as a number: the share of children who are not the biological offspring of the man raising them. In historical Dutch populations, reconstructed over four centuries through genetic genealogy, that number was about 1% per generation.^1 Among Himba pastoralists in northern Namibia, measured with genetic paternity testing, it was 48%.^2 Same species, same temptation, wildly different outcomes.

This article explains why that range exists and why it is the most informative thing we know about monogamy. The argument runs in five steps. First, "does anyone want to cheat?" is the wrong stability test, and there is a harder one called *evolutionary stability*. Second, a population of faithful partners is exactly the environment in which a cheater does best, so faithful monogamy fails that test. Third, cheating can't take over either, which means evolution's resting point is a stable *mix* of strategies, and the genetic data looks like that mix. Fourth, the people choosing partners and the people faking commitment are locked in an arms race that neither side wins. Fifth, the claim at the center: **monogamy is not an evolutionarily stable strategy. It is an unstable strategy held in place by enforcement, and the enforcement is the part worth understanding.** After that comes the strongest objection, and then what to do with all this, whether you are reading a partner, trying to be read by one, or sitting at that table.

That central claim is this article's own synthesis. No single paper states it in those words. It follows from putting together John Maynard Smith's stability test, Robert Trivers's account of why the sexes invest differently, and the anthropology of how monogamous norms spread. The sections below build it one step at a time so you can check each step.

## 2. The Wrong Test and the Harder One

The intuitive way to ask whether monogamy is stable is to ask whether any individual would want to break it. Economists formalize that intuition as a **Nash equilibrium**: a situation in which nobody can do better by changing their own strategy, given what everyone else is doing. A companion piece in this series treats the faithful couple as exactly that kind of equilibrium. Two people, each better off staying than straying, given that the other is staying.

That test checks one person at a time. Evolution checks a population.

In 1982, the biologist John Maynard Smith formalized a stricter standard, the **evolutionarily stable strategy**, or ESS.^3 The question changes from "would a rational person deviate?" to "if a small number of individuals start behaving differently, will that behavior spread?" Nobody has to decide anything. The new strategy spreads because its carriers leave more descendants, or, for culturally transmitted behavior, because it gets copied more often. The mechanism is differential success, not deliberation. A strategy is evolutionarily stable if a population playing it cannot be invaded by a rare alternative.

The gap between the two tests is where this article lives. A population can sit at a Nash equilibrium, with nobody wanting to deviate, and still be open to invasion by a rare alternative that happens to do better *because* it is rare. Nash asks whether the rule holds when everyone follows it. ESS asks whether the rule survives the first few people who don't.

> **The formal test (Maynard Smith 1982)**
>
> A strategy *S* is evolutionarily stable if, for any alternative strategy *T* introduced in small numbers, either:
>
> 1. *S* does strictly better against *S* than *T* does against *S*; or
> 2. *S* and *T* do equally well against *S*, but *S* does better against *T* than *T* does against itself.
>
> Nash equilibrium only requires that *S* does at least as well against *S* as *T* does. The ESS conditions add the extra demand. Every ESS is a Nash equilibrium. Not every Nash equilibrium is an ESS.

Put in terms of the table you were sitting at: the Nash question is whether you, personally, are better off faithful in a world of faithful people. The ESS question is whether a world of faithful people can stop the few who aren't from doing better than everyone else. The second question has a less comfortable answer.

## 3. The Game: Two Strategies, Two Sexes, One Asymmetry

To run the test, you need a game: who plays, what they can choose, and what each choice pays.

The currency is reproductive success, meaning surviving offspring. That is not what anyone consciously wants at a hotel bar. It is the scorekeeping that shaped the dispositions people bring to the bar. The model predicts the distribution of behavior in a population, not the contents of anyone's head.

Each player, male or female, has two strategies available.

**Faithful** means investing in a long-term pair bond: staying, provisioning, protecting, raising the children. When two faithful players pair up, the payoff is high, because children with two invested parents tend to do better. The risk is being exploited. A faithful man can end up raising a child who isn't his. A faithful woman can end up with a partner whose investment goes elsewhere or disappears.

**Cad** means pursuing short-term matings without sustained investment. A male cad gets offspring at almost no cost, often raised on someone else's resources. A female cad pursues short-term or extra-pair matings, which can mean seeking genetic quality from one partner while drawing investment from another. Gangestad and colleagues' "dual-mating" and strategic-pluralism work describes this female short-term strategy.^5 The evidence that women have a real short-term mating strategy is stronger than the evidence for the specific ovulatory-cycle mechanisms proposed for it, but the strategy belongs in the game.

> **The faithful-versus-cad game (qualitative payoffs; this article's synthesis, not a measured table)**
>
> |  | **Faithful female** | **Cad female** |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Faithful male** | High for both: two invested parents, best offspring survival | She keeps his investment plus outside genetic options; he risks raising another man's child |
> | **Cad male** | He gets offspring at near-zero cost; she carries most of the investment alone | Low for both: little investment, worst offspring survival |
>
> *When faithful play is nearly universal, the off-diagonal cells (exploiting a faithful partner) are where the cad's profits are. When cad play is common, everyone drifts toward the bottom-right cell.*

This game is not perfectly symmetric, and the asymmetry matters. In 1972 Robert Trivers explained why: the sexes start with unequal investment. A large, costly egg, then pregnancy and nursing, against cheap sperm.^4 By the moment of conception, a woman has already committed far more to any given child than a man has. That makes the cad strategy cheaper for men and more expensive for women. It does not make it unavailable to women. Trivers predicts how often and under what conditions each sex defects, not who is allowed to. Expect male defection to be more common and female defection to be more conditional. Both directions are live.

The same asymmetry explains a familiar pattern: the higher-investing party usually does the screening and the lower-investing party usually does the pursuing. Women have tended to evaluate and men to advertise. The roles can flip, and they often do, but the asymmetry tells you who usually has more to lose from a bad read.

One more simplification needs to be stated before it causes trouble. "Faithful" and "cad" are not personality types. Real people move between them depending on opportunity, life stage, and how good their current relationship is. The model tracks the *proportion* of behavior that looks like each strategy across a population. Whether that is enough to make the model honest is the strongest objection to it, and Section 9 takes it on directly.

## 4. A Room Full of Trusting People Is a Cheater's Paradise

Start with a population that is entirely faithful. Everyone invests, everyone stays, and children are raised by both parents. Is this stable?

It isn't, and the reason is the same one that made your calculation at the table feel so tempting.

Drop a single cad into this population. He meets partners who have every reason to take him at his word, because nearly everyone who says they will stay does stay. Trust is the default because it has almost always been justified. The model predicts that detection will be weak here, and the reason is not that people are naive. The ability to spot a defector is itself a trait under selection, and selection only sharpens it when misjudging is costly often enough to matter. In a population where defectors are vanishingly rare, being fooled almost never happens, so there is little pressure to become hard to fool. Detection tracks the threat, and here the threat is nearly absent.

So the rare cad's imitation of commitment doesn't need to be good. It only needs to be good enough for an audience that has never needed to be suspicious. He collects matings that a faithful man would have to pay for with years of investment, and he collects them at the price of a few convincing evenings. Some of his children are raised by women alone and some by faithful men who believe they are the fathers. In both cases, someone else bears the cost and he takes the reproductive return. His fitness exceeds the population average. In ESS terms, that is a successful invasion.

Notice what kind of game the cad is playing. It is one-shot. He passes through, collects, and moves on before any consequence can catch up with him. Each encounter is effectively the first and last time the two parties meet. This is what *nobody would ever know* means in strategic terms: the interaction has no future in which defection could be punished. Hold onto that, because the fix in Section 8 is to give that interaction a future.

Faithful players fight back. The individual-level defense is **mate guarding**: vigilance, keeping rivals away, displays of resources and commitment, sometimes threats. David Buss's review of human mate guarding shows it is real, varied, and expensive, and that people spend more on it as they perceive more risk.^6 The cost is paid by the people trying to stay faithful, in time and attention that could go elsewhere. And it leaks, because no amount of vigilance gets detection error to zero. A rare cad who puts effort into his performance will sometimes get through.

> A population of complete monogamists contains the conditions for its own invasion.
{: .pull}

This invasion result follows directly from the payoff structure, given the assumptions: detection is imperfect, investment matters to offspring, and the cad starts out rare. Change those assumptions and the result can change. They are not exotic assumptions, though. They describe the world most humans have lived in. **A population of complete monogamists contains the conditions for its own invasion.** That is the first half of the article's core claim, and it is the hawk-dove logic Maynard Smith developed with the labels changed to describe mating.^3

## 5. A Room Full of Cheaters Is Worse

If faithful monogamy can be invaded, does cheating win? It doesn't, and the reason is the same frequency logic run in the other direction.

Picture the opposite world, where nearly everyone plays cad. Three things go wrong for the cad at once.

First, the children suffer. With little sustained investment from either side, offspring survival falls. The cad's strategy of free-riding on someone else's investment stops working when nobody is investing.

Second, the choosers get choosy. When most suitors are cads, accepting the wrong one is no longer a rare mistake. It is the usual outcome. Now the selection pressure that was missing in Section 4 arrives in force: every mating decision carries a high chance of desertion, so discrimination pays. Buss and Schmitt's sexual strategies theory makes the relevant prediction. Male short-term mating success depends on female willingness, and that willingness is not a fixed fact about the world. It responds to the strategies men are running.^7

Third, a rare faithful player becomes the most valuable person around. In a world of cads, someone who will actually stay is scarce, and scarcity is worth a lot. He gets chosen. His children survive better. He out-reproduces the people around him, which means faithful play invades.

Neither pure world is stable. A faithful world invites cads, and a cad world invites the faithful. The population is pushed back from both ends toward a specific proportion: the point at which the two strategies do equally well, so neither can gain by becoming more common.

> **Frequency dependence at a glance**
>
> | Share of cads in the population | Who does better | What happens next |
> |---|---|---|
> | Near zero | The cad: trust is high, detection weak | Cads spread |
> | At the equilibrium share | Neither: payoffs are equal | The mix holds |
> | Near one | The faithful: investment is scarce, choosers are wary | Faithful play spreads |
>
> **The hawk-dove template.** In Maynard Smith's original game, two animals contest a resource worth *V*. Hawks fight and doves posture and back down. A fight costs the loser *C*. When fighting costs more than the prize is worth (*C* > *V*), neither pure strategy is stable, and the population settles where hawks make up a share of *V*/*C*.^3
>
> Read *V* loosely as what defection gains and *C* as what defection risks when it meets resistance. Raise *C* through better detection, harsher punishment, or more reputational exposure, and the stable share of defectors falls. The analogy is structural, not a formula for humans, but it contains the practical content of this article in a single fraction.

## 6. The Fingerprint: 1% to 48%

A mixed equilibrium leaves recognizable traces. It predicts that defection persists as a minority, that it never disappears entirely, and that its rate moves with the parameters: the cost of investment, the sharpness of detection, and the strength of enforcement. Human paternity data shows all three.

**Low but not zero.** The Dutch figure comes from a genetic-genealogy method: pairs of men whose family trees say they share a paternal ancestor, whose Y chromosomes test whether that is true. Across 68 such pairs, covering about a thousand conceptions over four hundred years, the extra-pair paternity rate came out at roughly 1% per generation, with a confidence interval of about 0.5% to 1.8%.^1 That is about as low as human measurements go, and it is still not zero.

**It moves with context.** A later historical-genetic reconstruction found that the rate was not uniform even within Western Europe. It reached around 6% among lower-status families in densely populated nineteenth-century cities.^8 Read through the model, those are settings where anonymity was easier and the watching that a village provides was thinner. The parameters changed, and the equilibrium moved with them.

**It can be very high.** The Himba figure of 48% (confidence interval roughly 32% to 66%), with about 70% of couples having at least one extra-pair child, needs its context to make sense.^2 Himba society is matrilineal, and marriage is duolocal, with spouses often not sharing a single household. Two of the model's levers are being pulled at once. Enforcement of paternal exclusivity is weak, and because a woman's kin network carries much of the child-rearing load, the return on a man's faithful investment is lower to begin with. The model predicts a higher defection share when both are true. That is the result, and it is not a curiosity about an exotic population.

Two more pieces of evidence help separate signal from noise. Kermyt Anderson's review of 67 studies found nonpaternity of roughly 1.7% to 2.9% among men who were confident of their paternity, and about 30% among men who had doubts serious enough to seek testing.^9 This tells you that the alarming figures that sometimes circulate come from samples of suspicious men, so clinical testing data inflates the estimates. It also tells you that suspicion is not random. Men who doubt are right far more often than men who don't. Detection is imperfect, but it is not blind.

You may have heard that one in ten children isn't fathered by the man who thinks he's the father. That figure traces largely to survey-based estimates from Robin Baker and Mark Bellis in the 1990s, and genetic methods have not supported it.^10 Their broader point that sperm competition is a real feature of human reproduction holds up. Their specific numbers do not, and nothing in this article depends on them.

None of this measures infidelity directly. Paternity data counts only the defections that produced a child and went into a family tree, which is a small and historically shifting fraction of the affairs people actually had. It works here as a proxy, and the proxy is good for one thing: showing that the defection rate is never zero and is never fixed.

The same structure appears outside humans. In the ruff, a Eurasian wading bird, territorial males and "satellite" males who hang around the territories and mate opportunistically coexist at a stable proportion, and the ruff has been analyzed as a candidate mixed ESS.^11 Geoffrey Parker's sperm-competition models show more generally how non-investing "sneaker" tactics can persist at a stable minority frequency alongside investing ones.^12 One caution: some biologists argue that the ruff's types are maintained by genetic polymorphism rather than strategic frequency-dependence, so the bird is supporting illustration and not proof. Either way, the result is the same as in humans: a persistent minority of defectors inside a population that looks paired.

The 1%-to-48% range is not a problem with the measurements. It is what you would expect from a mixed equilibrium in which the parameters differ from place to place.

## 7. The Arms Race Nobody Wins

If the rare cad's advantage comes from weak detection, the obvious counter is better detection. That counter exists, and it is the main source of stability *inside* the game, before any culture gets involved.

The chooser's problem is what economists call adverse selection. Both types claim to be faithful, and claims cost nothing to make. If the only evidence available is cheap talk, cads and faithful partners look the same, they **pool**, and the chooser has to guess. What separates the types is a signal that costs the wrong type more than it costs the right one. Amotz Zahavi's handicap principle describes the mechanism: a signal is credible when faking it costs more than the faker would gain.^13 Slow courtship, sustained presence, resources committed before sex rather than after, being introduced to family and friends: these work as filters because a cad has to pay for them with time he would rather spend elsewhere, and a faithful partner was going to spend that time anyway. Buss and Schmitt predicted that commitment cues of exactly this kind would be what choosers attend to most closely, and the prediction has held up well.^7

Cads, in turn, get better at producing the signal. Choosers get better at noticing when it is being faked. Deception about commitment and resources runs in both directions, as does deception about sexual history and intentions. Neither side has won. If choosers could perfectly separate the types, cads would disappear. If cads could perfectly mimic the faithful, investment would collapse. Human mating looks like neither outcome. It looks like a race still being run, which is what a mixed equilibrium looks like from the inside.

The race also runs in more than one direction. If women have a real short-term strategy (Section 3), then the faithful man is a chooser too, trying to read a partner whose commitment he can't directly observe. The stability problem is two-sided, which means the gap that detection fails to close is wider than a male-only story would suggest.

## 8. Enforcement Is What Fills the Gap

Here is where the argument has been heading. Left to individual-level evolution (cads, choosers, and costly mate guarding), the stable outcome is a mixed population with a persistent share of defectors of both sexes. Detection narrows the gap but does not close it. So any society that wants monogamy to be *the norm*, rather than one strategy among several, has to change the payoffs. That is what enforcement does.

Recall the shape of the cad's game in Section 4: one-shot, no future, nobody would ever know. Enforcement works by attacking each part of that. It gives a single encounter a future by attaching consequences that last: reputation, exclusion, lost alliances, legal and economic penalties. It turns the empty room into a room with witnesses, because once there is a shared norm, everyone in the community becomes a low-cost observer. This is the move from one-shot to repeated play, and it is earned because the cad's whole advantage depended on the game being one-shot. Once defection today puts tomorrow at risk, its expected value falls. In the hawk-dove terms of Section 5, *C* goes up.

Two routes to this kind of enforcement are on the table, and the argument does not depend on choosing between them.

**Cultural group selection.** Joseph Henrich, Robert Boyd, and Peter Richerson point out that about 85% of societies in the anthropological record have permitted polygyny. Monogamy is not humanity's default arrangement. Normative monogamy is, as they put it, a puzzle. Their answer is that monogamous marriage norms spread because groups that adopted them did better than groups that didn't: less competition among men over women, less violence, more investment in children, more cooperative productivity.^14 This is worth stating clearly. Polygyny is another way of defecting from monogamy, practiced by high-status men, and it pays well for those individual men. The norm spread *despite* not being individually optimal for the men best placed to break it. Group selection remains contested among evolutionary theorists, and this account depends on competition between groups being strong enough to outweigh selection within them.

**Repeated-game enforcement.** The same result can be produced without any group-level selection. In small, tightly connected communities, individuals have their own reasons to punish defectors: to protect their own pair bonds, to maintain alliances, and to avoid being the next target. When everyone knows everyone, a detected infidelity follows the defector into every future interaction. The punishment is decentralized and incidental, which is why it is cheap. It comes from gossip, closed doors, and lost trust, not from a husband standing guard.

Both routes raise the cost of defecting, and both move the equilibrium toward faithful play. The disagreement is about how enforcement *arose*, not about whether it *was needed*. On that question the ESS analysis is clear: if a faithful population can be invaded, then any stable monogamous norm requires an external force to hold it in place.

Distributed enforcement also beats individual guarding on cost. Mate guarding puts the whole burden on the person who has the most to lose and gets them only partial protection.^6 Social enforcement spreads the cost across everyone who shares the norm and charges each person almost nothing. Each community member pays a small cost so that no individual has to pay a crippling one. And because defection runs in both directions, the enforcement that works is symmetric. Norms that police only one sex leave half of the gap open.

Enforcement does not get the rate to zero, and it can't. It shifts the parameters, and that moves the equilibrium share down. It does not remove the equilibrium. Male investment itself is an equilibrium outcome that shifts as costs, benefits, and sex ratios change, not a fixed biological constant.^15 That is why the Dutch villages got to about 1% and no lower, and why the rate rose again in the anonymous city. Enforcement works to the extent that it actually changes payoffs. When it becomes purely ceremonial, meaning vows nobody monitors, norms nobody enforces, and a social world where nobody would find out, the cost of defection falls back below its benefit and the old equilibrium returns.

The companion piece on how monogamy got made covers how that machinery was designed. This article explains why it had to be built. The reason is not that people are especially weak or especially lustful. The reason is that the population-level math does not hold on its own. **The enforcement gap is structural, not moral.**

## 9. The Strongest Objection: This Is Just-So Evolutionary Psychology

The objection deserves its sharpest form. Evolutionary psychology dressed up as economics is still evolutionary psychology: post hoc, unfalsifiable, a way of rationalizing whatever norms we already have. Look at enforcement and call it necessary. Look at defection and call it predicted. Whatever the data shows, the story absorbs it. Worse, the whole frame depends on "faithful" and "cad" being real strategies, when behavior is obviously plastic. The same person is devoted in one decade and reckless in the next. If behavior is just situational, there is no strategy for selection to act on, and ESS is a category error.

Some of this should be conceded. The model cannot predict what any individual will do. It cannot tell you what the "natural" defection rate would be without culture, because no human population has ever lacked culture. It says nothing about whether monogamy is worth its enforcement costs, which is a question about values that a stability analysis cannot answer. And its best evidence, paternity data, measures only the small fraction of defection that produced a traceable child.

The two central charges still fail.

**Against unfalsifiability.** The model makes a prediction that a purely cultural account does not. A sophisticated cultural account can predict the gradient just as well: stronger norms, less cheating. What it doesn't predict is a floor. The ESS account says enforcement can push the defector share down but cannot eliminate it, because as defectors become rare, the conditions that favor them come back: trust rises, vigilance relaxes, and the empty room reappears. The strictest well-measured populations bottom out around 1%, not zero.^1 A rigorously measured population with effectively zero extra-pair paternity over many generations would be a real problem for the model. None has turned up.

**Against "strategies aren't real."** ESS does not require fixed types. It applies to *decision rules*, such as "invest when partners are watchful and the future is long; defect when the room is empty." A rule like that can be inherited, learned, or copied, and a population's mix of rules can be invaded by a rare alternative rule. Plasticity changes what counts as a strategy, from a fixed type to a conditional rule. It doesn't make the analysis disappear. In fact, the person who is devoted in one setting and reckless in another is exactly what a conditional rule predicts: someone whose behavior depends on enforcement.

The model does not explain everything about love, fidelity, or why particular people stray. What it does is replace a moral question that tends to go nowhere ("why do people cheat?") with a structural one that can be checked ("what is holding the defection rate where it is, and what would move it?"). That is a better question, and it is the one the last section uses.

## 10. What to Do With an Unstable Equilibrium

If monogamy is held in place rather than self-sustaining, then a relationship is not a fixed fact you discover. It is a small equilibrium that you and your partner maintain, and the forces that hold up a population's norm also hold up yours. Each piece of advice below follows from one specific mechanism in the argument.

### Reading a partner

**Your trust is the resource being harvested.** The cad does best in a room full of trusting people (Section 4). The more faithful your circle, the more you take faithfulness for granted, and the less work your detection does. That is exactly the environment where the rare defector profits. A high-trust social world is a reason to keep your judgment working, not to switch it off. Don't let the norm do your screening for you.

**Price every signal by what it would cost the wrong person to fake it for a year.** Words pool (Section 7), so "I'm not like that" carries no information because the people who are like that say it too. What separates the types is slow, expensive, and hard to keep up: time, consistency, being folded into their actual life. One grand gesture costs a cad an evening. Twelve months of steady presence costs him the strategy.

**Ask what their fidelity rests on: character, or structure?** Someone who keeps you sealed off from their friends, family, and colleagues, in a compartment of their life with no witnesses, has opted out of the enforcement that holds the floor (Section 8). That does not prove anything about them. It does mean that if they defect, you are the only enforcement there is.

### Being read

**If you are faithful, saying so is worthless. Volunteer for enforcement instead.** Cheap talk pools you with the cads. A credible commitment means giving your partner the means to punish you: merging your social worlds, making the relationship public, letting the people whose opinion you care about see it. When you voluntarily expose yourself to consequences, you send the signal a cad can't afford to send.

**Don't mistake surveillance for security.** Checking phones, tracking locations, and policing who they talk to is mate guarding (Section 4). It is the most expensive enforcement available, and you pay for all of it yourself in attention, resentment, and your own dignity. It still leaks. Distributed enforcement does more for less: shared friends, shared reputations, a life that is visible to the people in it. If you notice you are relying on guarding alone, that is a sign the cheaper machinery has failed.

### Maintaining the equilibrium

**Notice when your enforcement has gone ceremonial.** A move to a new city, separate social lives, a work world your partner never sees, months of travel: each of these lowers detection and weakens consequences, and none of them looks like a threat when you decide on it. The model doesn't say your partner will cheat. It says the parameters that kept the rate low have changed, and equilibria follow their parameters. Rebuild the structure deliberately before you need it, not after.

**Stop asking whether it could happen. Ask what makes it expensive.** No population has ever reached zero, and yours isn't going to be the first. "Could this ever happen to us?" is the wrong question because the honest answer is always yes. "What have we built that would make it costly?" has an answer you can act on.

## 11. Back at the Table

Go back to the table: the late hour, the face-down phone, the sentence that keeps coming back. *Nobody would ever know.*

You can now name what that sentence is. It is the cad's calculation from Section 4, the reasoning of a rare defector in a population that trusts him, inside a game with no future. It feels like a private thought, but it is the precise condition under which defection pays, and that is why it is so persuasive.

Whatever stops you, if something does, is probably not your nature alone. It is a set of connected things: the friends you share with your partner, a reputation you would have to carry, a partner who would notice, a story you would have to keep telling for the rest of your life. In other words, a room that turns out to have witnesses after all. People had to build that machinery because the evolutionary math wouldn't hold on its own. In places where it was built well, the rate fell to about one child in a hundred. In places where it barely existed, it rose to nearly one in two.

Every version of you who has ever sat at that table, in every population, has added a data point to that range. The range between 1% and 48% is not a measurement problem. It is the answer.
