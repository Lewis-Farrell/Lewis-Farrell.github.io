## 1. The Question You Are Standing In

It is a small thing, which is part of the problem. On Tuesday they said Friday's dinner had been called off. Tonight, half-listening while they run through the week, you hear that dinner described as though it happened: the long wait for a table, a name you don't know, a laugh at something the name said. Then the talk moves on and does not come back. You are drying the same glass for longer than a glass needs. None of it proves anything. It could be two different dinners, a plan that came back to life, a word you misheard on Tuesday. It could be something else. There are three doors in the room. You could ask, lightly, now, while it is still small. You could say nothing tonight and find out some other way. Or you could let it go, and mean it. You haven't chosen. The glass is dry.

Each door has a price, and none of the prices is written on the door. Ask, and the question says something before any answer does: that you were listening that closely, that the mismatch stayed with you, that it mattered enough to say. Let it go, and it may not go; it may sit in on the next conversation and the one after, waiting for a second slip that may never come. The middle door has a price that is hardest to read from where you stand. Under all three is a question you have not yet put to yourself. If you knew, what would you do? If the answer were the bad one, would you act, and how? If it were the harmless one, would you believe it, or would you want to hear it again next month? And if no answer would change what you do, what exactly are you about to pay for?

Now turn the chair around. You are the one who said both things, on Tuesday and tonight, and you notice the pause, the glass held a beat too long. Maybe there is an explanation you could give in a sentence, if anyone asked. Maybe there is one you would rather not give. It may be exactly what they fear, or it may be a conversation that was yours, a person who was someone else's business. You do not know what they heard. You do not know whether they will ask, what it will mean that they did, or what it will mean if you would rather not answer. Whatever happens next, part of it will be decided about you. Go back to the first chair holding that. Looking is not free, and you may not be the only one who pays.

## 2. What This Piece Is About

This essay treats that moment the way an economist would: as a decision about whether to buy an answer, with a price paid on both sides of the table. It works through seven things, in order. First, when a look is worth what it costs; the answer turns on whether any result could change what you would do. Second, a test you can apply to your own checking, with its limits stated: does good news make you look less? Sometimes, and the essay shows when. Third, what changes once the other person responds to being looked at: for a checker who wants to learn, there is a point past which more looking tells you less, not more. Fourth, the other chair: what refusing to be looked at says, and why the cost of clearing a name can fall on people with nothing to hide. Fifth, a line between a check that is a decision and one that is something else; that line is the essay's own construction, not a published result. Sixth, the strongest objections, and the cases the model cannot see. Seventh, questions for classifying your own case, from either chair.

Everything here is derived from models on stated assumptions, not measured in couples. The experiments cited were run on laboratory participants playing employer-and-worker style games, and are labelled that way. A boxed toy model asks what saying "I'm jealous" out loud gives away. The essay makes no claim about how many couples check, and it gives no method for looking and none for hiding.

## 3. Looking Is A Purchase

Start with the barest version of the problem, before anyone in it is allowed to react.

There are two people. One of them, call them the checker (in the lede, that is you), is unsure what kind of partner the other is: the kind who keeps to what the two of you have agreed (usually, that neither sleeps with anyone else), or the kind who does not. Call the first faithful and the second unfaithful, and call one act of the second kind a lapse. The checker can look. Looking costs something: time, attention and, as Section 5 will show, something else. Each look gives an imperfect reading. It flags an unfaithful partner more often than a faithful one, but not always, and now and then it flags a partner who has done nothing. That false flag is the noise the previous essay in this series, "Most Strategies Work, Until They Have To Be Carried Out", treated as a fact of the world: punish it and you punish the innocent, forgive it and you cover for the guilty.[^1] In this first version the noise is taken as given (the previous essay's misread action becomes, here, a false flag on a partner of a fixed kind, a mapping that is this essay's own), and the choice is how much to look at all. In the lede, the name you don't know has just moved the checker's suspicion from "could be anything" toward "worth a question".

After each reading the checker revises what they believe and picks one of three things. Look again. Stop and accept, which means carrying on as if the partner is the faithful kind. Or stop and act: raise it, change how much they put in, leave. Acting against a faithful partner costs something; accepting an unfaithful one costs something else; and both costs grow with what is at stake. In this first version the other person does nothing. They are what they are, and the checker is trying to find out which.

What a careful checker does follows quickly. There is a level of suspicion at which acting and accepting are, on average, equally bad bets. Call it the decision point. Where it sits depends on how bad each mistake is relative to the other, not on how much is at stake overall, because stakes scale both mistakes together. Well below the decision point, even a run of results is unlikely to carry suspicion across it, so you stop and accept. Well above it, even a run of clean results is unlikely to bring it back down at a price worth paying, so you stop and act. In between is a band where looking pays. It is wider than the set of beliefs where a single result could flip the decision, because a run of results can.


Raise the stakes (more shared, more invested, more to lose) and the band widens in both directions, because the cost of a look stays where it was while the cost of each mistake grows.

The lower half of the diagram is the part that surprises people. How many more looks should a checker expect to take before stopping? The answer is hump-shaped: few at low suspicion, the most near the decision point, and none at all at the top, where the checker has stopped looking and is acting. The hump was computed for one set of invented numbers, so read its shape, not the position of its peak. The highest suspicion does not produce the most checking. It produces a decision.

Economists will recognise this as sequential testing at a cost, the problem Abraham Wald formalised: after each observation, accept, reject, or sample again.[^2] The quantity underneath it is the value of information. For a single look the lesson is already there: a look that cannot flip a decision is worth exactly nothing as an input to that decision. Its value is highest at the decision point and falls to zero outside the narrower range where one result could flip the decision. And one weak look, too weak to flip a decision on its own, is worth nothing on its own; the benefit of a sharper look starts abruptly, once it is strong enough to change the action.[^3] A run of weak looks can still add up, which is what the band describes. That is a property of this model, not a law.

Four assumptions carry all of this, on top of a simple frame: two kinds of partner, two ways to stop, and a fixed cost per look. The partner's kind is fixed over the period in question. Stakes multiply the cost of each mistake but not the cost of a look. Readings are independent of one another once you know the partner's kind. And the person being looked at does not react. The last one is the first to go.

The model also hands the checker a ready excuse, and every comfortable excuse gets priced the moment it appears. This is the first. "It was a rational check" is true only if some result of that check, or of the run of checks it begins, could have changed what the checker would do. Call a check that passes this test decision-driven. Outside the band the look buys information that cannot change an action, though it may buy calm, which Section 7 allows. Decision-driven is also a claim about the checker's own objective; it does not settle whether the look is fair to the person being looked at, which Sections 5 and 6 price.

## 4. Does Good News Make You Stop Looking?

Here is the test. Suppose you have looked several times and each time it came back clean. Do you look less?

Most people would say yes. The model's answer is narrower: well below the decision point, yes; near it or above, not necessarily. Here "checking" means how many more looks to expect, or how hard to look. The unqualified statement "good news reduces checking" is false in the model, and it is worth seeing why.

Begin with what has to be true of anyone who updates honestly. You cannot expect every possible result to raise your suspicion. Weight each outcome by how likely it is, and the average of where you might end up is exactly where you started. So if a flag would raise suspicion, a clean result must lower it. The property is called Bayes plausibility.[^4]

Down toward what, though? Go back to the hump. Below the decision point, a clean result moves you down the slope, toward fewer expected looks, and eventually across the accept point, where looking stops. Above the decision point, but still inside the band, a clean result moves you down toward the decision point, which is exactly where the next look is most useful. Good news there can mean more looking, not less, and nothing irrational has happened. And above the band, good news that arrives some other way can pull you back into it and start the looking again.

An illustration, with numbers invented for shape and nothing else. Say the name at the dinner has moved you to 30 percent, near the top of the hump, and your reading flags an unfaithful partner four times in ten and a faithful one once in ten. Four clean results in a row bring suspicion to about 22, then 16, then 11, then 8 percent, which is below an accept point of about 9 percent in this example. Checking stops. The first clean result carried the checker over the top of the hump; the next three walked them down the far side.

So what would count as a violation? The narrow case is this: over a run of clean results, checking does not fall, while the stakes, the cost of a look, the reliability of the reading and the partner's behaviour are all unchanged, and the checker's own suspicion has fallen well below the decision point. Four things remove the violation and have to be ruled out first. Each also has a price if you want to claim it.

- **The stakes rose.** More shared, more invested: the band widens, and a lower suspicion is worth a look. The price: you have to name which stakes rose, by how much, and for how long; "more at stake" is easy to claim after the fact.
- **Looking got cheaper.** The same suspicion now justifies more of it. The price: a cheaper look still costs the person looked at whatever it costs them.
- **The partner or the reading changed,** including, as the next section shows, because of the looking itself. The price: the check is now less informative.
- **You started on the high side of the hump,** where good news means more looking. The price: you are in the region of greatest uncertainty, which is not where "it turned out fine" lives.

Three further candidates (clean results read as concealment, reassurance valued for itself, and a pull toward relief that a calmer self would not sign off on) belong to Section 7.

The test is a model result with no couples data behind it. It licenses a conditional claim and nothing more: if these things hold, this should happen. What would show it wrong is a setting in which stakes, cost and the partner's behaviour are held fixed or measured, the checker's stated suspicion falls below the decision point, and checking does not fall.

What the test cannot do is run from the outside. Stakes and beliefs live inside people, and only the checker can report them, as self-report. Two things break it in advance: a partner whose kind can change (suspicion then never settles, and checking recurs for good reasons), and readings that share a cause, which overstate how much has been learned. Section 8 returns to what this makes of the test.

## 5. The Look Changes What It Finds

So far the person being looked at has been furniture. That made the first model clean, and it is the first assumption to fail in any relationship that lasts. A relationship is not one-shot. The amount of looking becomes part of the weather both people live in, and the person inside that weather adjusts.

Here is the same checker, now choosing how hard to look rather than whether to look once more. The other person is a player, and responds in one of two ways. A partner who might be unfaithful chooses how often to lapse, knowing that harder looking makes a lapse more likely to be seen; so they lapse less. A faithful partner does not scheme, but being looked at harder can make their behaviour more guarded, more ambiguous, more withdrawn. The direction of that second response is an analogy from laboratory games, in which control lowered effort; it is not measured in couples (Box A). The checker still reads flags: the noise from Section 3, now responding to the look. The model needs only that their behaviour can respond to how much looking there is. Whether they know, what a discovered look does to trust, and what being watched without knowing does to a person all lie outside it, and nothing here is evidence on any of them.

Two things happen to a flag. At light looking the partner has barely changed, so a flag is still worth acting on, and each extra bit of looking mainly adds the chance of getting a reading at all: the decision value of looking rises. But real lapses become rarer, because a partner who might lapse is deterred, so there is less to find; and with fewer real lapses, a larger share of the flags that do turn up are false. The decision value of looking rises, peaks, and then falls, and past a further point a flag can no longer be acted on, because it is too likely to be false. Beyond the peak, one more look tells you less, even if it costs nothing. This needs a flag to be actionable at light looking, and it needs the partner who might lapse to lapse less when a lapse is more likely to be seen. If a partner answers scrutiny by lapsing more, the peak is gone.

The peak needs only the partner who lapses less. The faithful partner's drift toward ambiguity adds false flags of its own and pulls the peak lower and earlier. A partner who is lapsing can also cloud the picture, which the previous essay covers as signal jamming, along with the reason a falsely suspected partner cannot clear their name.[^1] The faithful partner's drift is a different thing: not an attempt to mislead anyone, only what being looked at does.

> **Box A: What the evidence on being looked at is, and is not**
>
> The direction of the faithful partner's response (being looked at read as distrust, effort and openness falling) rests on laboratory experiments in principal-agent and labour-market games, not on couples. In Falk and Kosfeld's game, "control" meant the principal imposing a minimum level of effort, which is not the same as being looked at. Most controlled agents reduced their effort, and most of those who reacted badly said they read the control as a sign of distrust.[^5] Four repetitions with 476 students largely confirmed that hidden costs of control exist but usually found them too small to undermine the incentive effect.[^5] Dickinson and Villeval, in a laboratory real-task experiment, found that most agents worked harder when monitored, with some evidence that effort was crowded out above a monitoring threshold.[^6] Ellingsen and Johannesson supply a mechanism, as theory rather than evidence: control tells people they work for someone not worth impressing.[^7]
>
> Carrying this to couples is an analogy twice over: from laboratory participants to partners, and from forced minimum effort to being checked. No study was found of how being looked at changes the looked-at partner in couples, none of control aversion in couples or close pairs, and no estimate of what checking buys the checker. The couples research found is mostly correlational surveys of students about partner-directed information seeking and jealousy, and it cannot settle which way causation runs: one study proposes that exposure to ambiguous information about a partner prompts jealousy and further use of the site, which in turn feeds more looking.[^8] Nothing here says how many couples check. The strategic results in this section rest instead on a plain assumption, that a partner who might be unfaithful lapses less when a lapse is more likely to be seen. That assumption is untested in couples, and if it fails, the information peak that does not rely on the laboratory direction goes with it.

Three consequences follow.

**Why a checker who wants to learn goes past the peak.** Picture two checkers, each mainly trying to find out which kind of partner they have. One sets a level they could hold to whatever the partner does in the meantime. The other decides in the moment, each time, given how the partner is behaving now. The second treats the partner's behaviour as fixed when choosing, and so never counts that looking harder is part of what makes lapses rare and flags weak. When looking is cheap, that checker overshoots the information peak and does worse by their own measure than the first would have: more looking, past the point where each look adds information, and for cheap enough looking less information in total and a worse result. Nothing irrational is required, and no anxiety. It is the familiar point that a known rule can beat discretion, applied to looking; treating a level held regardless of the partner's moment-to-moment behaviour as a rule is this essay's own step.[^9]

**Less information is not the same as a worse outcome.** The checker above cared only about finding out which kind of partner they have. Real checkers may also care about the lapses themselves, because a lapse that never happens is worth something. Add that motive and the best level of looking can sit above the information peak, for a deterrence reason, if that assumption holds; with a strong enough motive, even the extra looking from deciding in the moment can disappear. So "more checking, past the point where each look adds information" is a precise claim, and "more checking, worse outcome" holds only for a checker who is purely trying to learn, or once the costs to the faithful partner are counted. The two halves of the turn are not the same news. To a checker who wants to learn, a partner who lapses less is a loss of evidence. To a checker who also wants fewer lapses, it is the point. The cost that falls on someone either way is the faithful partner's, and it is the half the laboratory evidence is about, by analogy.

**A checker who leaves out the response learns the wrong thing.** Suppose the partner is entirely faithful, but heavier looking makes their behaviour more ambiguous, and the checker does not know this. If the rise in false flags is large enough that flags arrive several times as often as the checker's picture of a faithful partner allows for (in the simulated example, more than about four times), belief in unfaithfulness climbs. In a simulation run for this essay, with invented numbers and flags arriving at about six times the assumed false-alarm rate, the typical simulated checker ended up close to certain that a partner who had done nothing was unfaithful. With a smaller response, belief fell instead. Whether the response is ever that large in couples is unknown. The checker is not being irrational about preferences; they are reasoning correctly from a wrong model, and their own checking is producing the evidence. What a harsh rule then does with a false flag is the previous essay's subject: under a grim trigger, one false alarm is permanent.[^1]

The case where both people are checking, and each is also the one being checked, is open; this series has not built that model yet.

Two excuses get priced here. "I am only protecting the relationship from what is going on": the look is also a message about how the other person is read, and if the laboratory direction carries over, a faithful partner pays for it in withdrawn openness while the checker collects more false flags. "I would stop if I could, but I cannot hold to a level": that describes the commitment gap, since for a checker who mainly wants to learn, and when looking is cheap, not being able to hold to a level whatever the partner does is what puts the checker past the information peak.

## 6. What Refusing Says

Now take the other chair fully.

The person being checked can allow a look that would come back clean, or refuse. Someone who has something to hide cannot allow a clean look; that is what having something to hide means here: having something the look would show. Someone with nothing to hide can allow it, but pays a private cost for doing so: privacy, autonomy, whatever else the look would show that was never the checker's business. That cost differs from person to person. The checker sees only "allowed" or "refused" and draws an inference. The innocent decide whether to pay, knowing their refusal will be read. In the room with the glass, declining to get into it is itself an answer, and they will hear it as one. All of this assumes the choice is free. Where one partner depends on the other, financially or otherwise, allowing a look is not a free choice, and the model does not cover that case.

"Nothing to hide, nothing to fear" is a claim about how that game ends, and it can be checked. If no innocent person minds being looked at, refusal can only mean guilt; every innocent person, knowing that, allows the look; and refusal becomes a confession. Economists call that unravelling, and its classic statements come from Grossman and Milgrom.[^10] But full unravelling is possible only if every innocent person's cost of allowing the look is below what it would cost them to be taken for guilty with certainty. Let even a few innocent people value privacy above that, and it cannot happen. Even if none does, it is one possible outcome, not the only one. Where disclosing costs the innocent something, the chain of "I'll show you because I have nothing to hide" breaks, which is one standard reason full disclosure is rarer than the simple argument predicts.[^11]

How much a refusal says, then, is an equilibrium quantity: it depends on how many innocent people refuse in that setting. An illustration, numbers invented for shape. Start with a suspicion of one in ten. If no innocent person would refuse, a refusal moves that suspicion to certainty. If three in ten innocent people value privacy so much that they would refuse whatever the refusal is taken to mean, it moves to a little over one in four. If the costs are spread smoothly and the spread reaches well above the cost of being suspected, a refusal can barely move suspicion at all.

Two further results are less intuitive. First, the same people, with the same privacy costs and the same stakes, can end up in a state where almost everyone allows the look and refusal is damning, or in a state where many refuse and refusal says almost nothing. The model does not say which one a given pair is in, the analysis did not test which is stable, and nothing in the model lets a couple tell. Second, in privacy cost alone, the innocent pay far more on average where everyone allows the look than where many refuse. The cost of full disclosure falls, in that state, on exactly the people the check was meant to clear.

Two things sit under this. One is that an allowed look is hard evidence, which the guilty cannot fake. The assumption is that a clean look shows what is there and cannot be shaped by the person shown it. Where it can, a clean look stops being hard evidence, which is the previous essay's signal-jamming case.[^1] The other is that a clean look is decisive. If it is not, demands may ratchet and the innocent may pay more than the model says.

Section 5 links back here. If being asked is itself read as distrust, more innocent people might refuse, and the signal would weaken as the demand strengthened. No study tested this. It joins the laboratory finding about effort (laboratory participants, not couples) to the refusal model, and the join is this essay's own. The closest test found of privacy and unravelling, a laboratory study in a labour-market frame, observed unravelling often but less than theory predicts, and less again when the information was about workers' health; the authors find the under-revelation consistent with a level-k model of limited depth of reasoning.[^12] How many innocent partners value privacy enough to refuse is unknown.

Both chairs get their excuse priced. The asker says: "I only asked so you could clear your name." Its price: asking imposes the privacy cost on exactly the innocent, and the demand may push innocent people toward refusal. The refuser says: "My privacy is my right, so refusing says nothing." Its price: refusing says as little as the share of innocent refusers makes it say, and where few innocent people refuse it says a lot. Neither seat gets its excuse for free.

> **Box B: What saying "I'm jealous" reveals (model-only)**
>
> Model-only: this is a toy on invented assumptions, with no data behind it in either direction.
>
> One partner says, out loud, "I'm jealous." The listener now has to decide two things: how much to concede, and how much to keep investing. Both depend on what the words reveal, and they can reveal two different things. Read one way, they say *I value this a great deal*. Read the other way, they say *I have nowhere else to go*.
>
> Under the second reading, saying it only weakens the speaker's position: the listener learns the speaker is staying, so there is less reason to concede, and nothing about the listener's own investment improves. Under the first reading, the sign is ambiguous. Learning that the speaker values the relationship can make the listener's own investment look safer and raise it, which helps both, while the speaker still gives away some leverage. Which effect wins depends on their relative sizes.
>
> In the version where the words reveal how much the speaker values the relationship, the message is believable only in a middle range. Where being believed costs the speaker little in leverage, everyone says it and it means nothing; where being believed costs a lot, no one does; in between, only the person who really is committed will say it. This is cheap talk in the economist's sense, a costless message whose credibility depends on who would send it.[^13] The thresholds come from invented numbers; the regime structure is what the model shows.
>
> The sign depends on what the listener infers, and there is no data on which inference listeners draw. The box does not say whether to say it or hold it back. Holding it back has a price too, which the box does not model. When jealousy is an involuntary display rather than a chosen sentence, it is a question of credibility, not bargaining, and the previous essay covers it as emotional commitment (Frank).[^1]
>
> The price: "Saying it was just being honest." Under one reading, honesty here is also a message with a price, paid in leverage. The box says what the price is, not whether it is worth paying.
>
> *A question for this box:* If I said it, which inference would the listener draw: that I value this, or that I cannot leave?

## 7. A Decision, Or Something Else?

How would you know whether a pattern of checking is a decision or something else? This section draws a line, and the line is this essay's own construction: an original synthesis of results from the sections above and standard decision theory, not a published model. It classifies a pattern of checking, not a person, and it is for your own pattern, not someone else's. A clinical literature on reassurance seeking studies repeated reassurance seeking; the line does not separate that pattern from the others and makes no claim about it.

The line uses four questions, each a result already earned.

1. **Could any result change what I would do?** Could any result of this check, or of the run of checks it begins, do so? This is decision value, from Section 3.
2. **Do I update the way honest reasoning requires?** If a bad result would raise my suspicion, a clean one must be able to lower it. A checker for whom every result raises suspicion is not updating. A checker who holds that clean results are likelier from an unfaithful partner has a coherent but different picture of the signal; the question is whether that picture is right, and Section 5 shows how checking can build a wrong one. A checker who has been deceived before, or who knows a clean result can be produced by someone with something to hide, may reasonably treat a clean result as weak evidence, so that suspicion falls slowly. That is Bayesian, and the table gives it its own row.
3. **Does my checking fall as good news arrives,** with stakes, cost and the reading held fixed, once Section 4's rivals are ruled out? Think of the third time you raise the same dinner after it has been explained.
4. **Would my calm self pay this cost?** If I could decide now, in advance and with a clear head, would I sign off on the look I am about to take?

The fourth question needs its model. Suppose a look gives relief now and costs something a little later: time, or the erosion Section 5 described. Suppose also that the self in the moment weighs relief now more heavily against a later cost than the same person would have weighed that pair beforehand. Then for relief in a middle range the self in the moment checks and the calm self would not have endorsed it. That reversal is what economists call time inconsistency. The standard model of it is present-biased discounting; Caplin and Leahy showed that anticipatory feelings can also generate it.[^14] Applying it to checking is this essay's own step, and so is the move to relief that evidence about the partner does not touch, so that checking comes loose from results and from stakes and tracks the checker, not the partner. Why is the calm self the reference? Because the question is consistency over time, and the earlier self is the one the feeling does not yet have hold of. That is a choice, not a proof: relief now is a real gain, and a calm self can underrate how bad the moment feels.

Wanting reassurance does not by itself fail anything. If people value calm, a look with zero decision value is consumption, a purchase of calm. In the cases run for this essay, valuing reassurance lowered the point at which a checker stops, but did not make checking rise with good news at low suspicion; that was checked on examples with concave belief-utility forms, not proved in general. The two models retrieved on preferences over information (Kőszegi; the Golman, Hagmann and Loewenstein review) emphasise the avoidance case: anxiety leads people to avoid information, not to seek it again and again.[^15] No retrieved model generates repeated checking after clean results. That gap is why this line is labelled synthesis, and it means the pattern the line picks out is partly defined by what these models leave unexplained.

| P1 Deciding, with a correct picture | Yes | Yes | Yes, below the decision point | Yes | Decision-driven |
| P2 Valuing reassurance, consistently | Sometimes not | Yes | Yes, with a lower stopping point | Yes | Consistent, with preferences over beliefs |
| P3 Deciding, with a wrong picture of the partner's response | Appears to | Yes, given the wrong picture | No; suspicion may rise | Yes | Misled by its own checking (Section 5) |
| P4 Consuming relief against your own calmer judgement | No | May | No; decoupled from results and stakes | No | The pattern the line picks out |
| P5 Reading clean results as weak evidence, for a reason | Depends how weak | Yes, under a different picture of the signal | Slowly | Often yes | Coherent; the question is whether the picture is right |
| P6 Not updating | Not if already past the act point; otherwise a rising belief can cross it | No | No | Unclear | Fails on updating |

The tempting falsifier is "checking that rises with clean results". No model run here produces that, except in narrow regions already described. The defensible version is weaker and more useful: checking that fails to fall over a long run of clean results, with stakes, cost and the reading fixed, and with the checker's own suspicion near zero. That is the pattern the combined decision-plus-reassurance model does not generate in the cases run, with concave belief-utility forms; other forms might. Three limits stay. Stakes and beliefs can be reported only by the checker, as self-report. The calm-self test is self-report too, with all the hindsight that implies. And there are no couples data.

Three ways out of the table carry a price. "It's just reassurance" passes as P2, but only if the calm self would pay, and the price includes what the look does to the other person. "I can't help it" describes P4, and this section offers a classification, not a cure. "I was lied to before" passes as P5, but only if the picture of how weak a clean result is can be stated and is right for this partner; the price includes holding them to a deception that may not have been theirs.

## 8. The Objections That Matter

**Trust is a stance, not an estimate.** The strongest objection goes to the frame, not the table. *To trust someone is not to estimate the chance that they are the faithful kind. It is to treat them as a person who is owed belief, and checking is a withdrawal of that: a message, not a measurement. Your model assumes what is in dispute, that a partner has a fixed kind to be estimated and that looking is a purchase of evidence about it.*

Part of that is right. The model has a partner of one fixed kind and no term for repair, forgiveness or a changed mind. It counts the harm of a look only as withdrawn openness and false flags, and it cannot say that being believed is worth more than the evidence. What it does hold is narrower. If a look is a message, Sections 5 and 6 are about the message arriving, in the faithful partner's guardedness and in what a refusal says, and they put a price on it. The audit-target frame can also change how a reader treats the person across from them. The checker who works through this table at the sink has, in a small way, stopped being in the conversation.

**The test cannot be run.** *Stakes and beliefs are invisible, and the rival list can explain almost any pattern.* Conceded in part. The violation is defined exactly in Section 4, so the test is falsifiable in a design that measures stakes and beliefs, but nobody can run it on you from the outside. For a checker it is a way of asking whether one's own pattern is coherent, with self-report as the limit. The results in Sections 5 and 6 do not need it.

**Where is the conversation?** *The first door is asking, the cheapest and commonest source of information, and your model leaves it out.* It does. Asking appears only as the action "raise it", after a belief has formed. A question is a move in a game of talk with a partner who answers, and this essay has no model of that game.

**Checking is an emotion, not a decision.** *People who look at a partner are frightened, not auditing. You have put a spreadsheet around a feeling and then admired the spreadsheet.* The reply is not that emotion is irrelevant. Preferences over beliefs are a stated assumption, and a checker who values calm, consistently, passes the line. The line asks three things about a pattern and one about the person's own endorsement. A frightened checker can pass all four; a coldly calculating one can fail the second. And in the grip of the feeling nobody runs four tests; the fourth is addressed to the calm self because the feeling has hold of the other one. What the line picks out is the gap between the two. That is not a spreadsheet laid over a feeling. It is a description of the feeling's price, as judged by the person who pays it. What does not depend on the feeling is narrower than it may seem: the peak past which looking tells a checker less follows from how the other person responds, while the gap between a held level and a level chosen in the moment was derived for a checker who wants only to learn, and shrinks if the checker also cares about the lapses themselves. How reassurance seeking changes it was not tested.

**"The lab does not apply to couples."** Agreed, and Box A says so twice over: laboratory participants are not partners, and a forced minimum effort is not being looked at. The information peak (the version that does not rely on the laboratory direction) and the commitment gap do not use the laboratory results. They rest on a plain assumption, that a partner who might lapse lapses less when a lapse is more likely to be seen, which is untested in couples. The faithful partner's drift, the mislearning result and the link to refusal do use the laboratory direction.

**"This is self-help for the jealous."** The next section classifies and nothing else.

**Where the model is silent.** It has nothing to say about a check used as a lever of control. "Higher stakes widen the band" is what a decision-driven checker would say, and also what a controlling partner would say; the model cannot tell them apart. It says nothing where consent is not free: dependence, financial or otherwise, or coercive control. Where one partner is controlling the other, a look is not a purchase of evidence at all, and nothing here should be read as pricing it. It does not cover arrangements both partners have agreed to, such as shared transparency, where the laboratory direction was not tested in the material found; transparency negotiated as part of repair after a betrayal, where clean results may move suspicion slowly, for reasons the model leaves out; arrangements in which monitoring is the agreed core, around safety, addiction or money; both people checking each other; a partner who changes; or a check used as a pretext to leave. Attachment, mate-guarding, clinical reassurance seeking, the falling cost of looking and social norms each offer another account of the same behaviour. This essay's claim is narrower: what a check costs and what it can establish, given a model.

## 9. What To Ask About Your Own Case

These questions tell you what you are looking at, not what to do about it. Each comes from one result above, and none prescribes an answer.

**About the decision**

*Could any result of this check, or of the run of checks it begins, change what I would do? If not, what is it buying?* A yes places the check inside the band. It says the look is worth its cost to the one looking; it does not say it is fair to the person looked at. A no makes it something other than a decision, which the essay allows and prices.

*Which result would lower my suspicion enough to stop, and did I decide that before looking?* If no result could (a clean one that reads as careful concealment can never lower it), the check cannot inform, whatever it costs.

*Holding what is at stake fixed, has a run of good news reduced how often I look? If not, which rival explains it: stakes, cost, a changed partner or reading, or starting from high suspicion, where good news pulls you toward the point at which looking is most useful?* An answer of "none of them" is the narrow violation from Section 4.

**About the signal**

*Do my flags mean what they meant before I started looking more?* As looking rises a flag is more likely to be false, and past the zero point it can no longer be acted on; with a faithful partner whose behaviour shifts under scrutiny it may be produced by the looking itself.

*Is this the level I would choose if I allowed for the other person adjusting to it?* This describes a gap, not a rule. A checker who takes the partner's behaviour as fixed sets a higher level than one who counts the adjustment, when looking is cheap and the aim is mainly to learn. The gap describes a learner only; it does not say how hard anyone should look, and it leaves out what the level costs the person looked at.

**About yourself**

*Would my calm self pay the cost this check imposes?* A no, repeated across a long run of clean results, is the time-inconsistency pattern the line picks out.

**From the other seat**

*Am I hearing the question as a request for information or as a verdict on me?* If being asked reads as a verdict, more of the faithful pull back; that direction is a laboratory analogy, not a finding about couples.

*If I am asked to allow a look, is what I would lose a cost of being seen, or something I would rather not have found? And what does refusing say in this setting?* The model puts both losses under one name, privacy cost, and they are not the same thing. A refusal says as much as the share of innocent refusers lets it say, which is a fact about the setting, not about the refuser alone.

Answering honestly can mean admitting that no result could change what you would do. That classifies the check as not a decision and, if reassurance is the reason, as consumption. Consumption is allowed, and someone still pays for it.

---

## 10. Evidence, Or Belief

Go back to the glass. It is dry, the talk has moved on, and the three doors are where they were. Nothing in this essay tells you which one to open. What it changes is what you know the doors are made of.

The first door, asking, has a price in what the question says, which this essay does not model. The last, letting it go, is a decision to accept that holds only if it really is a decision. The middle door has the price that is hardest to read from where you stand. The two that involve a look or a request may be paid for partly in the other chair, where someone who may have nothing to hide is about to learn how they are read.

Put the two results side by side. In this model, once a partner who might be unfaithful lapses less under heavy looking, there is a point past which one more look tells the checker less. For a checker who only wants to learn, that is a loss; for one who also wants fewer lapses, it is the aim. The cost that falls either way is on the faithful partner, who, if the laboratory direction carries over, may grow guarded, so that the flags thin out into noise. And where full access is the norm, the cost of clearing a name can fall on those with nothing to hide, while where many refuse, a refusal says little. Looking buys evidence, not certainty, and past a point each further look buys less of it.

That leaves the question the model cannot answer, because in the model accepting is only what you do when the next look cannot change your mind, and it has no term for being believed. What is the person in the first chair actually after: evidence that a check can supply, or belief in a person that no check can? And if they need the second, how much of it can the first buy?
